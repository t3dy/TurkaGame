# Lettrism as Universal Science: From Ibn Arabi to Ibn Turka

## The Transformation of a Mystical Tradition into Systematic Philosophy

When **Ibn Arabi** developed his doctrine of the divine names in the 13th century, he was pursuing a mystical path: the direct experiential knowledge of God through contemplation of divine attributes. Five centuries later, **Ibn Turka** would take those same doctrines and transform them into something radically different—a comprehensive science claiming to encompass all knowledge and to explain the operative principles governing creation itself.

This transformation was neither simple preservation nor mere elaboration. It was a revolution in how Islamic thought approached the relationship between knowledge and reality, between contemplation and action, between reason and gnosis.

## The Mystical Foundation: Ibn Arabi and the Divine Names

Ibn Arabi's breakthrough was to place the divine names at the center of Islamic metaphysics. God is known through His 99 (or 28) names and attributes. Each divine name expresses a dimension of divinity: The Merciful, The Just, The Mighty. But for Ibn Arabi, these were not merely theological concepts. They were ontological principles—the very structure through which creation manifests.

In his *Futūḥāt al-Makkīyya*, Ibn Arabi argued that the universe unfolds through divine names. Creation itself is the self-disclosure of divine attributes. The visible world is not independent of God but a mirror reflecting divine reality at every level.

The imaginal realm, Ibn Arabi's distinctive contribution, was the domain where this reflection occurred most clearly—where divine names became forms perceptible to the mystical consciousness.

## The Systematization: Qūnavī and Jandī Codify and Elaborate

Qūnavī, Ibn Arabi's leading disciple, systematized his work. He organized the doctrine of divine names into philosophical categories and integrated it with peripatetic philosophy, showing how Ibn Arabi's metaphysics could be argued using Aristotelian logic.

By grounding Ibn Arabi's mysticism in rational philosophy, Qūnavī gave it legitimacy within Islamic intellectual orthodoxy. Mysticism was no longer "mere feeling" but rigorous knowledge grounded in reason.

Jandī extended this work further, elaborating the correspondence between divine names and human faculties. Through the Qūnavī-Jandī school, Ibn Arabi's mysticism had been transformed into systematic philosophy. The path was laid for Ibn Turka's innovation.

## The Philosophical Grounding: Suhrawardi and Neoplatonism

Suhrawardi's Illuminationism provided another crucial element. Where Ibn Arabi emphasized divine names as principles of manifestation, Suhrawardi emphasized light as the fundamental cosmic principle.

Neoplatonism, reaching the Islamic world through Greek texts and Byzantine scholarship, had established emanation as a philosophical framework. Reality flows from a transcendent source through progressively dimmer levels. But crucially, this emanation is not mechanical—it involves consciousness and mystical perception at each level.

Suhrawardi synthesized Neoplatonism with Islamic theology. Divine light is divine reality manifesting. The human soul can achieve direct perception of these light-principles through illumination.

## The Mathematical Revolution: Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Synthesis

Ibn Turka inherited:
- Ibn Arabi's doctrine of divine names as ontological principles
- Qūnavī's systematic philosophy and integration with peripatetic reason
- Suhrawardi's Illuminationist framework and emphasis on gnosis as legitimate knowledge
- Al-Ghazali's vindication of mysticism within orthodoxy

But Ibn Turka did something unprecedented. He explicitly grounded this entire system in Pythagorean mathematics.

Each of the 28 Arabic letters has a numerical value. Each letter corresponds to a divine name. Therefore, letters are not merely linguistic units—they are cosmic principles encoded as numbers. The universe itself is constituted through mathematical ratios that can be expressed through letters.

This was the crucial move: **making lettrism rational and universal**.

By grounding letters in Pythagorean mathematics, he showed that:
1. Letters are real cosmic entities, not symbols
2. Their operations follow mathematical principles, therefore rational laws
3. The system applies universally—to all creation and all knowledge
4. The science integrates reason and mysticism—mathematical rigor and mystical gnosis are not opposed but complementary

## The Operationalization: Letters, Talismans, and the Barzakh

In Ibn Turka's science of letters, a practitioner does not merely contemplate divine names—they work with letters. In the intermediate realm (barzakh), letters function as operative principles. By combining letters according to numerical correspondences, one creates words and talismans whose total value aligns with specific cosmic principles.

A talisman inscribed with letters whose abjad value matches the planet Venus embodies Venus-principle in the imaginal realm. Through the imaginal realm, this radiates influence on the sensible world.

This is not magic in the sense of violating natural law. It is technology of the intermediate realm—understanding and deploying the principles by which divine reality manifests as creation.

## The Universality: Occult Science as Total System

Ibn Turka claimed something revolutionary: lettrism is the universal science. It encompasses metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and practice.

This claim rested on a systematic argument:
1. Ontologically: Creation is constituted through letters and divine names
2. Epistemologically: Knowledge of letters is knowledge of reality's fundamental structure
3. Practically: By working with letters according to cosmic principles, one participates in creation
4. Universally: Since all creation flows through letters and divine names, this science applies to everything

This was not provincial superstition. It was a systematic alternative to Aristotelian philosophy, claiming to achieve what peripatetic philosophy attempted while additionally providing operative capability.

## The Isfahan Circle and Transregional Network

Ibn Turka did not work alone. The Isfahan Circle was the institutional expression of his project, with Sharaf al-Din Yazdi applying lettrist principles to history and Qazizada Rumi integrating mathematical rigor.

The New Brethren of Purity extended this project transregionally. By the 16th century, Ibn Turka's system had achieved canonical status. Mulla Sadra, the Safavid philosopher, received and elaborated Ibn Turka's doctrines.

## Conclusion: A Genuine Alternative Philosophy

What Ibn Turka accomplished was the creation of a genuine alternative to European Aristotelian philosophy. Just as the European Renaissance rediscovered Platonic philosophy, the Islamic world had developed its own Neopythagorean synthesis.

The historiographical significance is profound: **Islamic intellectual history is not decline but continuous innovation**. The 14th-15th centuries were a period of genuine philosophical renewal. By explicitly grounding lettrism in Pythagorean mathematics, Ibn Turka created something genuinely new.

This transformation—from mysticism to science, from scattered gnosis to systematic philosophy, from Ibn Arabi's contemplation to Ibn Turka's operative lettrist technology—is the story of Islamic Neopythagoreanism.
