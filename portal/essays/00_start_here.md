# Who Was Ibn Turka?

In the winter of 1430 an old man sat in a rented house at Natanz, in the Iranian desert
between Isfahan and Kashan, and wrote a logic textbook for his son.

He had been in exile three years. He had been, in his time, chief judge of two cities, the
client of Timurid princes, and the author of what he believed was the first complete
statement of the only science that could explain everything. He was now waiting for a court
five hundred miles away to decide whether he could go home. He would spend the following
year in the Timurid capital attending that court once a week, and he would die there still
waiting.

The book he wrote at Natanz was not about any of that. It was an introduction to the
philosophers' logic — the ordinary, respectable, entry-level discipline — written for a boy
named Jamāl al-Dīn Muḥammad, one of ten children. His father had spent thirty years arguing
that philosophy was not the universal science it took itself for. He taught his son
philosophy's tools anyway.

That is Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī, and this portal exists because almost nobody
knows the half of him that matters.

---

## The claim

Ibn Turka (1369–1432) argued that the letters of the Arabic alphabet are not marks that
stand for sounds. They are the constituents of reality — the principles through which
creation is actually put together — and a rigorous science of them would therefore be a
science of everything.

He put the claim in a form that leaves no room for compromise. Against philosophy: *the
faux-universal concepts of philosophical speculation notwithstanding, only the letter
encompasses all that is and is not, all that can and cannot be; it alone is the*
coincidentia oppositorum; *hence lettrism is the only truly universal science.*

Take that apart and three things are happening at once.

He is not defending a fringe practice against the charge of superstition. He is **going
after the crown**, and the crown belonged to peripatetic philosophy — the Aristotelian
tradition of Ibn Sīnā, the most prestigious intellectual enterprise in the Islamic world.

He is claiming the letter is the ***coincidentia oppositorum***, the point where opposites
coincide. A philosophical concept ranges over what is and what could be. The letter, he
says, reaches further: to what is not, and what could not be. A science whose object has
that range is the only candidate for a science of the whole.

And he says **only**. Not *also*, not *equally*. That word is why he could not have a quiet
career.

---

## Why this is not what it sounds like

Read cold, "the alphabet explains the universe" sounds like a man in a tower. It was
nothing of the kind, and four things keep it from being that.

**It was mathematics.** The great intellectual move of Ibn Turka's world was to take the
occult sciences — astrology, [[intersections/intersection-geomancy|geomancy]], lettrism —
and shift them **out of the natural sciences and into the mathematical ones**. That was a
jurisdictional claim before it was a metaphysical one. Four centuries of attack from Ibn
Sīnā, Ghazālī, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn had said these disciplines had no demonstrable
subject. Mathematics was the field whose credentials nobody disputed, and it is the one
place where you can settle a question **without appealing to an authority** — which matters
enormously when the authorities are all on the other side. You cannot out-cite Ghazālī. You
can, in principle, out-demonstrate him.

**It was orthodox, and meant to be.** Islamic theology holds that the Qur'an is
miraculously inimitable — that its perfection is itself the proof of its origin. Ibn Turka's
science is offered as the **demonstration** of that doctrine. If the letters of scripture
are the constituents of creation, the Qur'an's inimitability stops being a literary verdict
and becomes a cosmological fact. He was not smuggling foreign material into Islam. He was
claiming to prove Islam's central textual doctrine by exact means.

**It was a day job's second shift.** He was **Shāfiʿī chief judge of Isfahan and Yazd** — a
senior working magistracy in two cities, in a mainstream legal school, held as a hereditary
family office that Tīmūr himself had granted when he made Ibn Turka's elder brother judge in
Samarkand. Everything he wrote was written around a full caseload.

**And it was not eccentric.** He belonged to a network stretching from Anatolia through
Cairo to Iran, which called itself — cryptically — the **Brethren of Purity**, after the
tenth-century encyclopedists. His counterpart at the western end, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī,
was doing the same thing in the Ottoman lands. They had the same teacher.

## The life, briefly

**Isfahan, 1369; Samarkand, 1387.** Born, almost certainly in Isfahan, into a scholarly family with a judgeship in it (the year is worked back from his own stated age of 59 in an apology of 1426). In 1387 Temür took Isfahan and massacred most of its people but spared the Turka family, taking it to his court at Samarkand, where his elder brother Ṣadr al-Dīn was made judge. Ibn Turka, a teenager, studied under that brother for what the dissertation calls the first **twenty-five years** of his life — alongside a fellow pupil named Qāḍīzāda Rūmī, who would grow up to run Ulugh Beg's observatory at Samarkand. Their teacher
taught only the outward sciences, considering it **too dangerous to delve into esoteric
matters in public**. Both students went on to spend their lives making hidden things
calculable, one through the stars and one through the letters.

**Cairo, c. 1393–1408.** About fifteen years abroad (the dates are conjectural), leaving Samarkand at his brother's insistence, with a companion — Sharaf al-Dīn Yazdī, said to have travelled with him and who would remain his disciple and closest friend for the rest of his life. He trained in
law under a first-rank Shāfiʿī authority, and in the occult sciences under **Sayyid Ḥusayn
Akhlātī**, personal physician-alchemist to the Mamluk sultan. Akhlātī died in 1397, so the two overlapped for at most about four years. Ibn Turka became one of that
sultan's courtiers. He also, the sources say, made **mortal enemies at court** — decades
before they mattered.

**Isfahan and Shiraz, c. 1408–1414.** Home, and after a brief attachment to the court of Pīr-Muḥammad at Shiraz (murdered in 1409), into the court of his brother Iskandar Sulṭān, Tīmūr's grandson: the
leading cultural patron of the age, interested in alchemy and astrology, and — historians
now argue — **the first early modern absolutist sovereign**, running an eschatological
theory of his own authority against his uncle Shāhrukh's conservative, juridical one. Ibn
Turka's science was staff work for the first programme and had no place in the second.

In 1414 Shāhrukh destroyed Iskandar: captured, blinded, later executed.

**1414–1422.** Eight years attempting to retire from public life. It is the most productive
stretch of his career. The *Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ*, the Book of Inquiries
— **the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism** — carries a colophon date of **1420** (a date the manuscript itself says may refer to copying, and which was revised and expanded in 1425). Later papers pair it with Ulugh Beg's founding of the Samarkand observatory in the same year — one foundation in stone and one on paper; the dissertation does not draw that parallel.

**c. 1422.** Rivals denounce him at Herat for *Sufi bias*. He travels there, answers in person, **wins**, is offered Isfahan and chooses Yazd, where he is given the judgeship. It is the most impressive thing he ever did.

**1426.** Enemies in Yazd send a delegation to Herat, citing a youthful verse praising ʿAlī. Summoned again, he answers with an apology and a creed and regains the ruler's favour. (Melvin-Koushki writes of "three trials" without listing them; reading c. 1422, 1426 and 1427 as the three is this portal's inference.)

**1427.** After a Ḥurūfī tried to kill the ruler outside the Herat mosque, Ibn Turka was recalled, stripped of position and property, tortured, imprisoned and exiled — not for anything he wrote, but on suspicion by association with the Ḥurūfīs, a messianic letter-movement whose
founder the ruling family had executed.

**c. 1429–1432.** A refused hearing at Simnan. A winter at Natanz. A promise of reinstatement, obtained
at last in a military camp. Then nine months in the capital, attending court weekly, waiting
for the promise to become an order.

It never did. He died at Herat on 12 August 1432.

## What happened afterwards

Two things, and they run in opposite directions.

**He was misfiled.** His most famous book became the *Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid*, a work of
scholastic metaphysics that contains **no reference to his lettrism whatsoever**. It entered
the Iranian seminary curriculum, and his reputation formed around it: a mystical
philosopher, a link between Ibn ʿArabī and Mullā Ṣadrā. The acclaim is precisely what buried
him. Over half his roughly forty-five works remain unedited to this day — the lettrist ones
above all, on the assumption that they must be the marginal part.

**And he won, two centuries late.** In Safavid Iran, Mīr Dāmād carried a specifically
Timurid lettrism into the philosophical mainstream, and the turn went so far that **Ibn Sīnā
himself** — the head of the coalition that had spent four centuries attacking the occult
sciences — **was posthumously rewritten as a neopythagorean occultist**. In Mughal India,
Ulugh Beg's star tables were reissued behind the first thoroughly lettrist preface in the
history of Arabo-Persian astronomy.

His science reached the observatory's tables in the end. It took until the 1650s, and he
was not there.

## What this portal is, and how to read it

It is a reading of one scholar's research: **Matthew Melvin-Koushki**, whose work on Ibn
Turka and the Islamicate occult sciences is what makes any of this sayable. Forty-three of
his texts, his Yale dissertation among them, sit behind these pages. Nothing here supersedes them;
this organises them around one man.

Three ways in:

**The [[chronology|Chronology]]** — fifty dated events, each with a full account, from
a celestial conjunction in 1365 to a death at Herat in 1432. If you want the life, start
there.

**The Intersections** — six essays asking where Ibn Turka meets a wider context and what
changes when he does: the Brethren of Purity, al-Būnī, the occult court, the Pythagorean
revival, the Akbarian inheritance, the defence of divination. If you already know the
territory, start there.

**The entries** — figures, concepts, texts, institutions. If you arrived looking for
something specific, start there.

Two habits run through all of it. **Every claim carries its source and page**, because the
difference between this and occult-fantasy dressing is whether the footnote exists. And
every substantial entry ends with **What we do not know** — the gaps stated plainly, because
half this man's work is still in manuscript and pretending otherwise would be its own kind
of misfiling.
