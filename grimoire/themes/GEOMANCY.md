## The claim

Geomancy — divination by sixteen figures generated from random marks — is the test
case for the whole occultophobia argument, because it is the science *easiest to
mock* and yet was **defended in writing by the period's most rigorous
mathematician-historian**: Yazdī "wrote a tract in defense of the popular divinatory
art" against Ibn Khaldūn's critique (held excerpt). If Yazdī took raml seriously, the
modern reflex to laugh at it is doing no analytic work.

## Fixed points (held / project-synthesized)

- **The Khaldūn–Yazdī axis**: Ibn Khaldūn (d. 1406) as the period's own
  anti-occultist — his "confused polemics" blamed for sīmiyāʾ's terminological
  instability (*OC* n.15) — answered a generation later by Yazdī's defense. The
  debate the game stages between orthodox and occult reputations existed *as a
  literature*.
- **Akhlāṭī practiced it** (BIOGRAPHY): geomancer is one third of the mentor's
  triple identity (lettrist-alchemist-geomancer), so the art enters Turka's story at
  the source; the game's `cap:geomancy` grant from the circle's sand-figures option
  is correctly placed.
- **Perpetual dynastic cycling** (held excerpt at line ~250 of the Arabica file):
  the defense sits beside prognostic uses — raml as cheap, portable political
  forecasting, the poor scholar's observatory.

## What it gives the game

- Geomancy is the game's most under-used granted capability (`cap:geomancy` read
  rarely). This page argues for its niche: **the science you can do in exile** — no
  instruments, no library, no patron. A Phase V/legacy encounter where raml is what
  remains when the observatory is gone would be both poignant and true to its
  social register.
- The Khaldūn-figure (an in-world anti-occultist authority) is a strong recurring
  antagonist-of-ideas seed distinct from the jealous colleagues: principled critique
  rather than rivalry, deserving its own debate encounter.
