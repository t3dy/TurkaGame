## Act I — Cairo & Formation

*You have come to Cairo the way water finds a crack: because everywhere else was closed to you. The city is full of teachers. Most of them are wrong.*

**→ Chose: Seek unconventional teachers immediately**
> Skip the madrasa. The unconventional circles are where Sayyid Husayn Akhlati actually teaches.

*Also available:* Rigorous scholastic training first

*You skip the discipline and go straight for the strange teachers. Faster, riskier, and it shows in how you argue.*

### 2. How openly to align with Akhlati  `c02` · PLAUSIBLE-GAP · theme: loyalty

Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlātī draws the kind of student who does not advertise the association afterward. He is called brilliant by the same people who call him dangerous, often in the same breath. Attaching your name to his is not a neutral act.

**→ Chose: Study quietly, keep it deniable**
> Study with Akhlati quietly. Deniable for now — and he may trust you less for it.

> Keep Yazdi at a teacher's distance. Safer authority, a shallower bond.

*Also available:* Collaborate as equals

*Yazdi remains devoted, capable, and slightly outside the innermost room where the real thinking happens.*

### 4. How close to stand with Qasim-i Anvar  `c04` · ATTESTED · theme: loyalty

*Also available:* Bond deeply as fellow-travelers

### 5. Which lineage to foreground  `c05` · ATTESTED · theme: knowledge

Ibn ʿArabī's name opens doors and closes others just as fast; everyone has an opinion of the Andalusian mystic. Ḥamūya is quieter, less argued-over, and — you increasingly suspect — just as essential. No one before you has thought to need them both.

## Ending — The Lost Legacy

You held firm. But what you built did not survive intact — no one received the whole of it, and much of a life's work goes into the ground with you.

The attempt to return to Isfahan society was the highest-risk path available in exile. Whether it actually succeeded is not something the sources settle — only that you tried. You let your own name carry more of the credit than was strictly earned. No one caught it in your lifetime. Historians eventually do. You died certain that history would come around. It took five centuries longer than you expected. Your last transmission went to the New Brethren alone. Smaller. Much harder to burn. It was rīmiyā they remembered at gatherings — the astonishments, the demonstrations. Fewer remember the system underneath them. That was always the trade.
