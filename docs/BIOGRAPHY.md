# BIOGRAPHY.md — Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī (1369–1432)

A single, detailed, citation-grounded biography, built to be the canonical source
for drawing choices, game events, and encounters from — for the visual novel, the
roguelike, and the career sim alike. Synthesized from the three Melvin-Koushki
papers first in hand (`research/notes/`, `docs/RESEARCH_BRIEF.md`) plus the
50-event timeline (`site/data/timeline.json`, mirrored into
[IslamicateOccultPortal](../../IslamicateOccultPortal/)'s `timeline_events` table).
**Corrected 2026-09-27** against Melvin-Koushki's 2012 Yale dissertation, *The Quest for a
Universal Science* (corpus `SRC-610EE1D6BA`; pdf page = printed page + 17) and the rest of his
corpus, now held (see Sources). The generated, page-cited version of the life is
[`TurkaVita/docs/BIOGRAPHY.md`](../TurkaVita/docs/BIOGRAPHY.md); the reconciliation is
[`TurkaVita/docs/TURKA_AUDIT.md`](../TurkaVita/docs/TURKA_AUDIT.md). Dates carry the dissertation's
hedges ("c.", "(?)", "presumably"); a colophon date may record copying, not composition.
Every claim below is either **ATTESTED** (directly in a source), **COMPARATIVE** (an
explicit comparison the source itself draws, not a claim of contact), or **CONTEXT**
(standard general history) — see `docs/DECISIONS.md`'s grounding conventions.

## Vitals

- **Full name:** Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī
- **Lifespan:** 1369–1432 (born 770/1369, "almost certainly" in Isfahan, derived from his own stated age of 59 in *Nafsat al-Maṣdūr I*; died in Herat, Monday 14 Dhū l-Ḥijja 835 / 12 August 1432)
- **Day job:** Chief Judge (*qāḍī*) of Isfahan — the *Prologue*'s, *Grammatology*'s and *New Brethren*'s title
  (the last two say "of Isfahan and Yazd"). The dissertation has the Isfahan qadiship as a family office descended
  from his brother Ṣadr al-Dīn's appointment at Samarkand, and the **Yazd** judgeship as granted by Shāhrukh c. 1422 at
  his own request (CL-0735). Read "Chief Judge of Isfahan" as the later papers' summary label.
- **Life's project:** A mathematical-theurgic, consciously Pythagorean philosophy —
  the first fully systematic summa of Islamic lettrism (*ʿilm al-ḥurūf*)
- **Died:** In Herat, impoverished and in legal limbo, after the exile that followed the
  purge of 1427 ("much of the next five years"). Melvin-Koushki gives his age at death as 63;
  his own figures imply about 65, a flagged inconsistency in the source
- **Modern scholarly framing** (Melvin-Koushki): worth reading alongside Nicholas
  of Cusa, Pico della Mirandola, Giordano Bruno, and John Dee — a mathematizing,
  theurgic, empire-scaled system-builder who had far greater *immediate* political
  and scientific impact than any of his Latin peers

## The life, chronologically

### Isfahan and Samarkand (1369–c. 1393)

Ibn Turka is born in 1369 into a family of scholars, philosophers and qadis (his grandfather's
*Qawāʿid al-Tawḥīd* is one of his later commentary subjects). In **1387 Temür takes Isfahan**,
massacres most of its inhabitants and spares the Turka family, **taking it to his court at
Samarkand** (**ATTESTED**, dissertation pdf 51–52). His elder brother **Ṣadr al-Dīn** is made
qadi there; Ibn Turka, a teenager, studies under him. A life "from Cairo in 1385" is therefore
wrong: the sources put him in Samarkand in 1387. Around **1393**, at his brother's insistence, he
leaves for Mecca and Cairo, the first of some **fifteen years of study abroad** (dates
conjectured: "c.").

### Cairo (c. 1393–c. 1408)

The Cairo years, c. 1393–1408, leave the deepest imprint. He studies hadith under
**Bulqīnī** and becomes a disciple of **Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī** (d. 1397) — note that Akhlāṭī
dies *during* these years, so at most about four years overlap — a lettrist, alchemist and
geomancer at the Mamluk court of Barqūq, and the hub of a network Ibn Turka's circle will later
call the **"New Brethren of Purity"**, a name consciously invoking the medieval Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ
(Brethren of Purity), authors of the 10th-century *Rasāʾil* encyclopedia and part of the textual
tradition behind the *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* / *Picatrix*. Akhlāṭī's influence radiates from Cairo to
Iran, India, and Anatolia — this is the network, not any single institution, that shapes Ibn
Turka's entire later intellectual world. Akhlāṭī's death in 1397 is the first major loss the
sources record. Temür dies in 1405 while Ibn Turka is still abroad. He returns to **Isfahan
c. 1408** (perhaps 1408–9, "perhaps two or three years" after Temür's death), intending a retired
life of teaching and contemplation.

Two figures anchor Ibn Turka intellectually rather than personally, cited as
lettrism's two co-founders: **Ibn ʿArabī** (1165–1240), the Andalusian "Supreme
Master," and **Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya** (d. 1252), described as Ibn ʿArabī's "Iranian
twin." Ibn Turka is credited as the first to pair them explicitly as co-founders of
the tradition, after ʿAlī himself (identified in this tradition with a
reincarnated Pythagoras).

**Qāsim-i Anvār** (d. 1434), a Sufi poet, is described by Melvin-Koushki as one of Ibn Turka's
**companions in Cairo** — in a note of the dissertation (pdf 32, citing Niʿmatullahi hagiography, whose
tradition has him visiting Akhlāṭī with Ibn Turka and Yazdī; low confidence, pdf 68) and flatly in *The
Occult Court* (n. 24). The letters show warm relations in the later years, and he is arrested and
expelled from Herat in the purge of 1427 that also struck Ibn Turka (dissertation pdf 32, 56). The
Cairo companionship is therefore **one hagiographic tradition, adopted without its caveat by the later paper**.

### The courts of Fars (c. 1408–1414)

Ibn Turka is first summoned to Shiraz by **Pīr-Muḥammad b. ʿUmar-Shaykh** (c. 1408–9), who is
murdered in 1409; his brother **Iskandar Mīrzā** (Iskandar Sultan in the later papers) rises in
Fars and also makes Ibn Turka a member of his court, "presumably" with a teaching post and
judgeship. Iskandar's court at Isfahan is dated 1412–14 in the timeline (the ranges 812–15 and
815–17 AH disagree). Iskandar's atelier is known for fusing star science, poetry and manuscript
painting. Ibn Turka moves back to Shiraz because of harassment by envious individuals. Iskandar
rebels; in 1414 Shāhrukh marches against him and he is captured, blinded and later executed (the
timeline gives 817/1414; one CE conversion prints 1415), and Ibn Turka's earlier attachment to
him becomes a liability. **The later papers call Iskandar Ibn Turka's "first patron" and do not
mention Pīr-Muḥammad; the dissertation names both.**

### 1414–c. 1422 — an attempted retirement, and Bāysunghur

After Iskandar's fall Ibn Turka tries to retire from public life (c. 1414–22). **No source dates
a Bāysunghur patronage, and the older "second patron from c. 1416" is unsupported.** What is
attested: Shāhrukh appoints his son Bāysunghur governor of Mazandaran and western Khurasan in
1415; Bāysunghur appears as **addressee and commissioner** of the *R. Suʾl al-Mulūk* (before 829/1426,
"at his request") and of *Nafsat al-Maṣdūr II* (c. 1429–32); and his son ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn is the
dedicatee of the *Tuḥfa-yi ʿAlāʾī* (1428). The later papers call him "second patron" without a
start date; say "addressee and commissioner from 1426". The dissertation's survey of the letters does
show a "particularly close relationship" with him (four letters to Bāysunghur in the *Munshaʾāt-i Turka*,
and particular favour on one occasion), but it does not date its beginning (letter 18 is put at "825/1422?",
queried), and Melvin-Koushki's own account of his closeness is carried here as a relationship, not as a dated patronage. Bāysunghur was also an accomplished
calligrapher and the likely commissioner of the monumental **Bāysunghur Qurʾan** (**CONTEXT**).

### 1420 — the *Mafāḥiṣ*, and the *Shaqq-i Qamar* of 1426

The older "1420, the pivot year" bundled three things that are separate events.

1. The ***Investigations*** (*Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ*) — his masterwork, the first fully systematic
   summa of Islamic lettrism — is dated 823/**1420** in its colophon. That date **may record
   copying (*taswīd*), not composition**: a marginal correction in the Majlis manuscript says so;
   the day is disputed (1 Shaʿbān in Landberg 146, 19 Dhū l-Ḥijja in Majlis 10196); the place is
   "Isfahan or Yazd?"; and the work was **revised and expanded in 828/1425** with Sharaf al-Dīn
   Yazdī present. No patron is named in the dissertation (the 2025 papers say Iskandar
   commissioned it; Iskandar was dead by 1420 — a disagreement inside Melvin-Koushki's own
   texts). Its central diagram, the **Ṭahawī Circle**, is called the first "modern" lettrist
   reworking of the Pythagorean Tetractys; it is §1.15 of the work, on ff. 63a–65a of Tehran, Majlis
   Library MS 10196 (a strong candidate for a curated manuscript image). Of that manuscript, only
   ff. 52a and 118b (the incipit and explicit) are established as **autograph**; whether the
   circle's folios are in his own hand is not established.
2. **Ulugh Beg's Samarkand Observatory** is under construction in these years. Melvin-Koushki
   treats the coincidence as more than decorative — star science and letter science boomed
   together in this milieu — but it is **context, not an event of Ibn Turka's life**. His best
   friend and foremost student, **Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī** (d. 1454), later works at the
   observatory as a Timurid imperial historian and mathematician — the first, per
   Melvin-Koushki, to fully "astrolettrize" the writing of history itself. Of the
   manuscript's opening (ff. 52a–56a), Melvin-Koushki's own texts **differ**: the *Prologue* says
   it is "almost certainly" Yazdī's hand; the dissertation says the copy has autograph
   sections and that the earliest copies carry a note that Yazdī **checked the copy and attended
   teaching** on the work (f. 119a; Beinecke Landberg 146 f. 179b). Unresolved.
3. The Persian ***R. Shaqq-i Qamar u Bayān-i Sāʿat*** (*On the Splitting of the Moon and the Last
   Hour*) is dated 829/**1426** (a terminus "before 28 January 1426", possibly a copy date; another
   listing prints 27 February) — not 1420. On Qurʾan 54:1 it ranks **seven levels** of readers in
   ascending order: (1) jurists and traditionists, (2) dialectical theologians, (3) peripatetic
   philosophers, (4) illuminationist philosophers, (5) verifying mystics of Ibn ʿArabī's school,
   (6) lettrists, (7) ʿAlī and the Imams. Level seven is "not entirely what it seems" (Melvin-Koushki's
   reading): it is peculiar to the present time, marked by a conjunction (*saʿādat-qirān*), and is tied to *jafr*,
   so it is not simply the Imams. A three-tier version (philosophers, Sufis, lettrists) is in the
   *Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr*. The *Prologue* puts lettrism at the top and *Selenocentrism* has two
   lettrist levels — again a difference between his texts.

From c. 1422 to 1426 Ibn Turka lives in **Yazd** — productive, with many students and lettrist
works — after his first accusation (below).

*Investigations*'s own internal structure is worth knowing for its own sake: three
"Globes of Light" — **Planet, Pearl, and Peach** — corresponding to Mental,
Written, and Spoken registers of the Letter, organized as an ascent–descent–ascent
journey, with a fourth part treating the Letter itself as form-as-content (the
paper's own governing motto: "form is content").

### The Occult Quintet

A separate, cleaner hierarchy also documents this period: five canonical occult
sciences, named via the mnemonic *kullu-hu sirr* ("the whole is a secret,"
K-L-H-S-R), running in explicit descending order of technical difficulty, cost,
and elite status — *kīmiyā* (alchemy), *līmiyā* (talismanry), *hīmiyā*
(subjugation), *sīmiyā* (illusionism), *rīmiyā* (trickery). This is the game's
existing skill-tree spine (`games/visual-novel/STATE_MODEL.md`).

### Court and political economy

Occult science functioned as literal political technology across the entire
post-Mongol Persianate world. Patronage in the later period was transactional
and explicit — but the "royal boon" language of *The Occult Court* belongs to **ʿAlī Ṣafī's *Boon
for the Khan* (1522)**, a manual of illusionism and trickery commissioned by a Safavid Qizilbash
governor of Khurasan (Durmish Khān Shāmlū), cataloguing named operations: putrefactions,
suffumigations, sleeper interrogation, treasure dowsing, instant agriculture. It is **not** a
description of Ibn Turka's own court life, and earlier drafts of this file generalised it. Popularization was a live
tension: Ibn Turka's own style is deliberately dense and elite, in contrast to
Ḥusayn Vāʿiẓ Kāshifī and his son ʿAlī Ṣafī's explicit "Everyman's Library"
simplifications of the same material for wider circulation — hoard prestige vs.
teach for reach, a real axis in the historical record, not an invented game
mechanic.

### The three trials (c. 1422, 1426, 1427)

Ibn Turka — known specifically as a judge who defended the weak against the
powerful — is subjected to **"three trials"** (Melvin-Koushki, dissertation fn. 99), engineered by
jealous, rival colleagues. **Melvin-Koushki never lists the three in one place.** His timeline and
text give the following, and **mapping "the three" onto them is this project's reading**:

1. **c. 825/1422** — Isfahan enemies accuse him of *ṣūfīgarī* (Sufi bias) at Shāhrukh's court;
   he travels to Herat, wins favour, is offered Isfahan but chooses Yazd and the judgeship there
   (via *Nafsat al-Maṣdūr I*). He wins.
2. **829/1426** — Yazd enemies, angered by his impartial application of the Sharīʿa, send a
   delegation to Herat and cite a youthful verse praising ʿAlī; Shāhrukh summons him and he writes
   *Nafsat al-Maṣdūr I* and the *R. Iʿtiqādāt* (a creed tract), regaining favour. He wins.
3. **830/1427** — the Ḥurūfī **Aḥmad-i Lur attempts to kill Shāhrukh** in Herat and is killed; those
   with suspected Ḥurūfī sympathies are arrested or exiled. Ibn Turka is recalled to Herat, stripped
   of position and property, tortured and imprisoned, then exiled. He loses. In the same purge
   **Qāsim-i Anvār** and the calligrapher Maʿrūf Khaṭṭāt are expelled from Herat — a parallel
   persecution.

**Whose account this is.** The apologies to Shāhrukh (1426) and to Bāysunghur (c. 1429–32) are the
primary source *and* were, in Melvin-Koushki's words, "produced under great duress" and "hardly
reflective of his primary concerns" (pdf 25). They can show what he told whom, not simply what he
held. The *Prologue*'s remark that he "refused to bend the knee" at the inquisitions is
**uncited** in that paper; the dissertation shows defensive apologies. Record it as Melvin-Koushki's
remark, not a finding. (The claim that Ibn Khaldūn tried to have Ibn Turka executed is in *The New
Brethren of Purity* only; the dissertation says only that their Cairo residences overlapped. One
paper's claim.)

### Exile and death (1427–1432)

Exile follows the purge: teaching hadith and tafsir, writing for governors, and sending letters to
the Marʿashī and Kārkiyā sayyids and others for clemency. In 1428 he writes the *Tuḥfa-yi ʿAlāʾī* for
ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn b. Bāysunghur and the *Mabdaʾ u Maʿād* for Shāh Rażī l-Dīn in Mazandaran; in 1429–30 he
makes a fruitless attempt to get a hearing with Shāhrukh at Simnan ("c. 1429" in the timeline, 1430 in the
text); he winters in Natanz and writes the *K. al-Manāhij* for his son Muḥammad (1430), and gets a
hearing and a promise of reinstatement. For nine months (1431–32) he attends court weekly awaiting
review of his case and writes *Nafsat al-Maṣdūr II* for Bāysunghur. He dies in **Herat**,
frustrated, impoverished and in limbo, on 12 August 1432. Per Melvin-Koushki, *Investigations*
remains understudied for centuries afterward — effectively "on the Index." Yazdī outlives him by
22 years, dying in 1454.

## The comparative frame: Ibn Turka and the European Renaissance magi

**No direct contact is documented, and none should be implied or invented.** What
*is* directly attested is that Matthew Melvin-Koushki — the modern scholar whose
work this entire project is built on — explicitly and repeatedly compares Ibn
Turka to four European figures, arguing Ibn Turka deserves a place in the
"Scientific Revolution" story alongside them:

- **Nicholas of Cusa** (1401–1464) — "fellow lawyer, fellow Pythagoreanizer, but
  far less immediately influential" than Ibn Turka was in his own world.
- **Pico della Mirandola** (1463–1494) — named directly alongside Cusa, Bruno, and
  Dee as a comparandum.
- **Giordano Bruno** (1548–1600) — the most extensive comparison. Both
  subordinated all contemporary schools of thought to a totalizing
  Pythagorean/lettrist code; both were, in Melvin-Koushki's phrase,
  "historiographically burnt at the stake" — Bruno literally (executed by the
  Roman Inquisition, 1600), Ibn Turka's *Mafāḥiṣ* left understudied for centuries.
- **John Dee** (1527–1608) — the single richest comparison, developed at length in
  a dedicated essay ("Dr Dee's Ottoman Adventure"): *"Ibn Turka is best approached
  as a Timurid Dr. Dee — or Dee best approached as an Elizabethan Dr.
  Littleturk."* Both committed to mathematical, magical, kabbalistic/lettrist,
  alchemical, and anagogic unification of knowledge in service of a millenarian,
  one-world empire; both were smeared in their own lifetimes; both were more
  influential after death. The essay's counterfactual — what if Dee had sought
  patronage at the more occult-friendly Ottoman court instead of failing
  repeatedly in England? — is framed through **Sultan Murad III** (r. 1574–1595),
  a Sufi-initiate sultan devoted to oneiromancy, astrology, and talismanry,
  himself fashioning a prophet-saint-king persona through personal angelic
  communications directly paralleling Dee's own. The *Book of Soyga* material in
  Dee's own angelic diaries is argued to derive from the Bunian-Bistamian
  magical corpus (Ahmad al-Buni's tradition) popular in Ottoman courtly circles —
  a genuine textual thread, not just a thematic echo.
- **Akbar** (Mughal emperor, r. 1556–1605) is offered as the most successful
  *avatar* of the millennial occult-scientific sovereignty model Ibn Turka's
  circle helped originate, syncretizing Muslim, Hindu, Jain, and Christian
  traditions.

Everything else on the European side of `site/data/timeline.json` — Ficino's birth
and Hermetic translations, Agrippa, Reuchlin — is included as **orienting context
or structural parallel**, explicitly *not* claimed as a comparison Melvin-Koushki
himself draws, and tagged `COMPARATIVE` only where the source does draw it,
`CONTEXT` otherwise.

## Historiography — how this material got recovered

Ibn Turka's modern rediscovery traces to Matthew Melvin-Koushki's own scholarship,
beginning with his 2012 Yale doctoral dissertation, *The Quest for a Universal Science: The
Occult Philosophy of Ṣāʾin al-Dīn Turka Iṣfahānī (1369–1432) and Intellectual Millenarianism in
Early Timurid Iran* (held; this file's spine), through the later papers now also held (2021–2025). The broader historiographical context — why a figure this significant
stayed obscure so long — includes A.I. Sabra's push against "decline" narratives
of Islamic science (1987), George Saliba's argument for direct Islamic-to-European
astronomical transmission (2007), and Noah Gardiner's manuscript-transmission work
on al-Buni (2012). On the European side, Frances Yates's *Giordano Bruno and the
Hermetic Tradition* (1964) and Paul Oskar Kristeller's Ficino scholarship (1943)
did for European Renaissance Hermeticism what comparable Islamicate-studies
scholarship has only much more recently begun doing for Ibn Turka's world — a gap
Melvin-Koushki's own "Occult-Scientific Revolution" framing writes directly
against.

## For game design — what's usable, and what isn't (yet)

**Ready-made dramatic spine:** rising scholar under a great mentor (Akhlāṭī) →
abroad, then court service under Pīr-Muḥammad and Iskandar Mīrzā → the fall of Iskandar, an
attempted retirement, Yazd, and Bāysunghur as addressee → the third trial (1427), exile, and a
death that only later scholarship vindicates.
Already used for the visual novel's 8-act structure (`games/visual-novel/CHOICES.md`).

**Named, usable NPCs, all attested:** Akhlāṭī (mentor), Ṣadr al-Dīn (elder brother, qadi at Samarkand),
Bulqīnī (hadith teacher), Yazdī (best friend/student),
Qāsim-i Anvār (Sufi companion, parallel fate in 1427), Iskandar Mīrzā and Bāysunghur (Iskandar a patron,
Bāysunghur an addressee/commissioner),
Ibn ʿArabī and Ḥamūya (intellectual forebears, not living contacts). Ulugh Beg and
Shah Rukh are usable as background political figures.

**Open research gaps — don't invent past these:**
- *Closed 2026-09-27:* the full seven-tier hierarchy (all seven are on the page of the *Shaqq-i Qamar*;
  what level seven *means* is Melvin-Koushki's inference); exact years for the first two trials (c. 1422
  and 1426, though "three trials" is his phrase and he never lists them together); birthplace (almost
  certainly Isfahan; born 770/1369); the "4th source PDF" (the whole Melvin-Koushki corpus is held).
- No source dates a Bāysunghur patronage.
- Whether the *Mafāḥiṣ* was commissioned by Iskandar (the 2025 papers) or by no named patron (the
  dissertation), and whether its 1420 date is composition or copying.
- Whose hand ff. 52a–56a of MS Majlis 10196 are in (Yazdī's per the *Prologue*; autograph sections per
  the dissertation).
- Which of the years c. 1393–1408 he spent where (dates conjectured), and the dates of Pīr-Muḥammad's
  and Iskandar's courts (812–15 vs 815–17 AH).
- Internal inconsistencies carried, not resolved: the *Shaqq-i Qamar* terminus (28 January vs 27 February
  1426), his age at death (63 stated, about 65 implied), the *Sharḥ Fuṣūṣ* (813 vs 814), Simnan (1429
  vs 1430). See `TurkaVita/docs/TURKA_AUDIT.md` § C.
- Not held: Dee is a scan without a text layer; no Timurid chronicles; no Persian editions of his works.

**A live design temptation to resist:** treating the Cusa/Pico/Bruno/Dee
comparisons as license to write scenes of actual contact or correspondence between
Ibn Turka and any European figure. The comparison is the modern historian's
argument, not a historical event — the game can *dramatize the comparison itself*
(e.g. an ending epilogue noting how a modern reader might place this life
alongside Bruno's) without pretending the men ever met.
