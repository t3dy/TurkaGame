# BIOGRAPHY.md — Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī (1369–1432)

A single, detailed, citation-grounded biography, built to be the canonical source
for drawing choices, game events, and encounters from — for the visual novel, the
roguelike, and the career sim alike. Synthesized from the three Melvin-Koushki
papers currently in hand (`research/notes/`, `docs/RESEARCH_BRIEF.md`) plus the
50-event timeline (`site/data/timeline.json`, mirrored into
[IslamicateOccultPortal](../../IslamicateOccultPortal/)'s `timeline_events` table).
Every claim below is either **ATTESTED** (directly in a source), **COMPARATIVE** (an
explicit comparison the source itself draws, not a claim of contact), or **CONTEXT**
(standard general history) — see `docs/DECISIONS.md`'s grounding conventions.

## Vitals

- **Full name:** Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī
- **Lifespan:** 1369–1432
- **Day job:** Chief Judge (*qāḍī*) of Isfahan
- **Life's project:** A mathematical-theurgic, consciously Pythagorean philosophy —
  the first fully systematic summa of Islamic lettrism (*ʿilm al-ḥurūf*)
- **Died:** In exile, impoverished, in legal limbo, five years after losing his
  third state inquisition
- **Modern scholarly framing** (Melvin-Koushki): worth reading alongside Nicholas
  of Cusa, Pico della Mirandola, Giordano Bruno, and John Dee — a mathematizing,
  theurgic, empire-scaled system-builder who had far greater *immediate* political
  and scientific impact than any of his Latin peers

## The life, chronologically

### Formation (c. 1385–1397) — Cairo

Ibn Turka's formative years are spent in Cairo under **Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī**
(d. 1397), a lettrist, alchemist, and geomancer who is the hub of a network Ibn
Turka's circle will later call the **"New Brethren of Purity"** — a name
consciously invoking the medieval Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ (Brethren of Purity), authors of
the 10th-century *Rasāʾil* encyclopedia and part of the textual tradition behind
the *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* / *Picatrix*. Akhlāṭī's influence radiates from Cairo to Iran,
India, and Anatolia — this is the network, not any single institution, that shapes
Ibn Turka's entire later intellectual world. Akhlāṭī dies in 1397; his death is the
first major loss in Ibn Turka's life the sources record.

Two figures anchor Ibn Turka intellectually rather than personally, cited as
lettrism's two co-founders: **Ibn ʿArabī** (1165–1240), the Andalusian "Supreme
Master," and **Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya** (d. 1252), described as Ibn ʿArabī's "Iranian
twin." Ibn Turka is credited as the first to pair them explicitly as co-founders of
the tradition, after ʿAlī himself (identified in this tradition with a
reincarnated Pythagoras).

Also in Cairo: **Qāsim-i Anvār**, a Sufi poet, becomes a close companion. His fate
will later parallel Ibn Turka's own.

### First patron: Iskandar Sultan (c. 1409–1415)

Ibn Turka becomes attached to the court of **Iskandar Sultan**, a Timurid prince
and governor of Fars, whose atelier is known for fusing star science, poetry, and
manuscript painting. Iskandar Sultan's political ambitions eventually exceed his
position; he is defeated, blinded, and executed in 1415 on the order of his uncle
Shah Rukh (**CONTEXT** — standard Timurid political history, not itself stated in
the sources in hand, but the necessary explanation for why Ibn Turka's patronage
then shifts).

### Second patron: Bāysunghur (from c. 1416)

Ibn Turka's second and longer patron is **Bāysunghur**, Shah Rukh's son — an
accomplished calligrapher in his own right and the likely commissioner of the
monumental **Bāysunghur Qurʾan**. This is the more stable, productive half of Ibn
Turka's court career.

### 1420 — the pivot year

Three things converge in a single year:

1. Ibn Turka completes ***Investigations*** (*Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ*) — his masterwork,
   the first fully systematic summa of Islamic lettrism. Its central diagram, the
   **Ṭahawī Circle**, is called the first "modern" lettrist reworking of the
   Pythagorean Tetractys, and survives in Ibn Turka's own autograph (Tehran, Majlis
   Library MS 10196, f. 63a — the single strongest candidate for a curated
   manuscript image of his actual work).
2. **Ulugh Beg** begins constructing the **Samarkand Observatory**. Melvin-Koushki
   treats this coincidence as more than decorative: star science and letter
   science boomed together in this milieu. Ibn Turka's best friend and foremost
   student, **Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī** (d. 1454), later works at this
   observatory as a Timurid imperial historian and mathematician — the first, per
   Melvin-Koushki, to fully "astrolettrize" the writing of history itself. Yazdī
   likely copies Ibn Turka's own autograph (ff. 52a–56a of the same manuscript,
   c. 1420–25).
3. Ibn Turka writes a Persian treatise, ***On the Splitting of the Moon and the
   Last Hour***, laying out a seven-tier epistemic hierarchy from "traditionist
   literalism" at the bottom to "lettrism" at the apex of human perfection. Only
   the two endpoints plus three intermediate tiers (theologians, philosophers,
   Sufis) are confirmed in sources currently in hand — **the full seven-tier
   breakdown is an open research gap**, cited onward to a different
   Melvin-Koushki article, "Selenocentrism and Heliocentrism," not yet acquired.
   Don't invent the missing tiers.

*Investigations*'s own internal structure is worth knowing for its own sake: three
"Globes of Light" — **Planet, Pearl, and Peach** — corresponding to Mental,
Written, and Spoken registers of the Letter, organized as an ascent–descent–ascent
journey, with a fourth part treating the Letter itself as form-as-content (the
paper's own governing motto: "form is content").

### The Occult Quintet

A separate, cleaner hierarchy also documents this period: five canonical occult
sciences, named via the mnemonic *kullu-hu sirr* ("the whole is a secret,"
K-L-H-S-R), running in explicit descending order of technical difficulty, cost,
and elite status — *kīmiyā* (alchemy), *līmiyā* (talismanry), *hīmiyā*
(subjugation), *sīmiyā* (illusionism), *rīmiyā* (trickery). This is the game's
existing skill-tree spine (`games/visual-novel/STATE_MODEL.md`).

### Court and political economy

Occult science functioned as literal political technology across the entire
post-Mongol Persianate world. Patronage was transactional and explicit — works
were commissioned as "royal boons" (e.g. *Boon for the Khan*, ʿAlī Ṣafī's manual of
illusionism and trickery, dedicated to a specific Safavid Qizilbash patron,
cataloguing named operations: putrefactions, suffumigations, sleeper
interrogation, treasure dowsing, instant agriculture). Popularization was a live
tension: Ibn Turka's own style is deliberately dense and elite, in contrast to
Ḥusayn Vāʿiẓ Kāshifī and his son ʿAlī Ṣafī's explicit "Everyman's Library"
simplifications of the same material for wider circulation — hoard prestige vs.
teach for reach, a real axis in the historical record, not an invented game
mechanic.

### The three inquisitions (c. 1420s–1427)

Ibn Turka — known specifically as a judge who defended the weak against the
powerful — is subjected to **three separate state inquisitions**, engineered by
jealous, rival colleagues. He wins the first two; exact years are not established
in sources currently in hand. He **loses the third**, datable to approximately
**1427** by working backward from his 1432 death and the "five years of wandering
exile" the sources report. In the same year, 1427, his Cairo companion
**Qāsim-i Anvār is exiled** over the same lettrist associations — a parallel
persecution, not a coincidence Melvin-Koushki treats lightly.

### Exile and death (1427–1432)

Five years of wandering exile follow the third inquisition. Ibn Turka dies in
1432, impoverished, in legal limbo. Per Melvin-Koushki, *Investigations* remains
understudied for centuries afterward — effectively "on the Index." Yazdī outlives
him by 22 years, dying in 1454.

## The comparative frame: Ibn Turka and the European Renaissance magi

**No direct contact is documented, and none should be implied or invented.** What
*is* directly attested is that Matthew Melvin-Koushki — the modern scholar whose
work this entire project is built on — explicitly and repeatedly compares Ibn
Turka to four European figures, arguing Ibn Turka deserves a place in the
"Scientific Revolution" story alongside them:

- **Nicholas of Cusa** (1401–1464) — "fellow lawyer, fellow Pythagoreanizer, but
  far less immediately influential" than Ibn Turka was in his own world.
- **Pico della Mirandola** (1463–1494) — named directly alongside Cusa, Bruno, and
  Dee as a comparandum.
- **Giordano Bruno** (1548–1600) — the most extensive comparison. Both
  subordinated all contemporary schools of thought to a totalizing
  Pythagorean/lettrist code; both were, in Melvin-Koushki's phrase,
  "historiographically burnt at the stake" — Bruno literally (executed by the
  Roman Inquisition, 1600), Ibn Turka's *Mafāḥiṣ* left understudied for centuries.
- **John Dee** (1527–1608) — the single richest comparison, developed at length in
  a dedicated essay ("Dr Dee's Ottoman Adventure"): *"Ibn Turka is best approached
  as a Timurid Dr. Dee — or Dee best approached as an Elizabethan Dr.
  Littleturk."* Both committed to mathematical, magical, kabbalistic/lettrist,
  alchemical, and anagogic unification of knowledge in service of a millenarian,
  one-world empire; both were smeared in their own lifetimes; both were more
  influential after death. The essay's counterfactual — what if Dee had sought
  patronage at the more occult-friendly Ottoman court instead of failing
  repeatedly in England? — is framed through **Sultan Murad III** (r. 1574–1595),
  a Sufi-initiate sultan devoted to oneiromancy, astrology, and talismanry,
  himself fashioning a prophet-saint-king persona through personal angelic
  communications directly paralleling Dee's own. The *Book of Soyga* material in
  Dee's own angelic diaries is argued to derive from the Bunian-Bistamian
  magical corpus (Ahmad al-Buni's tradition) popular in Ottoman courtly circles —
  a genuine textual thread, not just a thematic echo.
- **Akbar** (Mughal emperor, r. 1556–1605) is offered as the most successful
  *avatar* of the millennial occult-scientific sovereignty model Ibn Turka's
  circle helped originate, syncretizing Muslim, Hindu, Jain, and Christian
  traditions.

Everything else on the European side of `site/data/timeline.json` — Ficino's birth
and Hermetic translations, Agrippa, Reuchlin — is included as **orienting context
or structural parallel**, explicitly *not* claimed as a comparison Melvin-Koushki
himself draws, and tagged `COMPARATIVE` only where the source does draw it,
`CONTEXT` otherwise.

## Historiography — how this material got recovered

Ibn Turka's modern rediscovery traces to Matthew Melvin-Koushki's own scholarship,
beginning with his Yale doctoral dissertation (**c. 2012** — recalled from general
field knowledge, flagged `LOW` confidence pending verification against a primary
bibliographic record) through the three papers this project has in hand (2021,
2025, 2025). The broader historiographical context — why a figure this significant
stayed obscure so long — includes A.I. Sabra's push against "decline" narratives
of Islamic science (1987), George Saliba's argument for direct Islamic-to-European
astronomical transmission (2007), and Noah Gardiner's manuscript-transmission work
on al-Buni (2012). On the European side, Frances Yates's *Giordano Bruno and the
Hermetic Tradition* (1964) and Paul Oskar Kristeller's Ficino scholarship (1943)
did for European Renaissance Hermeticism what comparable Islamicate-studies
scholarship has only much more recently begun doing for Ibn Turka's world — a gap
Melvin-Koushki's own "Occult-Scientific Revolution" framing writes directly
against.

## For game design — what's usable, and what isn't (yet)

**Ready-made dramatic spine:** rising scholar under a great mentor (Akhlāṭī) →
court success across two successive patrons (Iskandar Sultan, Bāysunghur) →
catastrophic third accusation, exile, death that only later scholarship vindicates.
Already used for the visual novel's 8-act structure (`games/visual-novel/CHOICES.md`).

**Named, usable NPCs, all attested:** Akhlāṭī (mentor), Yazdī (best friend/student),
Qāsim-i Anvār (companion, parallel fate), Iskandar Sultan and Bāysunghur (patrons),
Ibn ʿArabī and Ḥamūya (intellectual forebears, not living contacts). Ulugh Beg and
Shah Rukh are usable as background political figures.

**Open research gaps — don't invent past these:**
- The full seven-tier epistemic hierarchy (5 of 7 tiers unconfirmed).
- Exact years for the first two inquisitions.
- Ibn Turka's exact birthplace and early-life detail before Cairo.
- Whether a 4th source PDF (referenced but never located at kickoff) exists.

**A live design temptation to resist:** treating the Cusa/Pico/Bruno/Dee
comparisons as license to write scenes of actual contact or correspondence between
Ibn Turka and any European figure. The comparison is the modern historian's
argument, not a historical event — the game can *dramatize the comparison itself*
(e.g. an ending epilogue noting how a modern reader might place this life
alongside Bruno's) without pretending the men ever met.
