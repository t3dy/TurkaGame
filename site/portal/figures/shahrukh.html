Shāhrukh

Shāhrukh (r. 1405–1447), Tīmūr's son and the ruler under whom Ibn Turka spent almost his entire career. He is not a background figure: he destroyed Ibn Turka's first patron, later received him back into favour, and his court was the venue of the accusations that ended in exile. In 813/1411 he abrogated the Mongol yasa in favour of the sharīʿa and closed the taverns — the turn toward public piety that made an occult philosopher's position permanently precarious.

Shāhrukh: the ruler who made and unmade him

Muʿīn al-Dīn Shāhrukh b. Tīmūr (1377–1447) succeeded to the eastern half of his father's empire and ruled from Herat for forty-two years. By 815/1412 his control was established over Khurasan, Transoxania and Khwarazm. Almost the whole of Ibn Turka's adult career runs inside his reign, and the relationship between them turns twice.

The pious turn. In 813/1410 Shāhrukh completed a madrasa-khānaqāh complex at Herat and appointed its teachers. In 813/1411 he declared the abrogation of yasa law, enforced the sharīʿa, and closed the taverns (The Quest, chronology). Replacing Mongol customary law with Islamic law was a deliberate re-founding of Timurid legitimacy on religious rather than steppe grounds — and it set the terms on which a man like Ibn Turka would afterwards be judged. A court that has staked its authority on orthodoxy is a dangerous employer for an occultist.

The destruction of Iskandar. In 817/1414 Shāhrukh marched against [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] in Iraq. Iskandar fled, was captured and blinded, and was executed afterwards; Shāhrukh replaced him with his own son Ibrāhīm Sulṭān. Ibn Turka had been attached to Iskandar's brilliant Isfahan court in 815–17/1412–14, and had already withdrawn to Shiraz because of what the chronology calls harassment by envious individuals, putting forward his protégé Sharaf al-Dīn Yaʿqūb to replace him as qāḍī. From 817 to 825 (1414–22) he attempted to retire from public life (The Quest, chronology).

The turn back. Around 825/1422 his opponents went to Shāhrukh's court at Herat and accused him of Sufi bias. Ibn Turka travelled to Herat to clear his name, gained Shāhrukh's favour, and was given the judgeship of Yazd (The Quest, chronology). This is the first of the tribunals, and he won it — by going in person to the ruler who had destroyed his previous patron, and persuading him.

And the turn away again. It did not hold. In 1427 Ibn Turka was exiled from Herat along with [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], the official pretext being a putative connection with the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfīs]] (The Quest, 456 n.16). The same court that had vindicated him five years earlier expelled him.

Why he matters to the argument. Shāhrukh is the counterweight to the reading of the Timurid century as straightforwardly hospitable to the occult sciences. His son [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] built the observatory and his son [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]] ran the finest book-workshop in the world, but Shāhrukh himself governed through public piety, and the pressure Ibn Turka lived under came from his court. Patronage and prosecution were the same institution.

What the abrogation of the yasa actually did

The single most consequential act of the reign, for this portal's purposes, is dated 813/1411: Shāhrukh declares the abrogation of yasa law, enforces the sharīʿa, and closes the taverns (The Quest, chronology).

The yasa is the Mongol customary law code attributed to Chinggis Khan, and it had been the juridical backbone of Chinggisid and early Timurid legitimacy. Abrogating it in favour of Islamic law is a re-founding: it moves the basis of the dynasty's authority from steppe descent to religious orthodoxy. The taverns are the visible sign of a policy whose real content is jurisprudential.

For a man in Ibn Turka's position the consequences are structural rather than immediate. A court that has staked its legitimacy on public piety acquires (a) an interest in being seen to police doctrine, (b) a class of religious officials whose standing rises with that policing, and (c) a vocabulary in which professional rivalry can be conducted as accusation. All three appear in the tribunals of the 1420s.

The absolutism argument

Melvin-Koushki sets Shāhrukh against his nephew in a comparison with unusual force: if the rise of absolutist politics is one of the benchmarks for early modernity, in 816/1412 Shāhrukh was the last medieval Islamic ruler, and Mīrzā Iskandar was the first early modern absolutist sovereign (Imperial Talismanic Love, 21, citing his own "Eschatological Absolutism").

Read alongside the yasa abrogation, this produces a coherent picture. Shāhrukh's answer to the legitimacy problem was conservative and juridical — govern as a Muslim sovereign under the sharīʿa. [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar's]] was eschatological and absolutist — claim a sacred role in the unfolding of history, with occultists and philosophers as the staff who could articulate it. Ibn Turka's science fits the second programme and not the first, and Iskandar lost.

The three turns

The relationship reverses twice, and the pattern is the point.

1. 817/1414 — destruction. Shāhrukh marches against Iskandar in Iraq; Iskandar flees, is captured and blinded, and is executed afterwards. Ibn Turka's patron and the commissioner of his central works becomes a dangerous association. 2. c. 825/1422 — vindication. His opponents go to Shāhrukh's court at Herat and accuse him of Sufi bias. He travels there to answer in person, gains Shāhrukh's favour, and is given the judgeship of Yazd. He argued in front of the man who had destroyed his patron, and won. 3. 1427 — exile. He is expelled from Herat with [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], the official excuse a putative [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] connection.

And then a fourth movement that is neither: from 1429 he cannot get a hearing at all. A fruitless attempt at Simnan, a promise of reinstatement at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa around 834/1430, and nine months of weekly attendance at Herat waiting on a review that never came.

The court as an institution

Shāhrukh completed a madrasa-khānaqāh complex at Herat in 813/1410 and appointed its teachers; by 815/1412 his control was established over Khurasan, Transoxania and Khwarazm. His sons ran the century's two most important cultural enterprises — [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur's]] kitābkhāna and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg's]] [[samarkand-observatory|observatory]]. The reign is not hostile to learning; it is hostile to a particular claim about where authority over knowledge lies.

What we do not know

  • Whether Shāhrukh personally decided any of the three turns or simply ratified the outcome of factional pressure is not recoverable from these sources.
  • The identity of most of the opponents is unrecorded; only Muḥammad al-Jazarī, who died at Herat in 833/1429, is named as a powerful one.
  • Why the 825/1422 vindication did not hold is not explained in the chronology.
