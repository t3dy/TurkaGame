Bāysunghur

Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh (1397–1433), Timurid prince, governor, and the greatest book-patron of the age — his Herat kitābkhāna was the finest manuscript workshop in the world. The dissertation records Ibn Turka's close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular among the Timurid amirs. He is the middle term of the patron sequence between [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]].

Ghiyāth al-Dīn Bāysunghur (1397–1433), third son of [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]], was appointed governor of Mazandaran and western Khurasan in 818/1415 and is remembered above all as a patron of the book. The kitābkhāna he maintained at Herat gathered calligraphers, illuminators, painters and binders into the most accomplished manuscript workshop of the fifteenth century; he was himself a calligrapher of the first rank.

The connection to Ibn Turka. A survey of the Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka — Ibn Turka's collected letters — shows the frequency of his dealings with various Timurid amirs and his close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular (The Quest, 136). That the evidence comes from his own correspondence rather than from a chronicle makes it unusually direct.

Why the pairing is apt. Bāysunghur's interest was in how a page is made. Ibn Turka's science holds that the letter is an ontological entity before it is a written mark — that the shapes on the page participate in what they signify. A prince who cared about letterforms as craft and a philosopher who held letters to be cosmic principles had, unusually, a real subject in common.

The sequence. Bāysunghur sits between the patron who was destroyed ([[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]], 1414) and the patron who never quite arrived ([[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]]). He died in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka. Of the three Timurid patrons the career offers, he is the one that simply worked — which is why he is the least dramatic and the easiest to overlook.
