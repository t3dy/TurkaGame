Bāysunghur

Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh (1397–1433), Timurid prince, governor, and the greatest book-patron of the age — his Herat kitābkhāna was the finest manuscript workshop in the world. The dissertation records Ibn Turka's close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular among the Timurid amirs. He is the middle term of the patron sequence between [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]].

Ghiyāth al-Dīn Bāysunghur (1397–1433), third son of [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]], was appointed governor of Mazandaran and western Khurasan in 818/1415 and is remembered above all as a patron of the book. The kitābkhāna he maintained at Herat gathered calligraphers, illuminators, painters and binders into the most accomplished manuscript workshop of the fifteenth century; he was himself a calligrapher of the first rank.

The connection to Ibn Turka. A survey of the Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka — Ibn Turka's collected letters — shows the frequency of his dealings with various Timurid amirs and his close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular (The Quest, 136). That the evidence comes from his own correspondence rather than from a chronicle makes it unusually direct.

Why the pairing is apt. Bāysunghur's interest was in how a page is made. Ibn Turka's science holds that the letter is an ontological entity before it is a written mark — that the shapes on the page participate in what they signify. A prince who cared about letterforms as craft and a philosopher who held letters to be cosmic principles had, unusually, a real subject in common.

The sequence. Bāysunghur sits between the patron who was destroyed ([[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]], 1414) and the patron who never quite arrived ([[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]]). He died in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka. Of the three Timurid patrons the career offers, he is the one that simply worked — which is why he is the least dramatic and the easiest to overlook.

What a kitābkhāna actually was

The word translates badly as "library". Bāysunghur's kitābkhāna at Herat was a production house: calligraphers, illuminators, painters, gilders, paper-makers, rulers and binders working under one roof to a single standard, with the prince himself a calligrapher of the first rank supervising the result.

That matters for this portal because it makes him the one Timurid patron whose professional attention was on how letters sit on a page. A workshop of that kind exists to decide questions like which script for which text, how the page is ruled, where the gold goes — and those are questions about the disposition of letters in space.

Ibn Turka's [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] analyses the letter in three sensible registers: counted (iḥṣāʾī), written (kitābī) and spoken (kalāmī). The middle one is the kitābkhāna's entire subject. A prince who cared how a page was made and a philosopher who held that the written form of a letter is one of its real modes had, unusually for patron and client, a genuine common subject.

The evidence for the relationship

It comes from Ibn Turka's own correspondence rather than from a chronicle, which makes it unusually direct. A survey of the [[munshaat-i-turka|Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka]] shows the frequency of his dealings with various Timurid amirs and his close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular (The Quest, 136).

And it held to the end. During the [[nine-months-waiting|nine months of weekly attendance]] at Herat, waiting on a case review that never came, Ibn Turka wrote the [[risala-itiqadiyya|Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya]] for Bāysunghur — a statement of belief addressed not to the sovereign who could restore him but to the prince who might carry the argument inward. It is the last patronage relationship in the biography and it was being worked in the final year.

Where he sits in the sequence

Between the patron who was destroyed and the patron who never arrived: [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]] blinded in 817/1414, Bāysunghur, then [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] unreached. Of the three he is the one that simply worked — no catastrophe, no deferral — which is why he is the least dramatic and the easiest to overlook. He died in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka.

What we do not know

  • Whether he read the works dedicated or addressed to him.
  • Whether he intervened in the case during those nine months. Nothing records it.
  • What became of the Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya, or whether it reached him at all.
