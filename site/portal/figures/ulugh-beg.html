Ulugh Beg

Ulugh Beg (1394–1449), Shāhrukh's son, governor of Transoxiana and founder of the Samarkand Observatory — by a distance the most famous Timurid for history-of-science purposes. He was styled al-sulṭān al-faylasūf, the philosopher-sultan. For Ibn Turka he is the would-be third patron: the one ruler whose programme — the mathematicalization of the sciences — was the same programme, and whose patronage never quite arrived.

Ulugh Beg: the patron who should have been

The sultan-scientist. Muḥammad Ṭaraghay Ulugh Beg (1394–1449), Temür's grandson and Shāhrukh's son, governed Transoxiana from Samarkand and did something no other Timurid did: he practised the science he patronised. He founded the Samarkand Observatory, breaking ground in 1420, and the Zīj-i Sulṭānī produced there stood as the most accurate star table in the world for two centuries. He was awarded the title al-sulṭān al-faylasūf — the philosopher-sultan (Fazlıoğlu, Mathematical-Astronomical School, 41; on the observatory and the zīj, Blake, Astronomy and Astrology, ch. 6).

Why he belongs in Ibn Turka's story. Melvin-Koushki's argument makes the connection structural rather than incidental. His thesis is that the occult sciences in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran were moved out of the natural sciences and into the mathematical sciences to reassert their legitimacy — and that this mathematicalization is the immediate context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy by the members of the Samarkand Observatory (Powers of One, 1). Ulugh Beg's observatory and Ibn Turka's [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] are not two subjects that happen to share a century. They are two faces of one movement: the conviction that number is the ground on which a science must stand.

He names the pairing directly — the model established by Iskandar Sulṭān and Ulugh Beg ensured that astronomy-astrology and lettrism in particular would continue to be heavily patronised by subsequent Timurid and Indo-Timurid dynasts, and by their Safavid and Ottoman competitors (Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists, 599). Ibn Turka's first patron and his would-be last are the two poles of that model.

The would-be. The sequence of Ibn Turka's patrons is a sequence of losses. [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]], who commissioned Of Letters and at whose instance he began the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Book of Inquiries]], was defeated and blinded in 1415. Under Shāhrukh he found a place — he was close to Bāysunghur in particular — and then in 1427 was exiled from Herat, alongside his old Cairo associate Qāsim-i Anvār, the official excuse being a putative connection with the Ḥurūfīs (The Quest, 456 n.16).

Samarkand was the obvious remaining door, and the right one. A ruler who had staked his reputation on mathematical astronomy was the natural patron for a man arguing that the occult sciences belong among the mathematical sciences. Ibn Turka died in 1432, seventeen years before Ulugh Beg was killed by his own son's faction in 1449. The patronage that would have made the most sense of his career is the one the record does not show him receiving.

Afterlife. The lettrist-astronomical fusion Ulugh Beg's institution made possible outlived them both. Shāhjahān commissioned the Zīj-i Shāhjahānī, an updated and corrected version of Ulugh Beg's tables of two centuries prior — and it carries the first preface in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition to be explicitly and thoroughgoingly lettrist in tenor (Philosopher-Kings, 599). Ibn Turka's science reached Ulugh Beg's star tables in the end. It simply took two hundred years.

The title, and what it was awarded for

Ulugh Beg was styled al-sulṭān al-faylasūf, the philosopher-sultan (Fazlıoğlu, Mathematical-Astronomical School, 41, cited in Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists, 3 n.9). The title is not flattery of the ordinary courtly kind. Melvin-Koushki's study of Timurid and Mughal rulers is titled Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists precisely because a small number of these men practised the disciplines they patronised, and Ulugh Beg is the clearest case: he computed.

The distinction matters for how his relationship to Ibn Turka should be imagined. A patron who funds astronomy needs to be persuaded that astronomy is worth funding. A patron who does the arithmetic himself needs to be persuaded that the arithmetic is right. The second is a harder audience and a better one, and it is the audience Ibn Turka's [[occult-science-universal|mathematicalized]] science was built for.

Why the observatory belongs to this portal

The conventional division of labour treats Samarkand as a triumph of exact science and Timurid occultism as an embarrassment to be handled separately. Melvin-Koushki's thesis denies the division outright. The mathematicalization, neopythagoreanization and sanctification of occultism in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran is, he argues, the immediate intellectual and sociopolitical context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy by the members of the [[samarkand-observatory|Samarkand Observatory]] — processes which have heretofore been studied in atomistic isolation (Powers of One, 1).

The chronology makes the point concrete. The observatory broke ground in 1420. The [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was completed in 823/1420. Two foundations laid in one year, a few hundred miles apart, by men acting on the same conviction: that a science earns its standing by becoming mathematical.

There is also a personal link. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]], second director of the observatory, had spent twenty-five years in the same schoolroom as Ibn Turka, taught by Ibn Turka's own brother. The two institutions share a classroom.

The model, named

Melvin-Koushki treats Ibn Turka's first patron and his would-be last as a matched pair. The model established by [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] and Ulugh Beg ensured that astronomy-astrology and lettrism in particular would continue to be heavily patronised by subsequent Timurid and Indo-Timurid dynasts, as well as by their Safavid and Ottoman competitors (Philosopher-Kings, 599).

That sentence is the reason the "would-be third patron" framing is structural rather than sentimental. Ibn Turka's career opens under one pole of the model and ends within reach of the other, and the model outlives them all.

The coda

Two centuries later Shāhjahān — self-proclaimed Second Lord of Conjunction, after Tīmūr the First — commissioned for his accession the Zīj-i Shāhjahānī, an updated and corrected version of Ulugh Beg's star tables. Tellingly, it carries the first preface in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition to be explicitly and thoroughgoingly lettrist in tenor (Philosopher-Kings, 599).

The same feature appears in the preface to the Sirr-i Akbar, Dārā Shukūh's celebrated Persian translation of the Upaniṣads. The letter science reached the star tables in the end; it simply took until the seventeenth century, and Ibn Turka was not there for it.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka ever sought Ulugh Beg's patronage directly is not documented. The "would-be" framing rests on the structural fit and the absence of any record of patronage, not on a recorded approach and refusal.
  • Which conjunction the observatory's own astrological work took as its frame is outside these sources.
  • The observatory was abandoned and dismantled after Ulugh Beg's murder in 1449; its foundations were not rediscovered until 1908, so the physical record is largely lost.
