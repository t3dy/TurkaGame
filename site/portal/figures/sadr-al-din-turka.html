Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka

Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka, Ibn Turka's elder brother and his teacher for the first twenty-five years of his life — alongside a fellow student who would become [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]]. An eminent scholar of hadith, tafsīr, jurisprudence and uṣūl al-dīn. Ibn Turka's own verdict on this education is pointed: his brother taught nothing beyond the outward sciences.

Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka Iṣfahānī was the elder brother of Ṣāʾin al-Dīn and an eminent scholar in hadith, Qur'anic exegesis, jurisprudence and the principles of religion. Ibn Turka studied under him for the first twenty-five years of his life — and so did [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]] (The Quest, ch. 1). That single fact reorganises the [[isfahan-circle|Isfahan Circle]]: its lettrist and its mathematician were not colleagues who found each other at court but schoolfellows, taught side by side in the same household for a quarter of a century.

The verdict on the teaching. Ibn Turka states that his brother taught nothing beyond the outward sciences (al-ʿulūm al-ẓāhira). It is a mild sentence with a great deal in it. The education was excellent and, by his own account, insufficient — a complete grounding in the exoteric disciplines that left the bāṭin untouched. Everything he later built can be read as the supply of what that education lacked.

The family and the Ḥurūfīs. Ḥurūfī sources record that both Ṣadr al-Dīn and their father Afżal al-Dīn sought out Fażl Allāh Astarābādī during his residence in Isfahan to have their dreams interpreted; Naṣr Allāh Nāfajī's Khwābnāma preserves Ṣadr al-Dīn's dream of kissing the Prophet's hand. Melvin-Koushki cautions that this implies no [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] inclination in either man — the story's purpose is to show Fażl Allāh's standing as a Sufi before his messianic turn. It nonetheless put a documented family contact with the founder of the Ḥurūfiyya on the record, and that record was available to Ibn Turka's prosecutors.

The office that came with the name. Ṣadr al-Dīn was appointed qāḍī in Samarkand by Tīmūr himself, and that appointment established the judgeship as a Turka family office — the hereditary post his younger brother would later occupy in Isfahan and lose in the tribunals (The Quest, §3.1). The family's standing with the dynasty predates Ibn Turka's own career by a generation.

Why he taught only the outward sciences. The dissertation gives his reason, and it is not ignorance: he deemed it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public (The Quest, 41). The elder brother's caution and the younger brother's career are the same judgement about the same risk, answered in opposite directions — and the tribunals of the 1420s suggest Ṣadr al-Dīn had assessed it correctly.
