Qazizada Rumi

Qāżīzāda Rūmī (c. 1380–1432), celebrated mathematician and astronomer. Member of the Isfahan Circle; friend and colleague to [[ibn-turka]]. Active in Samarkand at Timur's court and later in Isfahan and Yazd. Pioneer of mathematical methods in astronomy; integrated mathematical and occult approaches to celestial phenomena. Represents the mathematicization of Islamic astronomy during the early Timurid period.

Qāḍīzāda Rūmī (also known as Ṣāliḥ al-Dīn Mūsā Qāḍīzāda, c. 1380–1432) was one of the most accomplished mathematicians and astronomers of the Timurid age. Born in Anatolia, he rose to prominence at Timur's court in Samarkand and later became a close colleague of [[ibn-turka]] in Isfahan and Yazd. Qazizada was renowned for advancing mathematical methods in astronomy—bringing rigorous calculation to celestial observations. He shared with Ibn Turka a commitment to integrating rational mathematical analysis with occult philosophy. Where Ibn Turka pursued lettrism as the universal science, Qazizada pursued mathematics and astronomy as paths to understanding cosmic principles. The two represent complementary approaches within the Isfahan Circle: Ibn Turka's linguistic-lettrist method and Qazizada's mathematical-astronomical method, both grounded in the belief that creation is knowable through precise systematic inquiry into its underlying patterns.

They were schoolfellows. The dissertation records that Ibn Turka studied under his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] for the first twenty-five years of his life along with Qāḍīzāda Rūmī (The Quest, ch. 1). The two were not colleagues who converged at a Timurid court but students taught side by side in the same Isfahani household for a quarter of a century — which makes the later division of labour between them, one taking the letters and one the stars, look less like coincidence and more like two answers to the same teaching.

Two precisions. He was the second director of Ulugh Beg's observatory at Samarkand, not its first. And he died in 835/1432 — the same year as Ibn Turka (The Quest, 41 n.17, citing Ragep, 'Ḳāḍī-zāda Rūmī', EI2). Two men taught side by side for twenty-five years, who then divided the century's work between the stars and the letters, died in the same season.

The schooling, and what it implies

The fact that reorganises this entry: Qāḍīzāda studied under [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka]] alongside Ibn Turka, for the first twenty-five years of Ibn Turka's life (The Quest, 41 n.17, citing Eichner, 'Afḍal al-Dīn Turka', EI3, and Ragep, 'Ḳāḍī-zāda Rūmī', EI2).

They were not colleagues who converged at a Timurid court. They were taught in the same household, by the same man, for a quarter of a century — an education that stopped deliberately at the outward sciences because their teacher deemed it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public.

Both pupils then spent their careers on disciplines by which the hidden order of things might be made calculable. One took the stars and the other the letters, and [[observatory-founded|the observatory]] and [[mafahis-completed|the summa]] were begun in the same year, 1420. Melvin-Koushki's thesis — that the mathematization of astronomy and the mathematicalization of the occult sciences are one movement studied in atomistic isolation — has, in these two men, a shared classroom underneath it.

The office

He was the second director of [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg's]] [[samarkand-observatory|observatory at Samarkand]] — a precision worth keeping, since he is often described simply as its director. The observatory's staff over its short life included Jamshīd al-Kāshī and ʿAlī Qushjī; Qāḍīzāda belongs to the first generation of that institution rather than to its founding.

The coincidence at the end

He died in 835/1432, the same year as Ibn Turka. One in an observatory that would be dismantled within two decades of his death, the other in an antechamber at Herat waiting on a case review that never came.

What we do not know

  • Whether they remained in contact after the Isfahan schoolroom.
  • What he thought of lettrism. The link between the two disciplines is argued at the level of intellectual context, not documented as an exchange of views between these two men.
  • His birth date is not given in these sources.
