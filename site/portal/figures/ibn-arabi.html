Ibn Arabi

Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240), Andalusian mystic and philosopher. Founder of the metaphysical school most influential on later Islamic occultism and mysticism. Author of the Futūḥāt al-Makkīyya (Meccan Illuminations) and other foundational works. His doctrines of [[divine-names|divine names]], the imaginal realm, and the macrocosm-microcosm correspondence profoundly influenced [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka's]] theoretical framework. Via the [[qunavi|Qūnavī]]-[[jandi|Jandī]] transmission, Ibn Arabi's philosophy became the philosophical grounding for Ibn Turka's lettrist innovations.

Ibn ʿArabī: The Shaykh al-Akbar and His Legacy

Muḥyī al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) was born in Murcia, Andalusia, during the waning years of Islamic rule in medieval Spain. He lived through an era of profound intellectual ferment—the collision of Islamic mysticism, philosophy, and theology in a period of cultural upheaval. By the end of his life, based in Damascus, Ibn Arabi had become the most influential mystical-philosophical voice in the Islamic world, with a legacy that would shape Islamic thought for centuries.

The Metaphysical System

Ibn Arabi's magnum opus, the Futūḥāt al-Makkīyya (Meccan Illuminations), presents a comprehensive metaphysical system grounded in several key doctrines:

1. The Unity of Existence (Waḥdat al-Wujūd): All being is fundamentally one; the apparent multiplicity of creation is manifestation of the single divine reality.

2. The Divine Names (Al-Asmaʾ al-Ilāhīyya): God is known through 99 (or 28) divine names and attributes. Each name expresses a dimension of the divine. Creation itself unfolds through these names.

3. The Perfect Human (Al-Insān al-Kāmil): The human being is the microcosm that mirrors the macrocosm. In the perfected human, all divine names are manifest.

4. The Imaginal Realm (Al-Khalīqa al-Mutawassita): Between pure intellect and material sense exists an intermediate realm of subtle forms—the imaginal world where mystical visions and symbolic knowledge occur.

Transmission Through Qūnavī and Jandī

Ibn Arabi's thought did not reach later occultists directly. Instead, it was systematized and reinterpreted by two crucial transmitters:

  • Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnavī (d. 1274), Ibn Arabi's leading disciple, who codified and organized his teachings into a more scholastic philosophical framework.
  • ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Jandī (fl. 13th century), who further elaborated Qūnavī's synthesis, emphasizing the metaphysical correspondences and the operations of divine names.

This Qūnavī-Jandī school tradition became the conduit through which Ibn Arabi's metaphysics reached [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] and the later Islamic occult philosophers. When Ibn Turka integrated [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean mathematics]] with Islamic mysticism, he was building on the Ibn Arabi–Qūnavī–Jandī foundation.

Ibn Arabi and Ibn Turka: The Philosophical Genealogy

Matthew Melvin-Koushki's historical reconstruction clarifies Ibn Turka's relationship to Ibn Arabi. Scholars had long viewed Ibn Turka primarily as a Sufi-philosopher in the Ibn Arabi tradition—a continuator of mystical-scholastic synthesis. However, this framework obscured Ibn Turka's more radical project. Ibn Turka was not simply synthesizing Ibn Arabi with peripatetic philosophy; he was deploying Ibn Arabi's metaphysics (especially the doctrine of divine names) as the theoretical foundation for a new kind of operative science: [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist cosmology]].

The key insight: For Ibn Arabi, the divine names were primarily objects of mystical knowledge and contemplation. For Ibn Turka, they become operative principles encoded in the structure of letters and numbers. This is not a contradiction but a systematic application of Ibn Arabi's principles to the domain of practice and causation.

Historical Significance

Ibn Arabi stands at the head of a tradition that includes mysticism, Sufism, Islamic philosophy, and occultism. He demonstrated that Islamic thought could achieve a synthesis of reason and intuition, philosophy and gnosis, theology and practice. His rehabilitation of the imaginal realm and his systematization of [[divine-names|divine attributes]] created the conceptual apparatus that later occultists like Ibn Turka would weaponize.

In the history of Islamic occult philosophy, Ibn Arabi is the foundational figure. Every subsequent occultist, from Safavid mystical philosophers to Ottoman talismanic practitioners, operated in the shadow of his legacy—and often in direct dialogue with his texts.

What Ibn Turka took, and what he did not

The portal's [[hurufism|three-strand]] account of lettrism makes the debt precise. From the Akbarian tradition Ibn Turka takes a metaphysics of the [[divine-names|divine names]] — the doctrine that creation unfolds as the self-disclosure of divine attributes, and that the [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]] is where that disclosure takes form. Without this there is nothing for letters to be.

What he does not take is the orientation. Ibn ʿArabī's names are objects of contemplation approached through spiritual discipline; the knower is transformed by knowing. Ibn Turka's are operative principles encoded in a notation, approached through calculation; the knower acts. The intersection essay on the Akbarian inheritance calls this contemplation converted to computation, and the phrase is exact.

This is why the entry insists on two fathers. Akbarian metaphysics alone does not produce an operative science. It needs the second inheritance — [[saad-al-din-hamuyya|Ḥamūya's]] lettrist application, and behind it the al-Būnī tradition that [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] held together with Ibn ʿArabī in a single teaching.

The school, not the man

A point about transmission that matters for reading this portal. Ibn Turka does not receive Ibn ʿArabī raw. He receives the Ibn ʿArabī school tradition as formulated by [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] and [[jandi|Jandī]] — this is the formula the dissertation uses of the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]], which relies on precisely that lineage (The Quest, 6).

The distinction is not pedantic. Ibn ʿArabī's own writing is vast, associative and resistant to system; what reached fifteenth-century Iran was a scholasticised version, organised into arguments that could be taught, defended and built on. Ibn Turka argues with the school's Ibn ʿArabī.

The reception problem this created

There is an irony the portal should name. Because Ibn Turka is demonstrably an Akbarian, and because the Tamhīd — his most Akbarian book — became his most famous, he was filed as a link in a chain: the synthesizer connecting Ibn ʿArabī to Mullā Ṣadrā. Melvin-Koushki's verdict is that this acclaim is what marginalised him, obscuring the occultist tenor of the larger project, abstracting it from its historical context and robbing it of its animating virtue (The Quest, ii).

Being correctly identified as Ibn ʿArabī's heir is what stopped people looking at what he did with the inheritance.

What we do not know

  • Which of Ibn ʿArabī's works Ibn Turka read directly, as against through Qūnavī and Jandī, is not established in these sources.
  • How Ibn ʿArabī's own attitude to operative practice should be characterised is a large contested question this portal does not attempt to settle.
