Saad al-Din Hamuyya

Saʿd al-Dīn al-Ḥamuwī (c. 1250–1320). Syrian-Anatolian scholar who pioneered the application of [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist]] doctrine to cosmological and metaphysical inquiry. Cited repeatedly by [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] in the Kitab al-Mafahis as an authoritative source on the theory and practice of letters. His writings on lettrist technique and the correspondences between divine names and letters established the technical foundations upon which Ibn Turka built his more systematic Pythagorean synthesis.

Saʿd al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Masʿūd al-Ḥamuwī (c. 1250–1320) was a Syrian scholar active in the late 13th and early 14th centuries, at the intersection of several major intellectual currents: the declining Ayyubid court culture, the Ottoman rise in Anatolia, and the flourishing of Mamluk Cairo. Little is known of his biography, but his writings on [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] and divine cosmology reveal him as a systematizer of lettrist doctrine in its pre-Turka form. Al-Ḥamuwī worked within the framework of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] metaphysics, particularly the doctrine of divine names (asmaʾ), but pushed toward more technical, operative applications. His treatises examined how the 28 letters correspond to the divine names, how [[abjad-numerology|numerical values]] encode cosmic principles, and how knowledge of these correspondences grants the practitioner insight into (and potential influence over) the structure of creation. [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] explicitly cites al-Ḥamuwī's works multiple times in the Kitab al-Mafahis, recognizing him as an authoritative predecessor. This public acknowledgment suggests that Ibn Turka saw himself not as an innovator ex nihilo but as the systematizer of a lettrist tradition stretching back through al-Ḥamuwī to earlier sources. The key difference: whereas al-Ḥamuwī's lettrist project remained embedded in mystical-theological discourse, Ibn Turka's [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean synthesis]] gave it a mathematical-philosophical rigor and a claim to universality that transformed lettrism from marginal gnosis into a systematic science rivaling any philosophical school.
