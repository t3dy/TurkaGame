Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya

Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya (d. 649/1252), Kubrawi shaykh and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī's]] Iranian contemporary — and, with Ibn ʿArabī, Ibn Turka's favourite lettrist source. Melvin-Koushki calls him Ibn Turka's only other identified source, with an oeuvre even more centrally lettrist, and even more slippery, than Ibn ʿArabī's. He once criticised Ibn ʿArabī by letter for being too rigid in his lettrism.

The second father

The portal's intersection essay on the Akbarian inheritance is subtitled two fathers for one science, and this is the other one. Melvin-Koushki is precise about his standing: besides [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]], Ḥamūya is Ibn Turka's only other identified source, a Kubrawi shaykh and Ibn ʿArabī's Iranian contemporary whose oeuvre is even more centrally lettrist, and even more slippery (Being with a Capital B, 10). Elsewhere the two together are named Ibn Turka's favourite lettrist source (Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Sensorium, 6).

That pairing is the point. Akbarian metaphysics alone does not produce an operative science of letters; it produces a metaphysics of [[divine-names|names]]. Ḥamūya supplies the half where letters are already the centre of the enterprise rather than a consequence of it.

Too rigid in his lettrism

The detail that makes him vivid: Ḥamūya went so far as to personally criticize Ibn ʿArabī in a letter as being too rigid in his lettrism (Being with a Capital B, 10 and n.6).

Read that from Ibn Turka's position. The two authorities behind his science were not in agreement, and their disagreement was about method in the very discipline he was systematising — one of them thinking the other insufficiently free with it. An heir who inherits a quarrel has to adjudicate it, and the fourfold analysis of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] can be read as an adjudication: a scheme rigorous enough to satisfy the systematiser and capacious enough to hold what the free player was doing.

Jazz philosophers, and a philosopher of jazz

Melvin-Koushki's formulation for the relationship is deliberately unexpected and worth quoting: what the critique makes clear is that both Ibn ʿArabī and Ḥamūya can be usefully read as jazz philosophers — and Ibn Turka, their systematizing and Pythagoreanizing lettrist heir, as a philosopher of jazz (Being with a Capital B, 10).

The distinction is exact and it is the best short account of what Ibn Turka did. His predecessors improvised on the letters — Ḥamūya's stylings are described as radically free-playing, Mind-manifesting, riffing on the visual and the aural simultaneously. Ibn Turka did not improvise. He wrote the theory of the improvisation: what the changes are, why they work, and what makes a run of them right rather than merely striking. A philosopher of jazz is not a jazz musician, and the difference is the whole of his originality.

Where he sits in the canon

A later lettrist genealogy preserved through Kāshifī's works lists the canonical authorities in order: [[al-ghazali|Abū Ḥāmid Ghazālī]] (d. 1111), Fakhr al-Dīn Rāzī (d. 1210), Aḥmad al-Būnī (d. between 1225 and 1233), Ibn al-ʿArabī (d. 1240), Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya (d. 1252), Ibn Tamīmī al-Yāfiʿī (d. 1367), [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Ḥusayn Akhlātī]] (d. 1397), Abū Yaʿqūb al-Kūmī (fl. 1407), Ibn Turka (d. 1432), and Yaʿqūb Charkhī (Qizilbash Magic, 4).

Two things stand out. Ibn Turka appears in it as a canonical authority — the tradition itself placed him in the line, whatever modern reception did. And Ghazālī heads the list, which sits oddly against his place in the [[peripatetic-philosophy|anti-occultist coalition]]: later lettrists claimed him as a predecessor whether or not he would have accepted the role.

The dissertation's treatment

Chapter 4 of The Quest gives him a section of his own, §4.1.2.3, between al-Būnī and letter magic (§4.1.2.1) and Ibn ʿArabī and letter theory (§4.1.2.2) on one side, and — unexpectedly — §4.1.3, Ibn Sīnā's philosophical lettrism, on the other. Even the arch-opponent turns out to have a lettrism worth a section.

What we do not know

  • Which of his works Ibn Turka used, and in what form.
  • What the letter criticising Ibn ʿArabī actually argued beyond the charge of rigidity.
  • How much of the Kubrawi context came with the doctrine; the order's own lettrist tradition is not detailed in the corpus in hand.
  • His oeuvre is described as slippery, which is a warning about attribution as much as about style.
