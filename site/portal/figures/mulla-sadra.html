Mulla Sadra

Mullā Ṣadrā (1571–1635), the most celebrated philosopher of Safavid Iran, conventionally named as the terminus of the line running from Ibn ʿArabī through Ibn Turka. This portal keeps the entry short on purpose. Ṣadrā appears twice in the forty-three sources it is built on, and the Safavid heir the scholarship actually documents is [[mir-damad|Mīr Dāmād]].

Why this entry is deliberately short

Mullā Ṣadrā is the most famous philosopher of Safavid Iran and appears in almost every account of Ibn Turka's afterlife. He is also barely present in this portal's corpus: a search across all forty-three Melvin-Koushki sources returns two hits.

The style guide for these pages sets a floor of 2,500 words and names exactly one acceptable reason to fall short — that the corpus genuinely does not hold more — with the requirement that the page then say so. This is that case, and this is it saying so. Writing five pages on Mullā Ṣadrā here would mean writing from general knowledge rather than from the research this portal is a reading of, which is the one thing these entries are not permitted to do.

Where the Ṣadrā association comes from

Not from the lettrism. It comes from the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]], whose fame produced the standard picture of Ibn Turka as a mystical-scholastic thinker lodged unobtrusively somewhere between Naṣīr al-Dīn Ṭūsī (d. 672/1274) and Mullā Ṣadrā (d. 1050/1640) (The Quest, 6).

That framing is precisely what the dissertation exists to dislodge. Being acclaimed as the link between [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] and Mullā Ṣadrā is identified as the cause of his marginalisation, since it obscures the occultist tenor of the larger project and abstracts it from its historical context. So the Ṣadrā connection is real, and it is also the specific piece of received opinion this portal is arguing against.

Who the documented successor is instead

[[mir-damad|Mīr Dāmād]] (d. 1631), with 135 corpus hits and an article substantially devoted to him. He is held largely responsible for the neopythagoreanization of Safavid philosophy, through adherence to a particular brand of Mamluk–Timurid–Aqquyunlu philosophical lettrism in at least three of his works — a transmission described in the vocabulary of Ibn Turka's own science rather than in the vocabulary of wujūd.

What we do not know

  • Whether Mullā Ṣadrā engaged Ibn Turka's lettrist works at all. The sources in hand do not say, and the question is worth someone's attention.
  • What the two corpus mentions contain beyond situating him as the later terminus of a philosophical line.
  • Anything about his own occult-scientific commitments, if he had them — a question Melvin-Koushki's framing of Safavid philosophy makes newly interesting.
