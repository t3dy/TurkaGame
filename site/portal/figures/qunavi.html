Qūnavī

Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnavī (1210–1274), leading disciple and systematizer of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] metaphysics. Through his philosophical elaboration of Ibn Arabi's doctrines, Qūnavī created the scholastic framework that transmitted Ibn Arabi's thought to later Islamic philosophers and occultists. His work provided the philosophical grounding for [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka's]] integration of divine-names doctrine with lettrist and Pythagorean theory. A central figure in the Qūnavī-[[jandi|Jandī]] school tradition.

Ṣadr al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq al-Qūnavī (1210–1274) was the most important early systematizer of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] metaphysical doctrines. Born in Konya, Anatolia, Qūnavī became Ibn Arabi's foremost disciple and literary executor, tasked with organizing, elaborating, and defending his master's vision. Whereas Ibn Arabi's own writings are voluminous, imaginative, and often enigmatic, Qūnavī's works present Ibn Arabi's metaphysics in a rigorous, scholastic form, amenable to philosophical analysis and teaching. This systematization proved decisive: it was Qūnavī's articulation of Ibn Arabi's thought—not Ibn Arabi's own texts directly—that shaped the transmission of Ibn Arabi's legacy to later philosophers, Sufis, and occultists. Qūnavī's central contributions include: (1) the codification of the doctrine of the [[divine-names|divine names]] as the mechanism of creation; (2) the elaboration of the imaginal realm (malakūt) as the intermediate domain where mystical knowledge operates; (3) the establishment of the Perfect Human (al-insān al-kāmil) as the microcosm in which all divine attributes are manifest. Through the Qūnavī-[[jandi|Jandī]] school tradition, these doctrines reached Ottoman and Persian philosophers, providing the theoretical foundations that [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] would deploy in systematizing [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist occultism]] as the operative application of divine-names metaphysics.

Why systematisation was the decisive act

The usual way of describing a disciple is that he preserved his master's teaching. Qūnavī did something more consequential and more distorting: he made it arguable.

Ibn ʿArabī writes in a mode that is visionary, associative and enormous. It can be read, quoted and meditated on; it cannot easily be disputed, because it does not present itself as a sequence of claims with grounds. Qūnavī reorganised it into the form a scholastic philosopher could use — definitions, distinctions, positions taken against named opponents.

The gain is transmissibility: after Qūnavī, Akbarian metaphysics can be taught in a madrasa and defended in a disputation. The cost is that the version transmitted is Qūnavī's. When [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] argues with Ibn ʿArabī he is arguing with a text that has already been through this process — the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] relies specifically on the Ibn ʿArabī school tradition as formulated by Qūnavī and [[jandi|Jandī]] (The Quest, 6).

What the school supplied to lettrism

Three things, each load-bearing for the later science:

1. The [[divine-names|divine names]] as an ordered structure rather than a devotional list — nameable, relatable, and therefore mappable onto something else. 2. The [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]] given a defined ontological status, which is what allows [[barzakh|the intermediate]] to be treated as a location where causation operates rather than a metaphor. 3. A scholastic vocabulary in which mystical claims could be stated as philosophical ones — the precondition for arguing that lettrism outranks philosophy on philosophy's own ground.

What "systematiser" meant in practice

It is worth being concrete about the difference Qūnavī made, because "he organised his master's teaching" undersells it.

[[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] writes at enormous length in a mode that is visionary, associative and self-interrupting. The Futūḥāt is not structured as a sequence of theses with grounds; it can be read, quoted and contemplated, but it cannot easily be disputed, because it does not present claims in a form that admits of objection and reply.

Qūnavī reorganised the material into that form. After him there are Akbarian positions, held for reasons, defensible against named opponents. That is what makes the school teachable in a madrasa and arguable in a disputation — and it is what allowed [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]], two centuries later, to argue with it rather than merely inherit it.

The cost is that what got transmitted is Qūnavī's Ibn ʿArabī. The [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] relies specifically on the Ibn ʿArabī school tradition as formulated by Qūnavī and [[jandi|Jandī]] (The Quest, 6) — not on the Futūḥāt directly.

The three things the school handed forward

1. The [[divine-names|divine names]] as an ordered structure rather than a devotional list — nameable, relatable, and therefore available to be mapped onto something else. 2. The [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]] with a defined ontological status, which is what permits [[barzakh|the intermediate]] to be treated as a place where causation happens rather than as a figure of speech. 3. A scholastic vocabulary in which mystical claims can be stated as philosophical ones.

Without the third, the claim that lettrism outranks philosophy could not even be formulated, let alone pressed. You cannot beat a discipline on its own ground without first learning to speak its language, and Qūnavī is where the Akbarian tradition learned to.
