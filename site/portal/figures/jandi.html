Jandi

ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Jandī (fl. 13th c.). Persian philosopher-mystic; student of Qunavi and successor in Illuminationist tradition. Elaborated the correspondence between divine names, letters, and cosmic principles. His commentaries on Ibn Arabi and development of divine-names epistemology became foundational to later Islamic occultism. Ibn Turka inherited and systematized Jandi's framework through the Qunavi-Jandi school tradition.

ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Jandī (fl. 13th century) was a Persian philosopher-mystic active in the post-Suhrawardi era. He is best known as a leading figure in the Qunavi-Jandi school tradition—the intellectual lineage that systematized Ibn Arabi's metaphysics for transmission to later generations. Where Qunavi had codified Ibn Arabi, Jandi extended and elaborated. His central contribution was developing the correspondence between divine names (asmaʾ), cosmic principles, and the structure of knowledge itself. Jandi argued that the 99 divine names are not merely attributes of God but constitute the framework through which creation manifests and through which the human intellect understands reality. Each name is a lens through which to perceive divine and created being. This doctrine proved transformative for later occultists: Ibn Turka adopted Jandi's framework and radicalized it by mapping the divine names onto the 28 Arabic letters, thereby making lettrism the operative expression of Jandi's metaphysics. Through Jandi, Ibn Turka inherited two centuries of Islamic philosophical synthesis: Ibn Arabi's metaphysics → Qunavi's systematization → Jandi's elaboration → Ibn Turka's operationalization.
