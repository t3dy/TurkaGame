Jandi

ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Jandī (fl. 13th c.). Persian philosopher-mystic; student of Qunavi and successor in Illuminationist tradition. Elaborated the correspondence between divine names, letters, and cosmic principles. His commentaries on Ibn Arabi and development of divine-names epistemology became foundational to later Islamic occultism. Ibn Turka inherited and systematized Jandi's framework through the Qunavi-Jandi school tradition.

ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Jandī (fl. 13th century) was a Persian philosopher-mystic active in the post-Suhrawardi era. He is best known as a leading figure in the Qunavi-Jandi school tradition—the intellectual lineage that systematized Ibn Arabi's metaphysics for transmission to later generations. Where Qunavi had codified Ibn Arabi, Jandi extended and elaborated. His central contribution was developing the correspondence between divine names (asmaʾ), cosmic principles, and the structure of knowledge itself. Jandi argued that the 99 divine names are not merely attributes of God but constitute the framework through which creation manifests and through which the human intellect understands reality. Each name is a lens through which to perceive divine and created being. This doctrine proved transformative for later occultists: Ibn Turka adopted Jandi's framework and radicalized it by mapping the divine names onto the 28 Arabic letters, thereby making lettrism the operative expression of Jandi's metaphysics. Through Jandi, Ibn Turka inherited two centuries of Islamic philosophical synthesis: Ibn Arabi's metaphysics → Qunavi's systematization → Jandi's elaboration → Ibn Turka's operationalization.

The elaboration, and why it mattered downstream

If [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] made Akbarian metaphysics arguable, Jandī extended it in the direction that lettrism would need: toward correspondence.

The doctrine of [[divine-names|divine names]] as the structure of manifestation invites an obvious question — if creation unfolds through named attributes, what else is ordered by the same set? Jandī's development, as the portal receives it through Melvin-Koushki's account of the school, presses the names into relation with the faculties of the knower and the levels of the cosmos, so that the same enumeration organises God, world and soul.

That is the structural precondition for [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]]. A correspondence system with the divine names on one side has an empty slot on the other, and Ibn Turka fills it with the twenty-eight letters. The move looks bold and is, but it is made into a framework already built to accept it.

The chain, stated plainly

The portal's genealogy runs: Ibn ʿArabī → Qūnavī → Jandī → [[saad-al-din-hamuyya|Ḥamūya]] → Ibn Turka, with [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] holding the Akbarian line together with the al-Būnī line in a single teaching at Cairo. Jandī is the least visible link and one of the more consequential, because his is the step at which a metaphysics of names becomes a system of correspondences.

What we do not know

  • His dates are not fixed in these sources beyond a floruit in the thirteenth century.
  • Which of his works Ibn Turka used, and whether directly or through intermediaries, is not established.
  • The account of his contribution here is synthesised from the school's shape rather than from a passage in the corpus describing it; treat it as MEDIUM confidence.
