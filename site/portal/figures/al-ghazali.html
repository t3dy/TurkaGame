Al-Ghazali

Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī (1058–1111). Theologian-philosopher whose Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn reconciled Sufi mysticism with Islamic orthodoxy. For this portal his importance is adversarial, not enabling: Melvin-Koushki lists him among the anti-occultist polemicists — with Ibn Sīnā, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn — whose four centuries of attack on the 'strange sciences' is precisely what Ibn Turka's mathematicalization of the occult sciences was built to answer.

Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad al-Ghazālī (1058–1111), born in Tus, taught at the Niẓāmiyya of Baghdad before a spiritual crisis drove him from public life into mystical retreat. His Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn argued that reason has limits, that mystical knowledge (dhawq, 'tasting') is genuine knowledge, and that Islamic intellectual life requires both. It became one of the most widely copied books in Islamic history, and the reconciliation it effected between Sufism and orthodoxy shaped everything after it.

Why he appears in a portal on Ibn Turka — and why not for the reason one expects. It is tempting to cast Ghazālī as the figure who cleared space for later occultists by vindicating mystical knowledge. Melvin-Koushki's account does not support that reading. He places Ghazālī squarely in the anti-occultist camp, observing that hostility to the ʿulūm al-gharība is one of the few subjects on which philosophers like Ibn Sīnā, theologians like Ghazālī, traditionalists like Ibn Taymiyya and historians like Ibn Khaldūn find common cause — a camp further boasting al-Ashʿarī, Fārābī, al-Bāqillānī and Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār (Powers of One, 1).

The consequence. Ghazālī belongs to the four centuries of polemic that Ibn Turka's generation had to answer, not to the resources it drew on. Vindicating mystical experience is not the same as vindicating an operative science of letters, and Ghazālī did the first while attacking the second. This is what makes the [[neoplatonism|move into the mathematical sciences]] necessary rather than merely elegant: the occultists needed ground that Ghazālī's own arguments could not reach. Reading him as an enabler obscures the very problem Ibn Turka set out to solve.

Why the correction matters beyond this entry

An earlier version of this page had Ghazālī enabling later occultists by vindicating mystical knowledge. That is a natural reading and it is wrong, and the reason it is wrong is worth stating because the same mistake is easy to make elsewhere in this material.

Vindicating mystical experience is not the same as vindicating an operative science. Ghazālī argued that dhawq — direct experiential knowledge — is genuine knowledge, and that reason has limits. Both claims are useful to a later occultist, and Ibn Turka's tradition does draw on the space they opened. But Ghazālī himself drew the opposite conclusion about the occult sciences, and Melvin-Koushki places him in the anti-occultist camp: hostility to the ʿulūm al-gharība is one of the few subjects on which philosophers like Ibn Sīnā, theologians like Ghazālī, traditionalists like Ibn Taymiyya and historians like Ibn Khaldūn find common cause, joined by al-Ashʿarī, Fārābī, al-Bāqillānī and Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār (Powers of One, 1).

The size of the opposition

That list is the point of this entry. It is not a fringe of hostile jurists; it is the mainstream of Islamic intellectual life across four centuries and every major discipline — philosophy, theology, traditionism, historiography, and the two great schools of kalām. Anti-occultism was the common ground on which figures who agreed about almost nothing else could stand together.

Understanding that changes how Ibn Turka's project reads. He was not defending a comfortable orthodoxy against cranks. He was attempting to move a body of disciplines out of a category in which the entire prestige structure of his tradition had placed them — which is why the answer had to be [[pythagorean-cosmology|jurisdictional]] rather than merely argumentative. Persuading this coalition on its own terms was not available; reclassifying the sciences into a field whose credentials it did not dispute was.

What Ghazālī does supply

The Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn remains one of the most widely copied books in Islamic history, and the settlement it effected between Sufism and orthodoxy is the intellectual weather every later figure works in — including Ibn Turka, whose relations with [[sufism|organised Sufism]] are conducted inside a framework Ghazālī largely built. The debt is real. It is simply not a debt about the occult sciences.

What we do not know

  • What Ghazālī says specifically about lettrism, as against the occult sciences generally, is not detailed in the corpus in hand.
  • Whether Ibn Turka answers him by name anywhere in the unedited works.
