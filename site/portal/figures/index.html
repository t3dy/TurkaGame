Figures

20 entries.

Akhlati

Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī (d. 1397), Kurdish occultist, alchemist, and lettrist mystic. The pivot of the "New Brethren of Purity" network stretching from Cairo through Anatolia to Iran, Central Asia, and India. Served as physician and confidant at the court of Mamluk sultan Barqūq (r. 1382–1399). Akhlāṭī's oral teachings on lettrism, alchemy, and talismanic magic shaped Ibn Turka, who became his principal systematizer and heir. Though Akhlāṭī left few written works, his influence on Islamic occultism proved foundational and lasting.

teacher

Al-Ghazali

Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī (1058–1111). Theologian-philosopher whose Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn reconciled Sufi mysticism with Islamic orthodoxy. For this portal his importance is adversarial, not enabling: Melvin-Koushki lists him among the anti-occultist polemicists — with Ibn Sīnā, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn — whose four centuries of attack on the 'strange sciences' is precisely what Ibn Turka's mathematicalization of the occult sciences was built to answer.

anti-occultist predecessor

Bāysunghur

Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh (1397–1433), Timurid prince, governor, and the greatest book-patron of the age — his Herat kitābkhāna was the finest manuscript workshop in the world. The dissertation records Ibn Turka's close relationship to Bāysunghur in particular among the Timurid amirs. He is the middle term of the patron sequence between [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]].

patron

Ibn Arabi

Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240), Andalusian mystic and philosopher. Founder of the metaphysical school most influential on later Islamic occultism and mysticism. Author of the Futūḥāt al-Makkīyya (Meccan Illuminations) and other foundational works. His doctrines of [[divine-names|divine names]], the imaginal realm, and the macrocosm-microcosm correspondence profoundly influenced [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka's]] theoretical framework. Via the [[qunavi|Qūnavī]]-[[jandi|Jandī]] transmission, Ibn Arabi's philosophy became the philosophical grounding for Ibn Turka's lettrist innovations.

spiritual_predecessor

Ibn Turka

Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī (1369–1432), Chief Judge of Isfahan and Yazd. The foremost occult philosopher of Timurid Iran and the first to systematize Islamic lettrism (ʿilm al-ḥurūf) into a coherent, Pythagorean-modeled philosophical cosmology. Leader of the Isfahan Circle and spiritual heir to Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī, Ibn Turka authored over 45 works across philosophy, mysticism, grammar, rhetoric, and occult science. His synthesis of lettrism and Neoplatonic philosophy became canonical; virtually every Islamic thinker engaging with lettrism after 1450 worked through his categories.

Iskandar Sulṭān

Iskandar Sulṭān (1384–1415), Temür's grandson and ruler of Fars — Ibn Turka's first and most consequential patron. He commissioned Of Letters, and it was almost certainly at his instance that Ibn Turka began the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Book of Inquiries]]. His court was famous for astrological and occult patronage on a scale that set a model; Shāhrukh marched against him in 817/1414; he fled, was captured and blinded, and was executed afterwards — removing Ibn Turka's protection at a stroke.

patron

Ismail I

Shah Ismail I (1487–1524), founder of Safavid dynasty. Established Twelver Shi'ism as state religion of Persia. Created court environment that canonized Ibn Turka's intellectual tradition. Safavid support transformed Ibn Turka from Isfahan Circle figure to foundational voice of Islamic philosophy.

political_successor

Jandi

ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Jandī (fl. 13th c.). Persian philosopher-mystic; student of Qunavi and successor in Illuminationist tradition. Elaborated the correspondence between divine names, letters, and cosmic principles. His commentaries on Ibn Arabi and development of divine-names epistemology became foundational to later Islamic occultism. Ibn Turka inherited and systematized Jandi's framework through the Qunavi-Jandi school tradition.

intellectual_ancestor

Mulla Sadra

Mullā Ṣadrā (1571–1635), the most celebrated philosopher of Safavid Iran, conventionally named as the terminus of the line running from Ibn ʿArabī through Ibn Turka. This portal keeps the entry short on purpose. Ṣadrā appears twice in the forty-three sources it is built on, and the Safavid heir the scholarship actually documents is [[mir-damad|Mīr Dāmād]].

intellectual_successor

Mīr Dāmād

Mīr Dāmād (d. 1631), Twelver Shiʿi philosopher of Safavid Isfahan and — on Melvin-Koushki's argument — the man largely responsible for the neopythagoreanization of Safavid philosophy. He did it by adopting a particular brand of Mamluk–Timurid–Aqquyunlu philosophical [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] in at least three of his works. He is the documented link by which Ibn Turka's science reaches the seventeenth century, and the reason the tradition's chief opponent ends up inside it.

the documented Safavid heir

Qazizada Rumi

Qāżīzāda Rūmī (c. 1380–1432), celebrated mathematician and astronomer. Member of the Isfahan Circle; friend and colleague to [[ibn-turka]]. Active in Samarkand at Timur's court and later in Isfahan and Yazd. Pioneer of mathematical methods in astronomy; integrated mathematical and occult approaches to celestial phenomena. Represents the mathematicization of Islamic astronomy during the early Timurid period.

peer

Qāsim-i Anvār

Qāsim-i Anvār (c. 1356–1433), Sufi poet, propagandist of the Ṣafaviyya order, and Ibn Turka's charismatic associate from his Cairo days. Exiled from Herat with him in 1427. Claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own, suspected of Ḥurūfī ties: the friendship was a liability, and the record shows exactly what kind.

associate and co-exile

Qūnavī

Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnavī (1210–1274), leading disciple and systematizer of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] metaphysics. Through his philosophical elaboration of Ibn Arabi's doctrines, Qūnavī created the scholastic framework that transmitted Ibn Arabi's thought to later Islamic philosophers and occultists. His work provided the philosophical grounding for [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka's]] integration of divine-names doctrine with lettrist and Pythagorean theory. A central figure in the Qūnavī-[[jandi|Jandī]] school tradition.

spiritual_ancestor

Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya

Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya (d. 649/1252), Kubrawi shaykh and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī's]] Iranian contemporary — and, with Ibn ʿArabī, Ibn Turka's favourite lettrist source. Melvin-Koushki calls him Ibn Turka's only other identified source, with an oeuvre even more centrally lettrist, and even more slippery, than Ibn ʿArabī's. He once criticised Ibn ʿArabī by letter for being too rigid in his lettrism.

precursor

Sharaf al-Din Yazdi

Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī (c. 1370s–1454), scholar, historian, and occult philosopher. One of the two most prominent intellectuals of Shahrukhid Iran (identified with Ibn Turka by Dawlatshah Samarqandi). Member of the Isfahan Circle; author of the Ẓafarnāma (universal history from an astrological-lettrist perspective). Integrated [[ilm-al-huruf]] with historical and political analysis. Represents the application of lettrist philosophy to the writing of history and the interpretation of empire.

disciple and closest associate

Shāhrukh

Shāhrukh (r. 1405–1447), Tīmūr's son and the ruler under whom Ibn Turka spent almost his entire career. He is not a background figure: he destroyed Ibn Turka's first patron, later received him back into favour, and his court was the venue of the accusations that ended in exile. In 813/1411 he abrogated the Mongol yasa in favour of the sharīʿa and closed the taverns — the turn toward public piety that made an occult philosopher's position permanently precarious.

patron and prosecutor

Suhrawardi

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (1154–1191). Founder of Illuminationist (Ishraqi) philosophy. Synthesized Neoplatonic emanationism with Islamic theology, emphasizing direct mystical intuition (ishraq; illumination) of divine light and angelic powers. Executed in Aleppo for his teachings. His system profoundly influenced later Islamic philosophers and occultists, including Ibn Turka, who adopted Illuminationist vocabulary and framework to ground lettrist doctrine.

intellectual_ancestor

Ulugh Beg

Ulugh Beg (1394–1449), Shāhrukh's son, governor of Transoxiana and founder of the Samarkand Observatory — by a distance the most famous Timurid for history-of-science purposes. He was styled al-sulṭān al-faylasūf, the philosopher-sultan. For Ibn Turka he is the would-be third patron: the one ruler whose programme — the mathematicalization of the sciences — was the same programme, and whose patronage never quite arrived.

would-be patron

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī (d. 1454) — the Ottoman arm of the same project. Like Ibn Turka an intellectual heir of [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]], he built a universalist lettrist programme in the Ottoman context that Melvin-Koushki calls cognate and more successful. Whether the two men ever met during their long residences in Mamluk Cairo is unclear; that they were doing the same thing at opposite ends of the network is not.

cognate; fellow heir of Akhlāṭī

Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka

Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka, Ibn Turka's elder brother and his teacher for the first twenty-five years of his life — alongside a fellow student who would become [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]]. An eminent scholar of hadith, tafsīr, jurisprudence and uṣūl al-dīn. Ibn Turka's own verdict on this education is pointed: his brother taught nothing beyond the outward sciences.

elder brother and first teacher
