Qāsim-i Anvār

Qāsim-i Anvār (c. 1356–1433), Sufi poet, propagandist of the Ṣafaviyya order, and Ibn Turka's charismatic associate from his Cairo days. Exiled from Herat with him in 1427. Claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own, suspected of Ḥurūfī ties: the friendship was a liability, and the record shows exactly what kind.

Qāsim-i Anvār was a Sufi poet of great popular reach and a propagandist of the Ṣafaviyya order — the movement that would, three generations later, become the Safavid dynasty. He and Ibn Turka knew each other from Cairo, and the dissertation calls him Ṣāʾin al-Dīn's charismatic associate from his Cairo days (The Quest, 456 n.16).

The liability. Qāsim was apparently claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own, at a moment when that was a dangerous thing to be: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] attempted a purge of Ismāʿīlīs in Quhistan in 1415. In 1427 Qāsim was exiled from Herat, and Ibn Turka with him, on account of such suspect connections — though the official excuse was his putative connection with the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfīs]] (The Quest, 456 n.16).

The detail worth holding onto is that the stated charge and the operative reason were different. Ibn Turka was not expelled for a doctrine he held; he was expelled for a man he knew, under a label that would carry in court.

Against the caricature of Ibn Turka the isolate. His letters show friendly relations with a whole set of prominent Iranian Sufis — Shāh Niʿmat Allāh Valī, Qāsim-i Anvār, Aḥmad Samarqandī, Ḥasan ʿAṭṭār, Sayyid Muḥammad b. Shaykh Kajujī — which sits oddly beside the criticisms of organised [[sufism|Sufism]] he makes in his written work. Melvin-Koushki reads the tension as informative rather than contradictory: the first three of those men were great promoters of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] against often hostile reaction, and that is the likely point of attraction (The Quest, 136). He criticised the institution and kept the company.

The company that cost him

The 1427 expulsion is the clearest case in the biography of a man being punished for an association rather than a doctrine, and the mechanics deserve setting out.

Qāsim was a propagandist of the Ṣafaviyya order and was apparently claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own (The Quest, 456 n.16). Neither is a doctrinal charge against Ibn Turka; both are facts about someone he knew. And the timing made them dangerous: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] had attempted a purge of Ismāʿīlīs in Quhistan in 1415, so the label named an active security concern rather than a theological abstraction.

The stated charge was different again. What provided the official excuse was Qāsim's putative connection with the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfīs]]. So the sequence runs: a real association (Ṣafaviyya), an alleged one (Ismāʿīlī), and a third label used in the paperwork (Ḥurūfī). Three different accusations, none of them about Ibn Turka's own work, producing one exile.

The corrective to the isolated-philosopher picture

His letters show friendly relations with a set of prominent Iranian Sufis — Shāh Niʿmat Allāh Valī, Qāsim-i Anvār, Aḥmad Samarqandī, Ḥasan ʿAṭṭār, Sayyid Muḥammad b. Shaykh Kajujī — which sits oddly beside his criticisms of organised [[sufism|Sufism]] in print, and beside the admonitory role toward shaykhs he claims in the [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]].

Melvin-Koushki reads the tension as informative rather than hypocritical: the first three of those men were great promoters of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] in the face of often hostile reaction, which suggests the point of attraction (The Quest, 136). He criticised the institution and kept the company, and the company was selected on doctrinal grounds.

The long irony

The Ṣafaviyya became the Safavid dynasty. Ibn Turka was exiled for association with the movement whose eventual state would host the [[mir-damad|neopythagorean turn]] in which his own science became establishment philosophy.

What we do not know

  • Where Qāsim went after the 1427 expulsion, and whether he and Ibn Turka were together in exile.
  • Whether the Ismāʿīlī claim had any substance, or was a hostile attribution.
  • Almost nothing of his poetry is discussed in the sources in hand, though its popular reach is what made him worth denouncing.
