ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī (d. 1454) — the Ottoman arm of the same project. Like Ibn Turka an intellectual heir of [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]], he built a universalist lettrist programme in the Ottoman context that Melvin-Koushki calls cognate and more successful. Whether the two men ever met during their long residences in Mamluk Cairo is unclear; that they were doing the same thing at opposite ends of the network is not.

The other half of the network

Who he is. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī (d. 858/1454) is the leading figure of the [[new-brethren-purity|Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ]] network in the Ottoman lands, and the closest thing Ibn Turka has to a counterpart. The dissertation names them together: the Isfahan Circle may be seen as the eastern arm of a larger network stretching to Anatolia via Cairo, and they were both the intellectual heirs of Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī, though it is unclear whether they met during their respective long residences in Mamluk Cairo (The Quest, 2).

Why he matters to a portal about Ibn Turka. Because of the comparative verdict. Melvin-Koushki describes the Ottoman projects — Bisṭāmī's above all — as cognate and more successful than Ibn Turka's (The Quest, ch. 1). The same programme, inherited from the same teacher, prospered in Anatolia while its Iranian version ended in three tribunals and an exile. That asymmetry is one of the sharpest questions the material raises, and it is a question about politics rather than about ideas.

The scholarship. The existence of this network as a network was posited by İhsan Fazlıoğlu in a 1996 article, and Melvin-Koushki states plainly that his own understanding of late medieval Islamic occult philosophy depends on the ongoing research of Cornell Fleischer and his Chicago Circle into the Ottoman branch. Logistically and conceptually the two networks are largely coterminous, so research on either has vital bearing on the whole complex (The Quest, ch. 1 n.87–88). Anyone reading this portal should know that half the story is being written by other people.

The book that became state equipment

The concrete measure of the Ottoman branch's success is a single title. Bisṭāmī's Miftāḥ al-jafr al-jāmiʿ — the Key to the Comprehensive Prognosticon — came to function in the 10th/16th century as a dynastic handbook, whereby the political fate of the House of ʿOs̱mān could be effectively managed over the generations (Another Scientific Revolution, 2).

Set that beside Ibn Turka's career. Same teacher, same science, same generation. One man's [[jafr|jafr]] manual becomes an instrument of imperial governance consulted across centuries; the other man is tried three times, exiled, and dies waiting on a case review with half his work unedited.

Melvin-Koushki's judgement that the Ottoman projects were cognate and more successful is not a vague comparative impression. This is what it refers to.

What the asymmetry is actually about

Not doctrine. The inheritance from [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] was the same and the two men's programmes are described as cognate. What differed was the state.

[[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] had [[yasa-abrogated|refounded Timurid legitimacy on the sharīʿa]] in 813/1411 and elevated the class of men who interpret it — creating both the incentive and the vocabulary for prosecuting an occult philosopher. The Ottoman court of the same decades made room for exactly this kind of universalist project, and then institutionalised it.

The comparison is the strongest available argument that Ibn Turka's fate was contingent rather than deserved: the same science, in a different polity, became a manual for running an empire.
