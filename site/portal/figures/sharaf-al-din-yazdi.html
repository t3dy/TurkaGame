Sharaf al-Din Yazdi

Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī (c. 1370s–1454), scholar, historian, and occult philosopher. One of the two most prominent intellectuals of Shahrukhid Iran (identified with Ibn Turka by Dawlatshah Samarqandi). Member of the Isfahan Circle; author of the Ẓafarnāma (universal history from an astrological-lettrist perspective). Integrated [[ilm-al-huruf]] with historical and political analysis. Represents the application of lettrist philosophy to the writing of history and the interpretation of empire.

Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī (c. 1370s–1454) was among the most accomplished intellectuals of 15th-century Iran. Born in Yazd, he emerged as one of the two leading figures of his age, identified by the historian Dawlatshah Samarqandi (d. 1494–1507) as equal in prominence to [[ibn-turka]] himself. Unlike Ibn Turka, who was primarily a philosopher and occultist, Sharaf al-Dīn applied lettrist and astrological principles to the practice of history-writing and political analysis. His magnum opus, the Ẓafarnāma (Book of Victories), is a universal history organized according to astrological and lettrist principles—demonstrating that history itself unfolds according to cosmic cycles encoded in letters and divine names. This work became canonical in the Timurid and Safavid periods. Sharaf al-Dīn's synthesis of lettrism, astrology, and historiography shows how Ibn Turka's philosophical framework could be applied practically to statecraft and the interpretation of events.

Correction on the relationship. An earlier version of this entry described Yazdī as Ibn Turka's peer. The dissertation is more specific: he is his disciple and closest associate. The Munshaʾāt-i Yazdī preserves eight items relating to Ibn Turka or his immediate family, showing Yazdī's attendance at his teacher's majālis, his accompanying Ibn Turka on journeys to Herat, and his concern for Ibn Turka's children (makhdūm-zādagān) after their father's death (The Quest, 135–36). The bond is pedagogical and personal, not collegial — which is what makes Yazdī the one figure positioned to carry the system forward.

The relationship, documented from his side

The evidence for the closeness is unusual in shape: it survives chiefly in Yazdī's archive rather than Ibn Turka's. Only one letter to Yazdī appears in the [[munshaat-i-turka|Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka]], from the first stage of Ibn Turka's career in Fars; Melvin-Koushki suggests the rest were simply too informal to be thought worth preserving.

The Munshaʾāt-i Yazdī, by contrast, contains eight items relating to Ibn Turka or his immediate family, and they sketch the relationship far more adequately: Yazdī's attendance at his teacher's majālis, his accompanying Ibn Turka on journeys to Herat, and his concern for Ibn Turka's children (makhdūm-zādagān) after their father's death (The Quest, 135–36).

That last item is the one that settles the character of the bond. Looking after a dead colleague's children is not a professional courtesy.

From the beginning of the journey

The association predates the Timurid courts entirely. The chronology places it at the start: around 795/1393 Ibn Turka leaves Samarkand for Mecca and Cairo, and Sharaf al-Dīn Yazdī accompanies him. Yazdī spent at least part of the period 795–810/1393–1408 in Cairo with him (Powers of One, 37).

So the fifteen years of formation under [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] were shared. Yazdī is not a disciple acquired at the height of Ibn Turka's career; he is the man who was there for the whole of it.

The Cairo overlap with Ibn Khaldūn

One tantalising possibility follows. Ibn Khaldūn lived in Cairo from 785/1383 until his death in 808/1406, overlapping Yazdī's residence there, and Melvin-Koushki notes that the two historians possibly met (Powers of One, 37).

The interest is obvious: Ibn Khaldūn is the most formidable critic of the occult sciences in the tradition, and Yazdī would go on to write in defence of divination against exactly that critique. Whether the defence was written by a man who had argued with him in person is unknown and would change how one reads it.

What we do not know

  • Whether he and Ibn Khaldūn actually met — "possibly" is as far as the evidence goes.
  • The bulk of the correspondence between him and Ibn Turka is lost.
  • How far he shared the lettrist doctrine as opposed to applying its methods to history is not resolved in the sources in hand.

The historian, and the argument he is used to make

Yazdī is described as dynastic historian to the Timurids and committed occultist (In Defense of Geomancy, 349) — and the two halves of that description are not in tension for him, which is the point.

His best-known work is the Ẓafarnāma, an ornate, long-admired and much-imitated account of Tīmūr's career. Less widely known: it is one of five separate chronicles he began (ibid., n.3). The output is that of a professional historian, not an occultist with a sideline.

Against Ibn Khaldūn — but not in the expected direction

Melvin-Koushki stages Yazdī against Ibn Khaldūn (d. 808/1406), and the confrontation inverts both reputations.

Ibn Khaldūn's supposed skepticism about the occult sciences is much admired, held up as a shining, solitary example of rationality amidst rampant superstition. What is less acknowledged is that he argues forcefully, and at length, for the incontrovertible reality of magic and divination, with the selfsame sober objectivity. He rejected astrology and alchemy; he did not reject the occult as such.

And Yazdī, from the other side, produces theories of historical evolution and human potential not dissimilar to Ibn Khaldūn's more famous formulations — yet predicated precisely on a thoroughgoingly occultist outlook.

Two historians of one generation reaching comparable accounts of how societies develop from opposite metaphysical premises. Both men's views on occultism go to the heart of their respective sociologies and political theologies, which accordingly converge and diverge in instructive ways.

This is what the portal's intersection essay on the defence of divination is about, and it is a better story than rationalism versus credulity.

The Cairo overlap again

Ibn Khaldūn lived in Cairo from 785/1383 until his death in 808/1406; Yazdī spent at least part of 795–810/1393–1408 there with Ibn Turka, and Melvin-Koushki notes the two historians possibly met (Powers of One, 37). If the Ẓafarnāma's occultist philosophy of history was written by a man who had argued with Ibn Khaldūn in person, it reads differently.
