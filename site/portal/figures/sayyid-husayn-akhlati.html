Akhlati

Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī (d. 1397), Kurdish occultist, alchemist, and lettrist mystic. The pivot of the "New Brethren of Purity" network stretching from Cairo through Anatolia to Iran, Central Asia, and India. Served as physician and confidant at the court of Mamluk sultan Barqūq (r. 1382–1399). Akhlāṭī's oral teachings on lettrism, alchemy, and talismanic magic shaped Ibn Turka, who became his principal systematizer and heir. Though Akhlāṭī left few written works, his influence on Islamic occultism proved foundational and lasting.

Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī (d. 1397)

The Pivot of the Network

Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī (also known as the Tabrizi, a reference to his Kurdish origins in Tabriz/Ahlat region) died in Cairo in 799/1397, at an unknown age. Yet despite the paucity of biographical detail—his birth date is unknown, few of his writings survive—Akhlāṭī stands as the intellectual and spiritual center of what Melvin-Koushki calls the "New Brethren of Purity," a transregional network of occultists operating at the highest levels of power across the late medieval Islamic world.

The network stretched from Cairo (where Akhlāṭī held court) northward to Ottoman Anatolia and eastward through western Iran, Central Asia, and eventually India. Its members called themselves cryptically the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ (Brethren of Purity)—deliberately invoking the medieval encyclopedists of the 10th century—and shared a common project: the retrieval, systematization, and open promulgation of the occult sciences (ʿulūm al-gharība) as a tool for human evolution and imperial power.

Life and Position

Akhlāṭī's prominence in Mamluk Cairo is documented in contemporary sources and visual art. A manuscript of the Majālis al-ʿUshshāq (Assemblies of Lovers) from Shiraz (BL Or. 11837 f. 117, ca. 1560) depicts an illumination showing Akhlāṭī receiving gifts from the Mamluk sultan Barqūq (r. 784–801/1382–1399). Two figures who may represent Ibn Turka and [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi]] look on as their master is honored by the sultan—a visual record of Akhlāṭī's extraordinary status as both scholar and court confidant.

Akhlāṭī served as physician to Barqūq and held intellectual authority on matters of alchemy, medicine, geomancy, and lettrism. The sources consistently describe him as a muʿajjiz (miracle-worker), indicating that his reputation extended beyond scholarly circles into the world of practical theurgy and wonder-working. He was, in short, a public intellectual at the apex of political power, able to advocate openly for the occult sciences in an environment where such advocacy could be dangerous.

Teachings and Method

Akhlāṭī's primary influence came through oral teaching rather than written treatises. He conducted regular scholarly gatherings (majālis) where he taught lettrism, alchemical theory, and talismanic operation. Unlike the gnostic-messianic lettrist movement of Fażl Allāh Astarābādī (d. 1394), which emphasized popular mysticism and ecstatic experience, Akhlāṭī's teaching was philosophical and technical. He integrated lettrist theory with Neoplatonic metaphysics, Islamic peripatetic philosophy, and practical alchemy—demonstrating that letters, divine names, and numerical values form a coherent system for understanding and manipulating reality.

Ibn Turka, who absorbed these teachings directly in Cairo before Akhlāṭī's death in 1397, repeatedly credits Akhlāṭī in his own writings. In the introduction to his magisterial Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ (Book of Inquiries), Ibn Turka names Akhlāṭī as one of his three primary sources (alongside Ibn ʿArabī and Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya). Yet while Ibn Turka consistently emphasizes that he is merely "the systematizer of Akhlāṭī's teachings," the reality is more subtle: Akhlāṭī provided the intellectual raw material and the occultist worldview, but Ibn Turka transformed oral teachings into a written, rigorous, Pythagorean-modeled philosophical system. Akhlāṭī pioneered; Ibn Turka systematized.

Influence and Legacy

Akhlāṭī's death in 1397 marked a turning point. With him gone, the New Brethren network began to decentralize. Yet his influence spread outward rather than contracting. Ibn Turka carried his teachings to Isfahan and Yazd, establishing them at the highest levels of Timurid power. Other disciples—[[sharaf-al-din-yazdi]], [[qazizada-rumi]], and unnamed others—propagated his methods across the Islamic world. By the 15th and 16th centuries, Akhlāṭī's fame had grown even as his original writings disappeared. Every Islamic occultist engaging with lettrism at scholarly levels was working within a framework Akhlāṭī had established.

Modern historiography, influenced by orientalist dismissal of "Islamic superstition," long sidelined Akhlāṭī as a marginal figure. Melvin-Koushki's work has demonstrated that Akhlāṭī was in fact a central intellectual figure—not in the narrow sense of academic philosophy, but in the broader sense of late medieval intellectual history. He was doing what Renaissance mages in Europe would later do: attempting to create a universal science integrating reason, mysticism, natural magic, and mathematics into a coherent system for understanding and transforming the world.

Sources

Akhlāṭī is mentioned frequently in Ibn Turka's surviving works and in the writings of other members of the Isfahan Circle. Some short treatises attributed to him survive in manuscript, particularly on the properties of letters and divine names. The most sustained modern treatment remains Melvin-Koushki's dissertation and subsequent articles, which have excavated Akhlāṭī from near-total obscurity and positioned him as the intellectual hub of a vast occultist network.

The office that put him at the centre

The single fact that organises Akhlāṭī's career is institutional rather than doctrinal: he was summoned to Cairo to serve as personal physician to Sultan Barqūq (r. 784–801/ 1382–1399), and Melvin-Koushki calls him more precisely Barqūq's personal physician-alchemist (Imperial Talismanic Love, 70; Powers of One, 6).

A court physician has something no other kind of scholar has: unsupervised, repeated, private access to the ruler's body. That access is the mechanism behind everything else. Under his influence Barqūq seems to have developed a keen interest in the occult sciences, and the evidence Melvin-Koushki adduces is exactly the evidence a historian would want — not testimony about the sultan's opinions but his personal library and his choice of courtiers, among whom was Ibn Turka himself.

From this Melvin-Koushki puts a question and answers it: was Barqūq the first Mamluk sultan to aspire to occult-philosopher-kingship, and so an inspiration for Timurid rivals? Likely so. If that is right, Akhlāṭī did not merely teach a generation of occultists; he converted a throne, and the model of the occult court that the Timurids would develop has a Cairene prototype.

Heir of two traditions at once

Melvin-Koushki's characterisation is compact and worth spelling out: Akhlāṭī is the conscious heir of both al-Būnī and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]], and master to the most important occult philosophers of the following generation (Powers of One, 6).

Those are two different inheritances and they do not obviously combine.

  • From al-Būnī comes operative talismanic lettrism: the Shams al-Maʿārif tradition, magic squares, divine names deployed for effect, a practical literature of procedures.
  • From Ibn ʿArabī comes a metaphysics: the [[divine-names|divine names]] as the structure through which being manifests, the [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]], a contemplative rather than operative orientation.

Holding both consciously is the achievement. The manual and the metaphysics are joined in one teacher before Ibn Turka receives them, which is why his own synthesis can take the combination for granted and spend its effort on the [[pythagorean-cosmology|mathematics]] instead.

The pivot of a network

The dissertation describes Akhlāṭī as the pivot of the network that would call itself, cryptically, the [[new-brethren-purity|Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ]] — a network stretching from Anatolia through Cairo to Iran and India. He proclaimed a new era of human development through the retrieval and open promulgation of the occult sciences (The Quest, 2).

Two heirs matter most, and their divergence is the network's central comparative fact. Ibn Turka is the eastern arm; [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī]] is the Ottoman one. They were both the intellectual heirs of Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī, and it is unclear whether they met during their respective long residences in Mamluk Cairo (The Quest, 2). The same teaching went west and east; Melvin-Koushki judges the western outcome more successful.

How Ibn Turka positioned himself in relation to him

Not as an innovator. Ibn Turka presents himself as simply the systematizer of Akhlāṭī's teachings, and was received as such in the later lettrist tradition (The Quest, 2).

This self-presentation should be read with care in both directions. It is partly genuine intellectual debt and partly the ordinary prudence of a man in a dangerous field, for whom a chain of transmission is a defence. But it also understates what he did: Akhlāṭī does not appear to have grounded lettrism in Pythagorean mathematics or argued it into the [[occult-science-universal|mathematical sciences]]. The systematiser's modesty conceals a reclassification.

What we do not know

  • Almost nothing of Akhlāṭī's own writing is discussed in the sources in hand; he is known chiefly through his students and his position.
  • Whether Bisṭāmī and Ibn Turka met under him is unresolved.
  • The claim that Barqūq aspired to occult-philosopher-kingship is offered as "likely", not as established.
  • His death in 799/1397 in Cairo is firm; his birth and origins are not treated in these sources.
