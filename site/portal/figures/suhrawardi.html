Suhrawardi

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (1154–1191). Founder of Illuminationist (Ishraqi) philosophy. Synthesized Neoplatonic emanationism with Islamic theology, emphasizing direct mystical intuition (ishraq; illumination) of divine light and angelic powers. Executed in Aleppo for his teachings. His system profoundly influenced later Islamic philosophers and occultists, including Ibn Turka, who adopted Illuminationist vocabulary and framework to ground lettrist doctrine.

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (1154–1191), known as Shaykh al-Ishraq (Master of Illumination), was born in Suhrawardi, Persian Azerbaijan. He became the founder of the Illuminationist (Ishraqi) school of Islamic philosophy—a synthesis of Neoplatonic emanationism, Islamic theology, and visionary mysticism. Suhrawardi's key innovation: reality unfolds through levels of light. The divine is pure light (nur); creation emanates downward through successively dimmer grades of illumination; each level manifests divine attributes. Crucially, knowledge of these levels is not rational inference but direct mystical intuition (ishraq—illumination, sudden gnosis). In Suhrawardi's system, the soul possesses the capacity for immediate perception of intelligible forms, celestial beings, and divine light. This is not imagination but genuine knowledge. Suhrawardi was executed in Aleppo in 1191, likely for heterodox teachings. Yet his influence proved immense: Illuminationism became the dominant philosophical framework for later Islamic thinkers. Ibn Arabi adopted Illuminationist language; Qunavi built on it; Safavid philosophers canonized it. For Ibn Turka, Suhrawardi's framework was essential: the doctrine that reality manifests through levels of light, that the soul can directly perceive intelligible realities, and that mystical intuition is genuine knowledge—all this authorized Ibn Turka's claim that letters are cosmic principles knowable through direct gnosis and operative through talismanic technique.

What Illuminationism supplied

Two things Ibn Turka's project could not do without.

First, the legitimation of non-discursive knowledge. Suhrawardi's ishrāq is illumination — knowledge that arrives by direct presence rather than by inference. Once that is granted standing as knowledge rather than as private experience, the whole [[walaya|category of sanctified knowing]] has a philosophical basis, and a science that claims access unavailable to ordinary reasoning is not thereby disqualified.

Second, a graded cosmos of light. Reality as hierarchies of luminosity, each level receiving from the one above, gives the framework in which [[barzakh|an intermediate domain]] is a natural feature rather than an ad-hoc addition. The Kitāb al-Nūr takes up this vocabulary directly.

The synthesis he made possible

The Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid is described as a synthesis of Ibn Sīnā, Suhrawardī and Ibn ʿArabī/Qūnavī on the topic of absolute being (The Quest, 6). Those three names are the three available philosophical positions of the period — peripatetic, illuminationist, Akbarian — and the fact that Ibn Turka is credited with reconciling them is precisely what earned him his modern reputation as a mystical philosopher.

The portal's argument is that the reconciliation was preliminary work. Having shown that the three could be held together, he went on to argue that all three are superseded by a fourth thing. The synthesis is the platform, not the achievement.

The precedent of his death

Suhrawardi was executed at Aleppo in 1191, at around thirty-six, for teachings judged dangerous. For a fifteenth-century philosopher working on the boundary of the permissible, this is not distant history but the field's standing warning. Ibn Turka faced three tribunals and an exile; the tradition he drew on had a martyr in it, and everyone concerned knew it.

What we do not know

  • How directly Ibn Turka read Suhrawardi, as against receiving Illuminationism through later commentators, is not established in these sources.
  • The precise charges against Suhrawardi at Aleppo are outside this corpus.

Illumination as a claim about knowledge, not about mood

Ishrāq is easy to soften into atmosphere. It is not atmosphere; it is an epistemological claim, and a strong one: that some knowledge arrives by direct presence rather than by inference from premises, and that such knowledge is not inferior to demonstrative knowledge but prior to it.

Grant that, and a great deal follows for anyone arguing about the occult sciences. A discipline claiming access unavailable to ordinary syllogistic reasoning is no longer disqualified by that fact alone. It is the general form of the licence that [[walaya|walāya]] gives in the particular case — sanctified knowing as a real mode of knowing.

The cosmos as graded light

The second inheritance is structural. Reality as hierarchies of luminosity, each level receiving from the one above and transmitting to the one below, supplies a framework in which [[barzakh|an intermediate domain]] is a natural feature rather than an ad-hoc insertion. Ibn Turka's cosmology of intelligible, imaginal and sensible is Suhrawardi's architecture with different furniture.

The vocabulary carries too. The [[divine-names|divine name]] Light (Nūr) does real work in the circle's arguments — [[geomancy|Yazdī's defence of geomancy]] turns on geomancy's numerological correspondence to it, and Melvin-Koushki's gloss is that given the central role of light in the lettrist metaphysics of Ibn Turka's circle, Yazdī is effectively declaring Q.E.D.

The three-way synthesis, and why it was only the platform

The [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] is a synthesis of Ibn Sīnā, Suhrawardī and Ibn ʿArabī/Qūnavī on absolute being — the three available philosophical positions of the period, reconciled. That reconciliation is what earned Ibn Turka his modern reputation.

The portal's argument is that it was preliminary work. Having shown the three could be held together, he went on to argue that all three are superseded by a fourth thing. The synthesis is the platform, not the achievement — which is precisely what a reputation built on the Tamhīd cannot see.

The precedent nobody in the field could forget

Suhrawardi was executed at Aleppo in 1191, aged around thirty-six, for teachings judged dangerous. For a fifteenth-century philosopher working the boundary of the permissible this is not distant history but the discipline's standing warning — and Ibn Turka faced three tribunals and an exile. His brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] taught only the outward sciences because he deemed it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public. The tradition had a martyr, and everyone concerned knew it.
