Suhrawardi

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (1154–1191). Founder of Illuminationist (Ishraqi) philosophy. Synthesized Neoplatonic emanationism with Islamic theology, emphasizing direct mystical intuition (ishraq; illumination) of divine light and angelic powers. Executed in Aleppo for his teachings. His system profoundly influenced later Islamic philosophers and occultists, including Ibn Turka, who adopted Illuminationist vocabulary and framework to ground lettrist doctrine.

Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (1154–1191), known as Shaykh al-Ishraq (Master of Illumination), was born in Suhrawardi, Persian Azerbaijan. He became the founder of the Illuminationist (Ishraqi) school of Islamic philosophy—a synthesis of Neoplatonic emanationism, Islamic theology, and visionary mysticism. Suhrawardi's key innovation: reality unfolds through levels of light. The divine is pure light (nur); creation emanates downward through successively dimmer grades of illumination; each level manifests divine attributes. Crucially, knowledge of these levels is not rational inference but direct mystical intuition (ishraq—illumination, sudden gnosis). In Suhrawardi's system, the soul possesses the capacity for immediate perception of intelligible forms, celestial beings, and divine light. This is not imagination but genuine knowledge. Suhrawardi was executed in Aleppo in 1191, likely for heterodox teachings. Yet his influence proved immense: Illuminationism became the dominant philosophical framework for later Islamic thinkers. Ibn Arabi adopted Illuminationist language; Qunavi built on it; Safavid philosophers canonized it. For Ibn Turka, Suhrawardi's framework was essential: the doctrine that reality manifests through levels of light, that the soul can directly perceive intelligible realities, and that mystical intuition is genuine knowledge—all this authorized Ibn Turka's claim that letters are cosmic principles knowable through direct gnosis and operative through talismanic technique.
