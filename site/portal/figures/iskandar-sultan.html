Iskandar Sulṭān

Iskandar Sulṭān (1384–1415), Temür's grandson and ruler of Fars — Ibn Turka's first and most consequential patron. He commissioned Of Letters, and it was almost certainly at his instance that Ibn Turka began the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Book of Inquiries]]. His court was famous for astrological and occult patronage on a scale that set a model; Shāhrukh marched against him in 817/1414; he fled, was captured and blinded, and was executed afterwards — removing Ibn Turka's protection at a stroke.

Iskandar Sulṭān b. ʿUmar Shaykh (1384–1415) ruled Fars from Shiraz and Isfahan as one of the most lavishly bookish and most astrologically preoccupied of the Timurid princes. His patronage is directly responsible for Ibn Turka's two central lettrist works: he commissioned Of Letters, and Melvin-Koushki judges that Ibn Turka began the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] shortly afterward, again almost certainly at Iskandar's instance (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

The relationship matters beyond biography. Melvin-Koushki names the model established by Iskandar Sulṭān and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] as what ensured astronomy-astrology and lettrism in particular would go on being heavily patronised by later Timurid, Indo-Timurid, Safavid and Ottoman courts (Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists, 599). Iskandar is the opening term of the pattern that defines the [[isfahan-circle|occult court]].

He rebelled against [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]], who marched against him in Iraq in 817/1414; Iskandar fled, was captured and blinded, and was executed afterwards, [[baysunghur|his cousin]]'s brother Ibrāhīm Sulṭān being installed in his place (The Quest, chronology). An earlier version of this entry compressed that sequence into a single date of 1415; the defeat and blinding belong to 1414. For Ibn Turka the consequence was immediate and lasting: the patron who had commissioned his life's work became, retroactively, a dangerous association — the first of the reversals that would culminate in his exile from Herat in 1427.

What kind of patron he was. Iskandar is accounted the leading Timurid patron of culture in the early Shahrukhid period, with a particular interest in alchemy and in astrological and astronomical illustrated works, and he was known for his [[imamophilism|imamophilism]] and esotericist bent (The Quest, §3.1). He was not a generic prince who happened to employ an occultist; he was looking for one.

The hereditary judgeship. Iskandar was presumably responsible for installing Ibn Turka in his hereditary office as qāḍī of Isfahan — an office established to the Turka family by Tīmūr himself, on his appointment of Ibn Turka's elder brother and teacher [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] as qāḍī in Samarkand (The Quest, §3.1). The judgeship Ibn Turka held, and later lost, was a family possession granted at the founding of the dynasty.

The court that was built for someone like Ibn Turka

Melvin-Koushki's description of Iskandar's Isfahan is emphatic: by every measure a brilliant though short-lived court, and Iskandar himself accounted the leading Timurid patron of culture in the early Shahrukhid period, showing a particular interest in alchemy and astrological and astronomical illustrated works (The Quest, §3.1 and n.1).

Add the confessional fit — he was known for his imamophilism and esotericist bent, which is precisely the [[imamophilism|position]] the portal ascribes to Ibn Turka — and the relationship stops looking like a scholar finding a job. Iskandar was assembling exactly this kind of household, and Ibn Turka was exactly what it was for.

The first early modern absolutist sovereign

The strongest claim made about him has nothing to do with book patronage. Melvin-Koushki's study of the 815/1412 experiment involving Iskandar, Shāh Niʿmatullāh Valī and Sayyid Sharīf Jurjānī concludes: if the rise of absolutist politics is one of the benchmarks for early modernity, in 816/1412 [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] was the last medieval Islamic ruler, and Mīrzā Iskandar was the first early modern absolutist sovereign (Imperial Talismanic Love, 21).

The two uncles-and-nephew were running incompatible answers to the same problem — on what basis does a Timurid govern now that Tīmūr is dead. Shāhrukh's answer was juridical: abrogate the [[yasa-abrogated|yasa]], enforce the sharīʿa, rule as a Muslim sovereign. Iskandar's was eschatological, with occultists and philosophers on the staff to articulate a sacred role in unfolding history.

Ibn Turka's science is staff work for the second programme and has no comparable role in the first. He was on the losing side of a constitutional argument, and the tribunals of the 1420s are the aftermath.

The commissions

Two, and they are the two that matter. It is possible that Iskandar personally commissioned the [[risalat-huruf|R. Ḥurūf]], Ibn Turka's first lettrist treatise; and the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was begun shortly afterwards, again almost certainly at his instance. The two central lettrist works of the century have one patron behind them — and the summa was not finished until 823/1420, five years after that patron was dead.

What we do not know

  • Whether either commission is documented. Melvin-Koushki writes "it is possible that" and "almost certainly", which are judgements from context, not records.
  • What Ibn Turka's role was, if any, in the 815/1412 experiment; the sources place him at the court rather than in the episode.
  • Whether he was ever formally associated with the rebellion Shāhrukh crushed.
