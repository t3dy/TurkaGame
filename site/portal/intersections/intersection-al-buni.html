Aḥmad al-Būnī (d. c. 1225) and the talismanic-lettrist corpus

Ibn Turka and al-Būnī

The general context. Aḥmad al-Būnī is the name attached to the most influential body of operative lettrist writing in Arabic — above all the Shams al-maʿārif ("Sun of Knowledge"), a work that grew by accretion long after his death into the grimoire that would define what most people mean by Islamicate magic: divine names arranged in grids, awfāq (magic squares) computed for planetary hours, inscriptions to be worn, buried, dissolved or burned. Al-Būnī's corpus is practical. It tells you what to write, when to write it, and what will happen.

The intersection. Ibn Turka inherits al-Būnī's material and does something almost perverse with it: he keeps the letters and discards the recipe format.

Where al-Būnī's tradition supplies operations — do this at this hour with these letters — Ibn Turka asks the question the operations presuppose and never answer: why should letters do anything at all? His answer, worked out across the Investigations, is that the alphabet is not a set of magically potent signs but the mathematical structure of creation itself, so that a correctly constructed letter-figure is less like a spell than like a correct equation. This is why Melvin-Koushki insists the book is a scientific manifesto rather than a magical one, and why calling Ibn Turka "a magician" mistakes the genre.

The relationship runs both ways, though, and the game is careful about this. Ibn Turka does not repudiate operative practice; the five occult sciences — kīmiyā, līmiyā, hīmiyā, sīmiyā, rīmiyā — remain a real taxonomy of real crafts, and a wafq remains a thing you can actually make. What changes is the warrant. A Būnian practitioner works because the tradition attests the operation; a Turkian one works because the cosmology entails it.

The awkward inheritance. It is also al-Būnī's reputation, not Ibn Turka's philosophy, that supplies the prosecution's vocabulary at a tribunal. When the charge is framed around Qurʾan 2:102 and the angels at Babel who taught men magic, the thing being described is the grimoire tradition — and Ibn Turka must either distinguish himself from it (safe, and a betrayal of colleagues) or defend the whole field (honest, and dangerous). The Career Sim makes this the load-bearing choice of the first inquisition, precisely because the distinction is real, defensible, and morally uncomfortable.
