Courtly patronage of the occult sciences across the Persianate world

Ibn Turka and the Occult Court

The general context. In the post-Mongol Persianate world, occult science was not a fringe pursuit tolerated at the edges of respectable courts. It was, in Melvin-Koushki's formulation, close to the default imperial cosmology and political science — a body of technique that told rulers when to march, whom to marry, how their dynasty stood in the order of things, and why their authority was inscribed in the structure of the cosmos rather than merely won in battle. Timurid, Aqquyunlu, Safavid, Uzbek, Ottoman and Mughal courts all invested in it. Astrologers held salaried posts. Observatories were imperial projects.

The intersection. Ibn Turka's career is unintelligible without this, and badly distorted by the modern instinct to separate "his philosophy" from "his court life." The same man who wrote the most systematic metaphysics of the letter ever attempted also reportedly reckoned, from the letters of Shāh Rukh's name, that the Timurid state would endure the ninth Islamic century. Those are not two activities. The second is the first, applied.

This is why the Career Sim refuses to have separate "magic" and "politics" encounters. A prince asking what do letters teach about kingship is asking a metaphysical question with a budget attached; a commander asking for an auspicious date is commissioning applied mathematics with men's lives on it. The design rule follows Melvin-Koushki's line directly: the political was and is magical as a rule.

The trap inside the opportunity. Court demand is what makes the science fundable, visible, and consequential — and each of those is also a mechanism of destruction. A useful prognostication creates a dependent patron. A dependent patron creates envious rivals. Rivals engineer tribunals. The Career Sim models this as compounding Exposure, a meter that rises with success and essentially never falls, because that is the shape the biography actually has: he is destroyed not despite being useful but because of it.

A corrective to the romance. The occult court was also funny. The later manuals — Boon for the Khan, the Qasimian Secrets — catalogue disappearing inks, wake-rites, tricks to make a sleeping man talk, and effects whose only purpose is astonishment at a feast. Court culture ran on bazm u razm, feasting and fighting, and the philosopher was expected at both. A portal that presents this world as uniformly solemn misrepresents it.
