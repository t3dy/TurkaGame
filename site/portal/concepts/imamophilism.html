Shiʿi-Sunnism (imamophilism)

Shiʿi-Sunnism, or imamophilism — reverence for the Imams held without a formal Shiʿi confessional commitment. Melvin-Koushki calls it a defining feature of 9th/15th-century Islamic religiosity, particularly in Iran and particularly in esotericist circles, and it is his answer to the long argument about whether Ibn Turka was 'really' a Shiʿi or a Sunni. The question is anachronistic; the position is the period's own.

Neither, and deliberately

The problem it solves. Ibn Turka's confessional identity has been read both ways. Henry Corbin embraced him as a standard-issue Shiʿi esotericist; Leonard Lewisohn, reading the [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]], presents him as an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker. Melvin-Koushki rejects both: Ibn Turka's ambiguous confessional position is best described as Shiʿi-Sunnism or imamophilism (The Quest, 7).

Why it is not fence-sitting. The category is not a compromise between two real options but a description of what was normal. Deep reverence for the Imams as sources of esoteric knowledge, held without the juridical and political commitments of Twelver Shiʿism, was a defining feature of fifteenth-century Islamic religiosity — most of all in Iran, and most of all among esotericists. Reading the period through the hardened Safavid confessional boundary that came after it produces a question the sources are not answering.

How it shows up in the work. Ibn Turka identifies the greatest exponents of the perennial philosophy as the Imams and the ancients together with their disciples in every age, the ūlū l-aydī wa-l-abṣār. He reports that Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq greatly vaunted the science of letters and that those who cleave to the threshold of his [[walaya|walāya]] have written much on it. The Imams function as guarantors of knowledge, which is a different office from the one Twelver jurisprudence assigns them.

The historiographical payoff. Insisting on the label 'Shiʿi' or 'Sunni' for Ibn Turka does not sharpen the picture, it flattens it — and, as with the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd]], the flattening has consequences: a man sorted into a confessional box is easy to file as a mystical philosopher and hard to see as an [[occult-science-universal|occult philosopher]] with a systematic programme.

What the Imams are for

The distinguishing feature of the position is what office the Imams hold in it. In Twelver Shiʿism they are the legitimate political and juridical authority, and the consequences are legal and constitutional. In imamophilism they are guarantors of knowledge — the channel through which the occult sciences were transmitted and authenticated.

The evidence is in what Ibn Turka says about them. Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq greatly vaunted the science of letters, and those who cleave to the threshold of his [[walaya|walāya]] have penned numerous works on the subject. The Imams appear as the head of a bibliography.

The strongest instance is [[jafr|jafr]], letter divination, whose inventor is held to be ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib — and who, in this tradition, was hailed the chief mathematician of Islam. That epithet is imamophilism in one phrase: reverence for the first Imam expressed as a claim about his standing in a science.

Why the period produced it

Because the alternative had not yet hardened. The Safavid consolidation after 1501 made Persia confessionally Twelver and forced a choice that fifteenth-century scholars had not been obliged to make. Melvin-Koushki's point is that Shiʿi-Sunnism is a defining feature of 9th/15th-century Islamic religiosity, particularly in Iran and particularly in esotericist circles — the normal condition of the milieu, not a hedge within it.

[[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] himself was known for his imamophilism and esotericist bent, which is presumably part of why he and Ibn Turka suited each other. The patron and the philosopher occupied the same unnamed middle.

The damage the anachronism does

Sorting Ibn Turka into a confessional box is not a harmless simplification. Henry Corbin's warm embrace of him as a standard-issue Shiʿi esotericist and Leonard Lewisohn's reading of him as an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker are opposite errors with a common structure: each takes a post-Safavid category and applies it to a pre-Safavid man.

And each produces a figure who is easy to file and hard to see. The de facto consensus presents him as a mystical philosopher, effectively obscuring his explicitly occultist project — and a confessional label is one of the instruments by which that obscuring happens, because it answers the question of what kind of thinker he was before the question has been properly asked.
