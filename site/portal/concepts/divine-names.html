The Divine Names

Al-Asmaʾ al-Ilāhīyya (The Divine Names and Attributes). Islamic doctrine positing 28 (or 99) divine names—each expressing an aspect of God's nature (The Merciful, The Just, The Mighty, etc.). In [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]], each of the 28 Arabic letters corresponds to one divine name, making letters themselves expressions of divine attributes. Ibn Turka's system treats mastery of the divine names through their corresponding letters as the path to aligning human will with cosmic principles.

The Divine Names (Al-Asmaʾ al-Ilāhīyya)

Islamic theology recognizes that God is known through His attributes and names. The Qur'an references divine names, and Islamic scholars compiled lists—commonly 99, though sometimes 28. Each name expresses a dimension of divinity: al-Raḥmān (The Merciful), al-ʿAdl (The Just), al-ʿAlīm (The All-Knowing), al-Qawī (The Mighty), etc.

In [[ilm-al-huruf|Ibn Turka's lettrist system]], the 28 divine names are mapped directly onto the 28 Arabic letters. This creates a correspondence where:

  • Each letter IS a divine name
  • Each letter carries the properties and powers of that name
  • Manipulation of letters becomes invocation of divine principles

For example:

  • The letter ʿAyn corresponds to al-ʿAlīm (The All-Knowing)
  • The letter Tāʾ corresponds to al-Tāʾib (The Returning/Repenting)
  • The letter Qāf corresponds to al-Qawī (The Mighty)

Through understanding these correspondences and using [[abjad-numerology|abjad numerology]], a practitioner can compose words, phrases, or talismans that embody specific divine qualities. The goal is not manipulation (in the magical sense) but attunement: aligning human intention and action with divine attributes, thereby participating in the structure of reality itself.

Ibn Turka's treatment of the divine names through letters represents a synthesis of Islamic theology, [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist gnosis]], and [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean mathematics]]. It is the theological foundation that legitimizes lettrism as a valid science within Islamic orthodoxy—because engaging with letters is, from this perspective, engaging with divine attributes themselves.

The mapping, and the arithmetic behind it

The technical claim is a correspondence: the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet answer to twenty-eight divine names. The number is doing work. The canonical enumeration of the names is ninety-nine, and the reduction to twenty-eight is what makes a one-to-one mapping onto the alphabet possible at all — the correspondence is not found lying about, it is constructed, and the construction is part of the doctrine.

With the mapping in place, [[abjad-numerology|abjad values]] become theologically loaded. A word's numerical sum is no longer arithmetic about letters; it is a statement about which divine attributes that word gathers and in what proportion. This is the machinery [[talismanic-science|talismanic practice]] runs on.

Where the doctrine comes from

From [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]], systematised by [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] and elaborated by [[jandi|Jandī]]: the names are not descriptions of a God who exists apart from them but the structure through which being manifests. Creation is the self-disclosure of the names.

That metaphysical claim is what makes the lettrist move available. If creation unfolds through named attributes, and if the letters answer to the names, then the letters are constitutive of creation and not merely descriptive of it. The whole claim that lettrism is the [[occult-science-universal|only truly universal science]] rests on this chain, and each link is inherited except the last.

Why it keeps the science inside orthodoxy

The divine names are unimpeachably mainstream. They are Qur'anic, they are recited, they are the common property of every school. A science that presents itself as the disciplined study of the names is not importing foreign material; it is claiming to systematise something the tradition already holds.

This is the same defensive structure as the appeal to iʿjāz — the miraculous inimitability of the Qur'an — that [[ilm-al-huruf|the science claims to demonstrate]]. In both cases the occultist stands on a doctrine his prosecutors also hold.

What we do not know

  • The full correspondence table as Ibn Turka gives it is not reproduced in the sources in hand; it survives in the unedited lettrist works.
  • How he justified the reduction from ninety-nine to twenty-eight is not set out in this corpus.
  • Whether the mapping was his or inherited from [[saad-al-din-hamuyya|Ḥamūya]] and the earlier tradition is not resolved.

Light and dark, and what the mapping has to accommodate

The correspondence of names to letters is not a flat table, because the letters themselves are not uniform. They divide into light (nūrānī) and dark (ẓulmānī), the light ones being the fourteen isolated letters — the muqaṭṭaʿāt opening twenty-nine Sūras, exactly half the alphabet, and a primary matrix of lettrism (Being with a Capital B, 2).

Any mapping of names onto letters therefore inherits a two-tier structure it did not choose, and has to say what it means that some divine names answer to luminous letters and others to dark ones. The [[risala-baiyya|R. al-Bāʾiyya]] is Ibn Turka working exactly this problem at its sharpest point: B, first of the dark letters and fountainhead of all duality, opens both the Qur'an and the Torah, where A, avatar of divine Oneness, seems far better suited.

The answer he gives — combining rational and traditional arguments — is not preserved in the corpus in hand, but the shape of the problem tells us what the doctrine of the names must do. It cannot simply rank the names by dignity and match them to letters by rank, because scripture itself refuses that ordering.

Why the names keep the science inside orthodoxy

The names are Qur'anic, recited, and the shared property of every school. A science presenting itself as their disciplined study is not importing foreign material; it is systematising what the tradition already holds. That defensive structure is the same one at work in the appeal to iʿjāz, the miraculous inimitability of the Qur'an, which [[ilm-al-huruf|the science claims to demonstrate]] rather than to assume.

In both cases the occultist stands on a doctrine his prosecutors also hold — which is what made the [[first-inquisition|first tribunal]] winnable, and what makes the whole project something other than the smuggling operation its critics took it for.
