'Ilm al-ḥurūf

'Ilm al-ḥurūf (the science of letters). Islamic science investigating the divine and cosmological meanings hidden in the forms, sounds, and numerical values of Arabic letters. The doctrine that Creation is fundamentally linguistic—that God speaks the world into being through the Arabic alphabet—and that mastery of letter-properties (form, sound, numerical value, and correspondence to divine names and cosmic principles) confers access to hidden causal powers. Central to Ibn Turka's entire philosophical system and foundational to late medieval and early modern Islamic occultism.

'Ilm al-ḥurūf: The Science of Letters

Definition and Etymology

The term ʿilm al-ḥurūf literally means "the science of letters." In Islamic intellectual tradition, it denotes a body of knowledge and practice investigating the divine, mystical, and cosmological meanings encoded in the letters of the Arabic alphabet. Unlike grammar (ʿilm al-naḥw) or rhetoric (ʿilm al-bayān), which treat letters as vehicles for communication, lettrism treats letters as primary entities carrying inherent causal and spiritual power.

The philosophical foundation of lettrism rests on a radical claim: Creation is fundamentally linguistic. God's first act was not merely to think or to will, but to speak. The Qur'an expresses this principle in the doctrine that God says kun ("Be!") and creation manifests. But if Creation itself is the result of divine speech, then the properties of language—especially the formal, numerical, and phonetic properties of the Arabic letters through which that speech is expressed—must encode the principles of reality itself.

Historical Development

Lettrism has deep roots in Islamic intellectual history. Early mystical traditions, particularly within Sufism, occasionally invoked letter-symbolism. But lettrism as a systematic science—with formal philosophical grounding and operative methods—emerged gradually in the medieval period.

The key figure in establishing intellectual lettrism was Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240), the Andalusian Sufi metaphysician. In his vast encyclopedic work the Futūḥāt al-Makkīyya (Meccan Illuminations), Ibn ʿArabī developed sophisticated theories of letters as expressions of divine names and cosmological principles. His approach was intuitive and poetic—he scattered lettrist insights throughout his work without systematizing them (what Melvin-Koushki calls the principle of tabdīd al-ʿilm, "scattering knowledge").

Saʿd al-Dīn Ḥamūya (d. 1252), Ibn ʿArabī's Iranian contemporary, took an even more radically lettrist approach, arguing that letters were the foundation of all metaphysics. Indeed, according to sources, Ḥamūya was so committed to lettrist principles that he criticized Ibn ʿArabī for being insufficiently rigorous in his application of lettrist logic.

Yet despite these precedents, lettrism remained scattered and esoteric through the medieval period. The crucial breakthrough came in the late 14th and early 15th centuries with Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī and his heir [[ibn-turka]].

Ibn Turka's Systematization

Ibn Turka's contribution was transforming lettrism from an intuitive, esoteric teaching into a rigorous, scientifically-grounded philosophy. He accomplished this through three moves:

1. Explicit Pythagorean Modeling. Ibn Turka grounded lettrism in Pythagorean mathematical philosophy—the ancient Greek doctrine that numbers and their ratios are the fundamental principles of reality. By arguing that Arabic letters and their numerical values (abjad system) encode cosmic principles in a Pythagorean manner, Ibn Turka gave lettrism mathematical legitimacy.

2. Integration with Formal Philosophy. Rather than treating lettrism as a mystical secret, Ibn Turka integrated it with Islamic peripatetic philosophy (falsafa), Neoplatonic metaphysics, and rational theology. His treatises are technically sophisticated, employing the logical tools and conceptual apparatus of mainstream Islamic philosophy. This move made lettrism a legitimate intellectual discipline, not marginal mysticism.

3. Codification and Transparency. Ibn Turka wrote extensively and clearly on lettrism, replacing the principle of "scattering knowledge" (Achlāṭī's approach, inherited from Ibn ʿArabī) with systematic exposition. His magisterial Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ (Book of Inquiries) lays out lettrist theory in organized chapters, with precise definitions, examples, and arguments. This transparency—making esoteric knowledge available to educated readers—was controversial but effective. It allowed his framework to propagate widely.

Core Doctrines

The Letter-Divine Name Correspondence. The 28 letters of the Arabic alphabet correspond to the 28 divine names recognized in Islamic theology. Each name has properties—creative power, protection, vengeance, mercy—that are encoded in the corresponding letter.

The Numerical Dimension. In the abjad system, each letter has a numerical value (alif = 1, bāʾ = 2, etc.). These values can be summed, multiplied, and manipulated to yield cosmic and operative results. A Qur'anic verse, understood as a sequence of letters with specific numerical values, encodes mathematical relationships reflecting cosmological structures.

The Cosmological Scale. Ibn Turka systematized a correspondence between:

  • The 28 letters
  • The 28 divine names
  • The 28 lunar mansions (in astrology)
  • 28 (or seven times four) levels of celestial and sublunary reality
  • 28 human faculties and attributes

This integrated system meant that knowledge of letters provided simultaneous access to theology, metaphysics, psychology, and cosmology.

Operative Power. Unlike purely theoretical systems, lettrism claims operative efficacy. Operations that recombine letters—anagrams (taqārīb al-ḥurūf), numerical rearrangements, geometric configurations—are not mere games but interventions in the fabric of reality. Because letters are the fundamental building blocks of creation, manipulating them (through knowledge, intention, and proper ritual) alters reality itself.

Distinctions: Intellectual vs. Mystical Lettrism

It is crucial to distinguish intellectual lettrism (as developed by Ibn Turka) from earlier mystical and gnostic lettrism.

Gnostic-Messianic Lettrism (exemplified by the Ḥurūfī movement of Fażl Allāh Astarābādī, d. 1394) treated letters as paths to ecstatic experience, mystical insight, and divine union. It often had popular appeal and sometimes messianic dimensions—the belief that lettrist knowledge would usher in a new age of humanity. This tradition valued direct mystical experience over systematic philosophical reasoning.

Intellectual Lettrism (as developed by Ibn Turka and the Isfahan Circle) treats letters as subjects of rigorous philosophical investigation. While mystical experience might accompany such knowledge, it is not the goal. Rather, lettrism is valued as the truest form of universal science—more comprehensive than logic or mathematics alone because it encompasses both rational and mystical dimensions, integrating them into a coherent system. The operative power of letters is explained through metaphysical principles, not through mystical intuition.

Melvin-Koushki emphasizes that Ibn Turka's intellectual approach did NOT negate the gnostic tradition; rather, it coexisted with it. The vigorous Ḥurūfī movement in 15th-century Iran testifies to the religio-cultural power of gnostic lettrism at all social levels. Ibn Turka's innovation was providing a philosophical framework that made lettrism intellectually respectable among the scholarly elite.

Later Development and Influence

Ibn Turka's framework became canonical. By the 16th century, virtually every Islamic philosopher engaging seriously with lettrism worked through Ibn Turka's categories. The tradition continued through Safavid Iran (with thinkers like Mullā Ṣadrā), Ottoman Anatolia, and Mughal India. Lettrism remained a living intellectual tradition until the 19th century.

In modern times, the dismissal of lettrism as superstition was part of orientalist ideology. Contemporary scholarship has recognized lettrism for what it was: a legitimate intellectual system with internal coherence, grounded in rigorous reasoning, and integrated with the best scholarship of its era. It was not proto-science awaiting correction by modern physics, but rather a distinct epistemic system with different starting assumptions.

The correspondence table, and what it is for

The technical core of the science is a mapping: each of the twenty-eight Arabic letters answers to one of the [[divine-names|divine names]], and so carries that name's properties. Working with a letter is therefore working with a divine attribute, and this is what makes the practice more than [[abjad-numerology|arithmetic]] — the numbers are the handle, the names are what is being handled.

Ibn Turka's own analysis in the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] is fourfold rather than simply tabular. He treats the letters in their three forms — numerological (iḥṣāʾī), symbological (kitābī) and phonological (kalāmī) — and then, separately, as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā). The first three are the letter as counted, written and spoken. The fourth is the letter as a being, prior to any of those. A correspondence table alone would give only the first three registers; the fourth is what turns the table into an ontology.

The three strands, and why the distinction is the whole argument

Lettrism in this period is not a single discipline, and almost every misreading of Ibn Turka comes from treating it as one. Melvin-Koushki separates:

1. Gnostic-messianic lettrism — the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfiyya]], for whom the letters announce a new dispensation. 2. Sufi lettrism — the devotional and contemplative strand descending through the [[ibn-arabi|Akbarian]] tradition and the orders. 3. Intellectual lettrism — Ibn Turka's, in which the letters are the object of a rigorous universal science grounded in [[pythagorean-cosmology|mathematics]].

His unprecedented lionization of lettrism vis-à-vis philosophy constitutes the third strand as a distinct thing (The Quest, iii). And the historiographical correction that follows is sharp: it is typically and erroneously assumed that the Ḥurūfī movement defines later Islamicate lettrism. It does not. The Ḥurūfīs are popular gnostic-messianic-Sufi lettrism, and their vigour testifies to how deeply the material had penetrated Iranian culture at all levels — which is evidence about circulation, not about definition.

The claim to universality, stated maximally

The argument against philosophy is not that philosophy is wrong but that it is parochial while believing itself universal. In the dissertation's formulation: the faux-universal concepts of philosophical speculation notwithstanding, only the letter encompasses all that is and is not, all that can and cannot be; it alone is the coincidentia oppositorum; hence lettrism is the only truly universal science (The Quest, iii).

The reasoning runs: a philosophical concept ranges over actual and possible being. The letter ranges over actuality, possibility, and their negations — it can signify non-being and impossibility as readily as being. A science whose object has that range is the only candidate for a science of everything. Whether one accepts the argument, it is an argument, and it is aimed precisely at the discipline that held the prestige.

What the practitioners took themselves to be proving

Two goals, held together. Lettrists of Ibn Turka's kind saw themselves as advancing human knowledge of the cosmos and simultaneously demonstrating the miraculous inimitability of the Qur'an (iʿjāz) as the clearest transcript of divine Speech in history (The Quest, iii).

The second is the theologically conservative half, and it is why the science reads as piety from inside. Iʿjāz is mainstream doctrine. If the letters of scripture are the constitutive principles of creation, the Qur'an's inimitability stops being a literary verdict and becomes a cosmological one. The science is offered as the proof of an orthodox commitment.

This is also why the whole edifice necessarily presupposes the uncreatedness of the Qur'an. Letters that were created artefacts could not be the principles through which creation is constituted. Ibn Turka accordingly censures the popularity of Zamakhsharī's Kashshāf, singling out its failure to recognise the intrinsic ontological majesty of the quranic letters (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66 n.88).

Reading and writing the world

The operative dimension is best put as Melvin-Koushki puts it: these are thinkers driven by the prospect of decoding and recoding the twin Books, the Qur'an and the cosmos (The Quest, iii).

Decoding makes lettrism a hermeneutic — creation is legible. Recoding makes it a technology — a reader fluent in the notation can compose in it. The [[barzakh|intermediate realm]] is where the writing takes effect, and [[talismanic-science|talismanic practice]] is the recoding carried out on matter. Everything operative in the tradition follows from the second verb.

Style as doctrine

Because letters are entities rather than marks, their arrangement is not presentation. The dissertation argues that lettrist theory informs the ornate literary practice of the period, and notes Ibn Turka's own standing as a vaunted stylist of Persian prose (The Quest, iii). The prosimetric elaboration of his writing is the doctrine applied to the page.

What we do not know

  • The relative chronology of the three strands is not settled; they overlap and cross-fertilise rather than succeeding one another cleanly.
  • Most of Ibn Turka's specifically lettrist works are still unedited, so the technical system is known chiefly through the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] and the treatises edited in Part 2 of the dissertation.
  • How far the Ottoman branch under [[bistami|Bisṭāmī]] developed the same technical apparatus is a question for Fleischer's research rather than this portal's sources.

The letters are not all alike

A technical division the portal has so far left out, and it is fundamental. The Arabic letters sort into light (nūrānī) and dark (ẓulmānī).

The light letters are the fourteen isolated letters — the muqaṭṭaʿāt, the detached letters that open twenty-nine Sūras of the Qur'an, whose meaning is one of the oldest unresolved questions in Qur'anic exegesis. They are exactly half the alphabet, and Melvin-Koushki calls them naturally a primary matrix of lettrism (Being with a Capital B, 2).

That numerical fact is doing a great deal of work. A tradition claiming that scripture encodes the structure of creation is handed, in the muqaṭṭaʿāt, a set of letters that scripture itself sets apart for no stated reason — and which happens to be precisely half the alphabet. It is the strongest single piece of internal evidence the science has.

B, by contrast, is the first of the dark letters and the fountainhead of all duality — which generates the conundrum the [[risala-baiyya|R. al-Bāʾiyya]] is written to solve: why do the Qur'an and the Torah both open on it, rather than on A, the avatar of divine Oneness and of the beginning of creation?

Jazz, and the theory of jazz

The best short account of Ibn Turka's originality is Melvin-Koushki's. Both [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] and [[saad-al-din-hamuyya|Ḥamūya]] can be usefully read as jazz philosophers; Ibn Turka, their systematizing and Pythagoreanizing lettrist heir, is a philosopher of jazz (Being with a Capital B, 10).

His predecessors improvised on the letters — Ḥamūya's manner is described as radically free-playing, riffing on the visual and the aural at once. Ibn Turka wrote the theory of the improvisation: what the changes are, why they work, what makes a run of them correct rather than merely striking. That is a different activity from playing, and it is the one nobody had done.
