Ḥurūfism

Ḥurūfism — the messianic letter-movement founded by Fażl Allāh Astarābādī (d. 796/1394), which read the letters as announcing a new dispensation. It is the heresy Ibn Turka had to be distinguished from, and the charge on which he was exiled from Herat in 1427. The distinction is not technique but motive: the Ḥurūfīs proclaimed a new revelation; the lettrists claimed to be working within the tradition to extend human knowledge.

Ḥurūfism, and the line Ibn Turka had to draw

The movement. Fażl Allāh Astarābādī (d. 796/1394) taught that the letters disclose a new phase of revelation, and his followers — the Ḥurūfiyya — carried that claim into open messianism across Iran and Anatolia. Both they and Ibn Turka worked on letters. That shared subject matter is precisely the problem.

The distinction is motive, not method. Melvin-Koushki puts the difference plainly: what separates the lettrists from the Ḥurūfīs and the other messianic movements of early 9th/15th-century Iran is motive — the gnostic Hurufis proclaim a new, Persian dispensation; the messianic Sufis promote themselves as political saviors; the lettrists present themselves as occult philosophers working within the tradition to expand the boundaries of human knowledge (The Quest, 456).

Ibn Turka is himself, by any standard, a profoundly millenarian thinker: he proclaims the advent of a new age, heralded by heavenly portent, in which the occult knowledge of the Imams and the ancients will be made manifest. What keeps this inside orthodoxy is its insistence on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad (The Quest, 456). A new age of knowledge, not a new prophecy. The whole of his position rests on that distinction, and it is a fine one.

Why the charge stuck to him. Because it was not baseless. Fażl Allāh arrived in Isfahan around 766/1365 and quickly became famous as a dream interpreter, and Ḥurūfī sources record that Ibn Turka's own father Afżal al-Dīn and his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] sought him out there to have their dreams interpreted. Naṣr Allāh Nāfajī's Khwābnāma preserves the episode (The Quest, ch. 1, and Eichner, 'Afḍal al-Dīn Turka', EI3).

Melvin-Koushki is careful about what this does and does not show: the contact does not imply Ḥurūfī inclinations on the part of the two Turkas, and the hagiographical point of the story is to establish Fażl Allāh's fame as a Sufi before his messianic transformation — respected scholars of the religious sciences came to him for counsel. But a family documented as having consulted the founder of the Ḥurūfiyya gives a prosecutor something to work with, and in 1427 a prosecutor used it.

The cost. [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court exiled Ibn Turka from Herat alongside [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], the official excuse being a putative Ḥurūfī connection (The Quest, 456 n.16). The science and the heresy were close enough in appearance that the difference had to be argued, and arguing it was a lifetime's work.

Where it sits among the three lettrisms

The clearest way to hold the distinction is to see that lettrism in this period runs in three strands, and that Ḥurūfism is one of them rather than the whole. Melvin-Koushki separates gnostic-messianic, Sufi, and intellectual lettrism, and argues that Ibn Turka's unprecedented elevation of lettrism against philosophy constitutes the third as a distinct thing (The Quest, iii).

He then corrects a standing assumption in the field, and the correction is sharp: it is typically — and erroneously — assumed that the Ḥurūfī movement of Fażl Allāh Astarābādī defines later Islamicate lettrism. It does not. Ḥurūfism is an instance of popular gnostic-messianic-Sufi lettrism, and what its vigour actually demonstrates is the religio-cultural valency of lettrism in Iran at all levels of society.

That last clause is worth pausing on, because it cuts both ways. The Ḥurūfī movement is evidence that letter-speculation had penetrated Iranian culture deeply enough to sustain a mass movement — which is context Ibn Turka benefits from and is endangered by in equal measure. A science whose vocabulary is in general circulation is a science whose practitioners can be mistaken for anybody using that vocabulary.

The millenarianism problem, stated honestly

The entry would be dishonest if it presented Ibn Turka as a sober rationalist accused by mistake. Melvin-Koushki does not: Ṣāʾin al-Dīn is a profoundly millenarian thinker by any standard (The Quest, 456). He proclaims the advent of a new age heralded by heavenly portent — the [[chronology|Saturn–Jupiter conjunction of 767/1365]] — in which the occult knowledge of the Imams and the ancients will be made manifest.

The difference from Ḥurūfism is therefore not the presence or absence of millenarian expectation. It is what the expected age contains. Ibn Turka's is unimpeachably Islamic in its insistence on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad: a golden age of knowledge, in which what was already revealed becomes fully actual. The Ḥurūfīs proclaim a new, Persian dispensation. One is an epistemological claim about timing; the other is a prophetic claim.

Three movements, three motives

The dissertation's formulation sorts the field by motive rather than doctrine, which is what makes it usable:

  • the gnostic Ḥurūfīs proclaim a new, Persian dispensation;
  • the messianic Sufis promote themselves as political saviours;
  • the lettrists present themselves as occult philosophers working within the tradition to expand the boundaries of human knowledge (The Quest, 456).

Doctrinally these overlap heavily. By motive they are three different enterprises, and a tribunal that cannot or will not distinguish motives will convict all three together.

The political weather that made the charge lethal

Fażl Allāh was executed at the fortress of Alinjaq by Mīrānshāh b. Tīmūr in 796/1394 — the dynasty had killed the founder within Ibn Turka's own lifetime. [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] attempted a purge of Ismāʿīlīs in Quhistan in 1415. And in 835/1432, the year Ibn Turka died, there were Ḥurūfī uprisings in Isfahan and Tabriz. The label was not an abstract theological category; it named an active insurgency that the state was killing people over.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka ever addressed the Ḥurūfīs directly in a surviving text, and in what terms, is not established in the sources in hand.
  • What his prosecutors actually alleged in 1427 beyond the bare "putative connection" is not recorded.
  • How the family's consultation of Fażl Allāh became known to his opponents — whether through the Ḥurūfī sources that preserve it or through local memory — is unclear.
