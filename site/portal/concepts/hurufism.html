Ḥurūfism

Ḥurūfism — the messianic letter-movement founded by Fażl Allāh Astarābādī (d. 796/1394), which read the letters as announcing a new dispensation. It is the heresy Ibn Turka had to be distinguished from, and the charge on which he was exiled from Herat in 1427. The distinction is not technique but motive: the Ḥurūfīs proclaimed a new revelation; the lettrists claimed to be working within the tradition to extend human knowledge.

Ḥurūfism, and the line Ibn Turka had to draw

The movement. Fażl Allāh Astarābādī (d. 796/1394) taught that the letters disclose a new phase of revelation, and his followers — the Ḥurūfiyya — carried that claim into open messianism across Iran and Anatolia. Both they and Ibn Turka worked on letters. That shared subject matter is precisely the problem.

The distinction is motive, not method. Melvin-Koushki puts the difference plainly: what separates the lettrists from the Ḥurūfīs and the other messianic movements of early 9th/15th-century Iran is motive — the gnostic Hurufis proclaim a new, Persian dispensation; the messianic Sufis promote themselves as political saviors; the lettrists present themselves as occult philosophers working within the tradition to expand the boundaries of human knowledge (The Quest, 456).

Ibn Turka is himself, by any standard, a profoundly millenarian thinker: he proclaims the advent of a new age, heralded by heavenly portent, in which the occult knowledge of the Imams and the ancients will be made manifest. What keeps this inside orthodoxy is its insistence on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad (The Quest, 456). A new age of knowledge, not a new prophecy. The whole of his position rests on that distinction, and it is a fine one.

Why the charge stuck to him. Because it was not baseless. Fażl Allāh arrived in Isfahan around 766/1365 and quickly became famous as a dream interpreter, and Ḥurūfī sources record that Ibn Turka's own father Afżal al-Dīn and his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] sought him out there to have their dreams interpreted. Naṣr Allāh Nāfajī's Khwābnāma preserves the episode (The Quest, ch. 1, and Eichner, 'Afḍal al-Dīn Turka', EI3).

Melvin-Koushki is careful about what this does and does not show: the contact does not imply Ḥurūfī inclinations on the part of the two Turkas, and the hagiographical point of the story is to establish Fażl Allāh's fame as a Sufi before his messianic transformation — respected scholars of the religious sciences came to him for counsel. But a family documented as having consulted the founder of the Ḥurūfiyya gives a prosecutor something to work with, and in 1427 a prosecutor used it.

The cost. [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court exiled Ibn Turka from Herat alongside [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], the official excuse being a putative Ḥurūfī connection (The Quest, 456 n.16). The science and the heresy were close enough in appearance that the difference had to be argued, and arguing it was a lifetime's work.
