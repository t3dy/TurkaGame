Abjad numerology

Abjad numerology (Hisab al-Jumul). The system assigning numerical values to Arabic letters (alif=1, bāʾ=2, etc.), allowing letters and words to be manipulated mathematically. Central to [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]]: Ibn Turka used abjad values to establish correspondences between divine names, cosmic principles, and human faculties. Enables the calculation of ''operative words'' whose numerical sum aligns with specific celestial or talismanic purposes.

Abjad Numerology (Hisab al-Jumul)

The abjad system is an ancient method of letter-to-number correspondence in Arabic and Hebrew. Each of the 28 Arabic letters carries a numerical value:

  • alif (a) = 1, bāʾ (b) = 2, jīm (j) = 3... up to ghīn (gh) = 1000

This allowed Arabic letters, words, and verses to be treated as mathematical entities. The sum of a word's letters yields a number; that number can be matched against cosmic cycles, divine names, or talismanic intent.

In Ibn Turka's system, abjad numerology becomes the bridge between language and mathematics, between expression and causation. By selecting or composing words whose abjad sum equals a specific number—say, the number corresponding to the planet Venus or to a particular divine name—one creates a linguistic-mathematical object that resonates with that principle. Written or chanted correctly, such a word becomes operative.

Example: the divine name al-ʿAlīm ("the All-Knowing") has a specific abjad value. A talisman inscribed with a word or phrase whose abjad value matches that of al-ʿAlīm would, in theory, embody the properties of divine knowledge and could be used to attract or enhance that quality.

Ibn Turka's genius was systematizing abjad manipulation into a rigorous science rather than leaving it as scattered numerological tricks. He showed how abjad calculations, combined with proper understanding of the barzakh and divine names, provide a methodology for intentional causation grounded in cosmic principles.

The system itself

The twenty-eight letters carry fixed values in a scheme inherited from the Semitic alphabetic order rather than the alphabetical order used for dictionaries. Units run alif = 1 through ṭāʾ = 9; tens yāʾ = 10 through ṣād = 90; hundreds qāf = 100 through ẓāʾ = 900; and ghayn closes the series at 1000. The mnemonic abjad hawwaz ḥuṭṭī… preserves that order, and gives the system its name.

Two properties make it useful for what Ibn Turka wants. It is total — every letter has a value, so every word has a sum, with no remainder and no exceptions. And it is bidirectional — one can compute the value of a given word, or search for words of a given value. The second is what turns a notation into a technique.

What it is doing in this system, and what it is not

Abjad by itself is old, widely known, and not in any interesting sense occult; it is used for chronograms, for numbering, for dating buildings. Encountering it in a poem does not imply a cosmology.

What Ibn Turka adds is the claim that the values are not conventional. If letters correspond to [[divine-names|divine names]] and the names are the structure of manifestation, then a letter's number is a fact about the order of being rather than an agreed tag. On that reading two words sharing a sum share something real, and the correspondence can be worked.

This is precisely where the [[hurufism|distinction between the three lettrisms]] bites. Popular practice uses abjad as a source of resonances and portents. Intellectual lettrism claims the values are ontologically grounded and reasons from them systematically. Same arithmetic, incompatible claims about what it is arithmetic about.

Its place in the fourfold analysis

In the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] abjad is one register of four: the letter as counted (iḥṣāʾī), alongside the letter as written (kitābī) and as spoken (kalāmī), and then, separately, the letter as it is in itself (fī anfusi-hā). Numerology is therefore a third of the sensible analysis and a quarter of the whole — a corrective worth stating, since lettrism is often described as though it were numerology and nothing else.

What we do not know

  • Which variant conventions Ibn Turka used — eastern and western abjad differ on several values — is not specified in the sources in hand.
  • His worked examples survive chiefly in the unedited lettrist treatises.

Where the arithmetic stops and the science begins

The distinction this entry most needs to hold is between two things that look identical on the page.

Abjad as convention is old, ubiquitous and uncontroversial: chronograms, dating inscriptions, numbering sections. Encountering it in a poem implies nothing about the poet's metaphysics.

Abjad as ontology is Ibn Turka's claim: that the values are facts about the order of being rather than agreed tags. On that reading two words sharing a sum share something real, and the correspondence can be worked. The arithmetic is unchanged; what changes is what the arithmetic is about.

This is exactly where the [[hurufism|three lettrisms]] separate. Popular practice uses abjad for resonances and portents. Intellectual lettrism claims the values are grounded and reasons from them systematically. A tribunal looking at two practitioners cannot tell them apart by watching what they compute.

The corrective the portal needs to keep making

Lettrism is routinely described as though it were numerology and nothing else. In the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] the numerological register (iḥṣāʾī) is one of four — alongside the symbological (kitābī) and phonological (kalāmī), and then the letters as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā).

So abjad is a third of the sensible analysis and a quarter of the whole. A description of the science that stops at number has taken the most legible register for the discipline.

The uneven alphabet

Abjad also has to reckon with the fact that the letters are not equivalent. They divide into light and dark, with the fourteen muqaṭṭaʿāt forming exactly half the alphabet and serving as the primary matrix. Numerical operations run across a set whose members already differ in rank before any sum is taken.
