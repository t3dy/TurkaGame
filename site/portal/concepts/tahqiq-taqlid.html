Taḥqīq and Taqlīd

Taḥqīq — verification, working a question through to its ground for oneself — against taqlīd, following an authority. The axis on which Islamicate intellectual life sorted itself, and the one Melvin-Koushki uses to compare it with the European Renaissance. His finding runs against the usual story: taḥqīq was a province dominated by Arabic, and European humanism's strict Grecism was a reaction to Arabism's success, not evidence of its absence.

The pair

Taqlīd is acceptance on authority: the position is held because a recognised master held it. Taḥqīq is verification: the question is worked through to its grounds, and the conclusion held because it has been established. Every discipline in the tradition has a view about which is appropriate where, and the pair functions as the standing axis of intellectual self-description.

It is not a straightforward good-and-bad. Taqlīd in law is a virtue — it is what makes a legal system predictable — and a jurist who verified everything from first principles would be a menace. The argument is always about scope.

Where Ibn Turka stands

On the side of verification, aggressively. The whole claim that [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] is the [[occult-science-universal|only truly universal science]] is a taḥqīq claim: it says the matter has been worked through and philosophy has been found wanting on its own ground. His invidious comparison with philosophy is not a preference between schools but an assertion that one of them has verified something the other has not.

The same commitment shows in the treatment of scripture. He censures the popularity of Zamakhsharī's Kashshāf for failing to recognise the intrinsic ontological majesty of the quranic letters — an attack on the standard exegesis of his century for not having gone far enough into its own subject.

And it shows in the family. Where his brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] taught only the outward sciences because he deemed it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public, the younger brother delved. Same material, opposite judgement about how far verification should be pushed in the open.

The comparative claim, which is the striking part

Melvin-Koushki uses the pair to compare the Islamicate and European renaissances, and the result inverts a familiar narrative.

European humanists, committed to a purely European prisca sapientia, pushed for an extreme taqlīd of Greek sources to the exclusion of Arabic — but they did this in reaction to the unprecedented success of Arabism in European scholarly circles, whence the boom in printing Latin editions of Arabic texts in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries (Taḥqīq vs. Taqlīd, 195).

And the purificationists could not hold the line: even these ideological purificationists could not avoid the scientific seductions of taḥqīq, a province dominated by Arabic, which their strict Grecism was far too narrow and too impractical to sustain. Scholarly compromises abounded. Arabism thus remained essential to Renaissance scientific-philosophical inquiry (ibid.).

Two consequences follow, and both matter for how this portal frames its subject. European humanism's turn to the Greeks was a defensive move against a live rival, not the natural recovery of an obvious inheritance. And on the axis that mattered for getting science done — verification — the Arabic tradition held the advantage, which is why the humanists' own programme kept leaking.

Why this belongs in a portal about Ibn Turka

Because it supplies the comparative frame the intersection essays work in. When the portal sets Isfahan 1420 against Cusa 1440 and Pico 1486 without asserting contact, this is the ground of the comparison: two renaissances arguing about the same epistemological pair, in which the Islamicate side is not the belated one.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka uses the terms as a formal pair in the unedited works, or whether the framing is Melvin-Koushki's analytical vocabulary applied to him.
  • How far the Arabism thesis is accepted by historians of the European Renaissance; it is a strong claim made in a comparative venue.
