Occult Science as Universal System

Occult Science as Universal System. The core historiographical claim that Ibn Turka and the Isfahan Circle systematized Islamic lettrism, talismanism, and numerology into a comprehensive philosophy claiming universal validity—a science encompassing metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, cosmology, and operative practice. Not marginal superstition but legitimate intellectual achievement rivaling any philosophical school. Melvin-Koushki's central argument.

historiographyuniversal-scienceoccultismphilosophy

The claim that Islamic occultism constitutes a genuine universal science—this is the animating thesis of Melvin-Koushki's entire body of work and the central historiographical claim of Ibn Turka's own project. What did occultism offer that rational philosophy did not? (1) Integration of knowledge and practice: peripatetic philosophy described the cosmos; occult science engaged it operatively. (2) Rigor grounded in systematic principle: not folk magic or superstition but coherent system derived from fundamental doctrines (divine names, letters, Pythagorean numbers). (3) Universality: not provincial tradition but claim to encompass all knowledge—metaphysics via cosmology, epistemology via gnosis, ethics via attunement to cosmic principles. (4) Legitimacy within orthodoxy: by grounding lettrist science in Ibn Arabi's metaphysics and Pythagorean philosophy, Ibn Turka positioned it not as heresy but as sophisticated development within Islamic orthodoxy. The claim to universality is not arrogance but serious metaphysical commitment: if letters are cosmic principles and divine names are ontological foundations, then knowledge of letters and names is knowledge of reality itself—hence universal. This positioning of occult science as universal system represents a major historiographical revaluation: instead of viewing Islamic philosophy as decline into mysticism and formalism, we see a tradition of genuine innovation and intellectual achievement.

What 'universal' is actually claiming

The word is doing precise work and is worth unpacking, because it is the claim on which everything else rests.

A science is universal, in Ibn Turka's sense, if its object is the principle by which all things are constituted. His argument is that letters and [[divine-names|divine names]] are exactly that: creation unfolds through them, so knowledge of them is knowledge of creation as such, and not of one region of it. This is why he is not content to place [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] among the sciences — a science of everything cannot be one item in a list.

The claim is also what distinguishes his project from what preceded it. Occult practice before him could be defended as useful, or as permitted, or as inherited. Ibn Turka defends it as systematic: derived from stated principles, answerable to [[pythagorean-cosmology|mathematics]], and therefore assessable as true or false rather than merely effective or ineffective. That is the difference between a body of technique and a philosophy, and it is the difference he spent the Mafāḥiṣ establishing.

It should be said plainly that the claim is very strong and was not conceded. The [[neoplatonism|anti-occultist coalition]] — Ibn Sīnā, [[al-ghazali|Ghazālī]], Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Khaldūn — denied that these were sciences at all, and Ibn Turka's move into the mathematical sciences is an answer to them rather than a victory over them.

The verification claim underneath it

The universality claim is, in the period's own vocabulary, a [[tahqiq-taqlid|taḥqīq]] claim rather than a preference between schools. Ibn Turka is not saying that lettrism suits him better than philosophy; he is saying the matter has been worked through to its ground and philosophy has been found wanting on terrain it claimed as its own.

That is why the argument is conducted against [[peripatetic-philosophy|the peripatetics]] rather than against sceptics. A discipline that merely wanted to be tolerated would argue with its detractors. A discipline claiming to have verified something argues with whoever currently holds the position it wants.

The two audiences, and why both were needed

The portal's [[walaya|walāya]] entry sets out the double move — mathematicalization to answer the philosophers, sanctification to answer the court — and the universality claim sits on top of both. Neither alone would carry it.

Mathematics establishes that the science has a subject, against the charge that it deals in unverifiable sympathies. Sanctity establishes who may practise it, which is what turns a demonstrated science into a fundable one. Universality is the claim the two together license: a science whose object is the principles by which all things are constituted, and whose practitioners stand close enough to God to work them.

What it cost to claim it

A maximal claim invites a maximal response, and the [[hurufism|three tribunals]] are what a maximal response looks like. It is worth registering that Ibn Turka could have had a quiet career: the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] shows he was entirely capable of respectable scholastic metaphysics, and it is the book that made his modern reputation. The universality claim is the thing he did not have to make.
