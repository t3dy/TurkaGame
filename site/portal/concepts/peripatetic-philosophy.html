Peripatetic Philosophy

Al-Falsafa al-Mashaʾiya (Peripatetic Philosophy). The Aristotelian philosophical tradition as transmitted and developed in Islamic world. Emphasizes rational inquiry, logical syllogism, study of natural world, metaphysics grounded in being and causation. Ibn Turka synthesized peripatetic logic and metaphysics with [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] and [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean mathematics]], arguing that occult science achieves what peripatetic philosophy merely attempts—systematic knowledge of causation and being.

Peripatetic philosophy (al-falsafa al-mashaʾiya) refers to the Aristotelian tradition as developed and transmitted in the Islamic world. The name derives from Aristotle's practice of teaching while walking (peripateo) in the Lyceum. Islamic philosophers—al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina—adopted and adapted Aristotelian logic, metaphysics, and natural philosophy, integrating them with Islamic theology and the philosophical heritage of Greek Neoplatonism. Key commitments: (1) Reason (ʿaql) is primary tool for understanding reality. (2) Logic (syllogistic reasoning) provides rigorous methodology. (3) Being (wujud) is fundamental category; understanding causation requires understanding gradations and dependencies of being. (4) Metaphysics is possible: systematic knowledge of principles transcending particular things. By Ibn Turka's era, peripatetic philosophy was the dominant intellectual framework across Islamic world. But Ibn Turka and his circle saw its limitations: peripatetic method, while rigorous, remained abstract and speculative. It described cosmic structure but did not engage it operatively. Peripatetic philosophers asked how creation unfolds; Ibn Turka asked how to participate in its unfolding. By integrating peripatetic logic with [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] and [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean mathematics]], Ibn Turka claimed to achieve what peripatetic philosophy merely attempted—systematic, rationally grounded knowledge of causation and being that is simultaneously operative (producing real effects). In this sense, Ibn Turka's system transcends peripatetic philosophy by incorporating and surpassing it.

The opponent that defines the project

Ibn Turka's argument is not conducted against superstition or against Sufism. It is conducted against the discipline that held the prestige, and the sharpness of his claim only registers if that is kept in view: the faux-universal concepts of philosophical speculation notwithstanding, only the letter encompasses all that is and is not, all that can and cannot be; it alone is the coincidentia oppositorum; hence lettrism is the only truly universal science (The Quest, iii).

"Faux-universal" is the operative insult. Peripatetic metaphysics claims to treat being qua being — the most general subject there is. Ibn Turka's counter is that a concept ranges over the actual and the possible and stops there, while the letter reaches also to non-being and impossibility. On that argument philosophy's universality is a boast its instruments cannot support.

The other side of the ledger

The relationship is not simple opposition, and the portal would mislead if it said so. Ibn Sīnā is one of the three positions the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] synthesises, alongside [[suhrawardi|Suhrawardī]] and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]/[[qunavi|Qūnavī]], on absolute being. Ibn Turka is a competent peripatetic before he is a critic of peripateticism, and the Mafāḥiṣ argues in a vocabulary the philosophers built.

He also has a treatise against Avicenna on the senses, and the sensorium article is devoted to his Pythagorean elevation of sight over hearing as vehicle of total perception — a technical disagreement conducted on peripatetic ground rather than a refusal of it.

Ibn Sīnā the anti-occultist, and his afterlife

The most striking fact in this relationship is the reversal. Ibn Sīnā heads the [[neoplatonism|anti-occultist coalition]] — with Ghazālī, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn — whose four centuries of polemic the mathematicalization was designed to answer (Powers of One, 1).

And then, in Safavid Iran, in the resurgence of neoplatonic-neopythagorean philosophy that followed, Ibn Sīnā himself was transformed into a neopythagorean-occultist. The tradition's chief prosecutor was posthumously enrolled in the defence.

What we do not know

  • Which peripatetic texts Ibn Turka taught or commented on beyond what the Tamhīd implies is not established here.
  • The treatise against Avicenna on the senses is discussed in the sensorium article but is not edited in the corpus in hand.
