Walāya

Walāya — sainthood, or the authority that comes of proximity to God. The most consequential reclassification in Melvin-Koushki's account, and the one usually missed: alongside their move into the mathematical sciences, the occult sciences were recast as sciences of walāya. This, he argues, is what alone explains the surge in patronage of professional occultists at the Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman courts in the runup to the Islamic millennium (1592 CE). A science of walāya is not a technique anyone may practise; it is the prerogative of the sanctified.

Walāya: occultism as a science of sanctity

The term. Walāya denotes sainthood, sanctity, and the proximity to God from which spiritual authority derives; a walī is a friend of God. In Shiʿi usage it carries the further charge of the Imams' authority. This is not peripheral vocabulary: it recurs across every major source in the corpus, some two hundred times in total.

The reclassification. Melvin-Koushki's thesis has two limbs, and walāya is the one usually overlooked. The occult sciences were shifted from the natural to the [[neoplatonism|mathematical]] sciences to reassert their scientific legitimacy; they were simultaneously reclassified as the sciences of walāya. He is emphatic about the explanatory weight the second move carries — it alone, he writes, explains the massive increase in patronage of professional occultists at the Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman courts in the runup to the Islamic millennium of 1592 CE (Powers of One, 1).

Why the second limb matters. Mathematicalization answers the epistemological objection: is this a science? Sanctification answers a different and, at court, more urgent question: who may practise it, and why should a ruler pay for them? A science of walāya cannot be picked up by anyone with a manual. It belongs to those near to God — which makes the occultist not a technician but a figure of religious authority, and his patronage a matter of dynastic legitimacy rather than curiosity. This is the mechanism underneath the [[isfahan-circle|occult court]].

The Shiʿi thread. The lineage runs through the Imams. Ibn Turka reports that the holy Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq greatly vaunted the science of letters, and that 'those who cleave to the threshold of his walāya have penned numerous works on the subject' (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66). Walāya is thus at once the ground of the science's authority and the channel of its transmission — the reason a chain of lettrist writing exists at all.

Bearing on Ibn Turka. It positions him. He is not merely systematising a technique; he is claiming for [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] the standing of a sanctified science, whose exponents are the ūlū l-aydī wa-l-abṣār — the Imams, the ancients and their disciples in every age — who preserved prophetically revealed philosophy in written form. It also explains how the science travelled after him: what reached the Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman courts was not a body of techniques but a claim about who stands close to God.

Why "sanctification" is the second half of a two-part thesis

It is easy to read Melvin-Koushki as arguing one thing — that the occult sciences were mathematicalized — and to treat walāya as a footnote. The text does not permit that. The formulation is deliberately double: the occult sciences were gradually but definitively shifted from the natural to the mathematical sciences as a means of reasserting their scientific legitimacy, and they were simultaneously reclassified as the sciences of walāya (Powers of One, 1).

The two moves answer different objections from different audiences.

Mathematicalization answers the philosophers. Against Ibn Sīnā, Ghazālī, Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn, who denied that these were sciences at all, the reply is that they belong among the mathematical disciplines, whose credentials were not in dispute.

Sanctification answers the court. A ruler does not need to be told that a discipline is epistemically respectable; he needs to know who may practise it and why he should pay them. A science of walāya is not available to anyone with a manual. It belongs to those near to God, which makes the practitioner a figure of religious authority and his retention a matter of dynastic legitimacy.

Melvin-Koushki gives the second limb the heavier causal load, and says so: it is this that alone explains the massive increase in patronage of professional occultists at the Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman courts in the runup to the Islamic millennium of 1592 (Powers of One, 1). Not mathematics — sanctity — is what opened the treasuries.

The Imams as the channel

The lineage runs through the Imams, which is what ties walāya to [[imamophilism|imamophilism]] rather than to formal Shiʿism. Ibn Turka reports that the holy Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq greatly vaunted this science, and those who cleave to the threshold of his walāya have penned numerous works on the subject (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

The phrasing is worth attention. Authorship is attributed not to a school or a lineage of teachers but to those who cleave to the threshold of his walāya — proximity to sanctity is what qualifies a person to write on letters at all. Walāya is simultaneously the ground of the science's authority and the channel of its transmission.

Timing: why the millennium matters

The surge in patronage is dated to the runup to the Islamic millennium of 1592, and the dating is not incidental. A court approaching a millennial turn has an acute interest in knowing where it stands in sacred history and in employing people who can say. A science of walāya supplies both a reading of the times and a sanctified reader. This is the mechanism underneath the [[isfahan-circle|occult court]], and it explains why the phenomenon is transregional — Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman courts arriving at the same solution because they faced the same calendar.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka used the term with the same technical force the later tradition gives it is not laid out in the sources in hand.
  • How the reclassification was argued — the specific texts in which occult sciences are formally reassigned to walāya — is treated by Melvin-Koushki at the level of the movement rather than the individual treatise.

Magically incarnating the Imams

The strongest statement of what the sanctification move actually claims comes in Melvin-Koushki's account of why occultists seized on these disciplines. They took them up as both the primary mode of philosophical praxis and the best means of harnessing walāya — which is to say, of magically incarnating the Imams (Another Scientific Revolution, 8).

That last phrase is worth not softening. The claim is not that the practitioner studies the Imams, or venerates them, or transmits their teaching. It is that the operations make their sanctity present and operative in the practitioner. [[jafr|Jafr]] is the clearest case: to divine by letters is to participate in the knowledge ʿAlī possessed, which is why it is simultaneously a science, a devotion and a political instrument.

This also explains the phrase in the Grammatology passage — that those who cleave to the threshold of Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq's walāya have written on the science. Authorship is licensed by proximity, and proximity is something one can move toward.

Why courts paid

A ruler acquiring an occultist under this description is not hiring a technician. He is attaching to his household a man whose competence is a form of sanctity, and whose prognostications carry the authority of the Imams' own knowledge. That is worth a stipend in a way that a clever calculator is not.

It also explains the timing of the surge Melvin-Koushki dates to the runup to the Islamic millennium of 1592. A court approaching a millennial turn has an urgent interest in knowing where it stands in sacred history, and in being seen to employ someone qualified to say. The phenomenon is transregional — Safavid, Mughal and Ottoman — because the calendar was.
