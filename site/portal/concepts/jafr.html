Jafr

Jafr — letter divination, and the political edge of [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]]. Its inventor is held to be ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, who in this tradition is hailed the chief mathematician of Islam; its foundational text is the K. al-Jafr wa-l-Jāmiʿa, an omniscient book recording past and future history and all knowledge of the cosmos. The dissertation gives it a chapter, under the heading political uses of lettrism.

What it is for

Where [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] in general is a science of what letters are, jafr is the application that tells you what is going to happen. The dissertation places its definition at the head of Chapter 5, Political uses of lettrism (§5.1), followed by lettrist political divination based on the Qur'an (§5.2). The classification is the argument: this is the branch that touches power.

The founding attribution

Jafr's inventor is ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, and the phrase Melvin-Koushki records for his standing in the later tradition is remarkable: ʿAlī, inventor of letter divination, was hailed the chief mathematician of Islam (Another Scientific Revolution, 8).

That is [[pythagorean-cosmology|the reclassification]] in a single epithet. Calling the first Imam a mathematician rather than a gnostic relocates the whole discipline: it is not that the Imams had secret knowledge, it is that they had exact knowledge, and exact knowledge belongs among the mathematical sciences.

The omniscient book

The science of letters entered Islam at its inception in the messianic-gnostic syncretism of the early ghulāt, and was associated in more sedate Shiʿi circles with the K. al-Jafr wa-l-Jāmiʿa of ʿAlī — an omniscient text recording past and future history and all knowledge of the cosmos (The Quest, 22).

The dual genealogy matters. Lettrism has, from the beginning, both an extreme wing and a respectable one, and the [[hurufism|three-strand]] distinction Ibn Turka's career depends on is a late episode in a very old sorting problem.

A story preserved in the tradition shows what possession of such a book was taken to mean. Offered the caliphate by al-Maʾmūn, the Imam replied that the compact would come to nothing; asked how he knew, he said: 'It is counterindicated in the Jafr and Jāmiʿ.' The document of compact itself is said to have carried the line, I have written this in accordance with your desire, but the Jafr and Jāmiʿ indicate the contrary (Better Than Sufi Sex, 23).

Incarnating the Imams

The link to [[walaya|walāya]] is direct and is put in strong terms. Occultists of this period seize on these sciences as both the primary mode of philosophical praxis and the best means of harnessing walāya — which is to say, of magically incarnating the Imams (Another Scientific Revolution, 8).

Jafr is therefore not fortune-telling with letters. It is a claim to participate in the Imams' knowledge, which is why it is simultaneously a science, a devotion and a political instrument, and why a court would pay for it.

The Ottoman success

The clearest demonstration of what jafr could become is not Iranian. [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Bisṭāmī's]] Miftāḥ al-jafr al-jāmiʿ, the Key to the Comprehensive Prognosticon, came to function in the 10th/16th century as a dynastic handbook, whereby the political fate of the House of ʿOs̱mān could be effectively managed over the generations (Another Scientific Revolution, 2).

A manual of letter divination operating as an instrument of dynastic management across generations is the most concrete evidence available for Melvin-Koushki's claim that the Ottoman branch of the network was more successful. Ibn Turka was tried three times; his counterpart's book became state equipment.

Ibn Turka's family and jafr

His son Najīb al-Dīn Ḥabīb Allāh appears to have written a brief lettrist treatise entitled Qavāʿid-i Jafr-i Jāmiʿ (The Quest, 41) — the son of a man exiled on occultist charges writing on the political-divinatory branch specifically.

What we do not know

  • Ibn Turka's own jafr practice, if any. The dissertation treats political uses of lettrism as a chapter; whether he cast prognostications himself is not established here.
  • Whether the K. al-Jafr wa-l-Jāmiʿa ever existed as a text in the form the tradition describes.
  • The contents of his son's treatise.
