The Imaginal Realm

Al-ʿAlam al-Khiyāl (The Imaginal Realm). In [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] metaphysics, the ontological layer between pure intellect and material sense where forms (ashkāl) exist independently of physical matter. The domain where mystical visions occur, where symbols manifest, where divine names express themselves in subtle forms. Central to understanding how knowledge operates in Islamic mysticism and occultism: the imaginal realm is where letters and divine names acquire form, where talismanic operations work, where the barzakh functions.

Al-ʿAlam al-Khiyāl: The Imaginal Realm

Al-ʿAlam al-Khiyāl (literally "the imaginal realm" or "the realm of imagination") is [[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi's]] term for an intermediate ontological domain between pure intellect (ʿaql) and material sensation (ḥiss). The Arabic word khiyāl is often mistranslated as "imagination" in the modern sense (the faculty of fantasy); Ibn Arabi means something far more precise and metaphysically serious: a realm of subtle forms (ashkāl) that possess reality independent of physical matter.

The imaginal realm occupies the same position in Ibn Arabi's cosmology that the [[barzakh|barzakh]] occupies in Ibn Turka's refinement of it: the crucial intermediate domain where causation operates, where divine principles become manifest in form, where knowledge acquires structure.

Three Realms of Being

Ibn Arabi divides creation into three principal domains:

1. The Intelligible Realm (ʿālam al-ʿaql): Pure intellect, transcendent principles, divine names in their unmanifested essence 2. The Imaginal Realm (ʿālam al-khiyāl): Forms, symbols, subtle bodies, the world of vision and mystical knowledge 3. The Sensible Realm (ʿālam al-ḥiss): Material existence, the physical world knowable through sense perception

Each realm participates in the others; none is hermetically sealed. The imaginal realm serves as the medium through which the intelligible becomes sensible, through which the transcendent becomes immanent.

The Imaginal in Mystical Experience

For Ibn Arabi, mystical visions (mushāhadāt) are not hallucinations or mere psychological phenomena—they are direct perception of the imaginal realm. When a mystic "sees" an angel, perceives a divine light, or encounters a teacher in a dream, they are perceiving realities that exist in the imaginal realm. These perceptions are as real as sensory experience, though they operate in a different ontological register.

This doctrine revolutionizes Islamic mysticism: it legitimizes mystical experience as a form of genuine knowledge by establishing that there is an objective realm (the imaginal) in which such experiences occur. Mysticism is not projection; it is perception.

The Imaginal in Occult Practice

[[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]] and the later occult philosophers adopted this framework. When a practitioner creates a talisman by writing letters and numbers with specific intention, the operation works in the imaginal realm first: the talisman, as an imaginal object (a form constituted by [[abjad-numerology|numerical correspondences]] and divine names), exerts influence on the sensible world. The imaginal realm is where the power resides; the physical talisman is the expression.

Similarly, [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist operations]] work because letters—as forms carrying [[divine-names|divine names]]—are primarily imaginal realities. Mastery of letters grants access to the imaginal realm, whence all material creation flows.

Legacy and Transmission

[[qunavi|Qūnavī]] elaborated Ibn Arabi's doctrine of the imaginal realm into a full philosophy of knowledge (gnosis vs. reason). Through the [[qunavi|Qūnavī]]-Jandī school, the imaginal realm became part of the theoretical apparatus available to philosophers, mystics, and occultists across the Islamic world. By Ibn Turka's time, the imaginal realm was accepted as a foundational concept: its reality was presupposed, not debated.

Why the translation matters

Khayāl is routinely rendered "imagination", and the rendering does real damage. In modern English, imagination names a faculty that produces things which are not real — the opposite of perception. Ibn ʿArabī means something almost inverted: a domain that is real, possessing its own subsistence, which the soul perceives rather than invents.

Hence "imaginal", Henry Corbin's coinage, adopted precisely to block the modern implication. The imaginal is not the imaginary. A vision occurring there is a perception of something, and can be true or false in the way perceptions can.

What follows from granting it reality

Everything operative in the tradition. If the intermediate domain is real and structured, then:

  • Visions are evidence. They report on something, and disagreement about them is substantive rather than a clash of private impressions.
  • Forms can be introduced there. A [[talismanic-science|talisman]] is an object composed at the imaginal level whose material inscription is secondary — which is why its efficacy is held to depend on intention and correspondence rather than on the physical substance alone.
  • [[ilm-al-huruf|Letters]] have a native home. A letter treated fī anfusi-hā — as it is in itself, apart from being counted, written or spoken — is a letter considered as an imaginal entity.

Two names for one domain

This entry and [[barzakh|barzakh]] describe substantially the same region under two vocabularies. The imaginal is the Akbarian name, approached from the side of the knower and what can be perceived. The barzakh is the name Ibn Turka's system leans on, approached from the side of causation and what can be done. Reading the two entries together gives the domain from both directions, which is how the sources present it.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka used the two terms interchangeably or maintained a technical distinction between them is not settled in these sources.
  • How the imaginal realm relates to the ordinary faculty of imagination in his psychology — whether they are the same power at different ranges — is not treated in the corpus in hand.
