Geomancy (ʿilm al-raml)

ʿIlm al-raml, the science of sand — divination by generating figures from marks made at random and reading them through a fixed combinatorial system. One of the three sciences Melvin-Koushki tracks as shifting from the natural to the mathematical in this period, alongside astrology and [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]]. It is also the ground of the portal's sharpest confrontation: Ibn Khaldūn against [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī]] — which does not run the way the reputations suggest.

What it is

Geomancy generates a set of figures from marks made without deliberation — traditionally in sand, hence the name — and reads them through a fixed system of combination. Each figure is a column of four positions, each either single or double, giving sixteen possible figures; these are then combined by rule to produce further figures and a chart to be interpreted.

The structure is what matters for this portal. The randomness is at the input only. Everything downstream is deterministic combination, which is precisely why the discipline could be argued into the mathematical sciences: what a geomancer does after the marks are made is calculate.

The reclassification

Melvin-Koushki's thesis names three sciences specifically — astrology, lettrism and geomancy — as those gradually but definitively shifted from the natural to the mathematical sciences as a means of reasserting their scientific legitimacy against four centuries of anti-occultist polemic (Powers of One, 1). Geomancy is arguably the easiest of the three to make the case for, since its operations are overtly combinatorial.

Ibn Khaldūn is not who the reputation says

This is the finding that reorganises the subject, and it is worth stating slowly.

The received picture. Ibn Khaldūn's purported objectivity and skepticism about the occult sciences is now much admired — held up as a shining, solitary example of rationality amidst rampant superstition, a man ahead of his time, famously rejecting well-patronised sciences like astrology and alchemy out of hand (In Defense of Geomancy, 349).

What is less acknowledged. That the same Tunisian historian argues forcefully, and at length, for the incontrovertible reality of magic and divination — with the selfsame sober objectivity (ibid.).

So the modern hero of Islamic rationalism was not a debunker of the occult. He rejected some occult sciences and accepted others, on grounds he took to be equally rigorous in both directions. The picture of a lone sceptic among credulous contemporaries is a modern construction that survives by not reading him all the way through.

And Yazdī is not the mirror image

The other side of the confrontation is equally unexpected. [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAlī Yazdī]] (d. 858/1454) — dynastic historian to the Timurids and committed occultist — has theories of historical evolution and human potential not dissimilar to Ibn Khaldūn's more famous formulations, yet predicated precisely on a thoroughgoingly occultist outlook (In Defense of Geomancy, 349).

Two historians of the same generation, arriving at comparable accounts of how societies develop, from opposite metaphysical premises. Both men's views on occultism go to the heart of their respective sociologies and political theologies, which accordingly converge and diverge in instructive ways.

The confrontation is therefore not rationalism against superstition. It is two sophisticated theories of history disagreeing about which occult sciences are real.

What we do not know

  • Whether they met. Ibn Khaldūn lived in Cairo 785–808/1383–1406, overlapping Yazdī's residence there with Ibn Turka; Melvin-Koushki notes they possibly met (Powers of One, 37).
  • Ibn Turka's own position on geomancy specifically is not set out in the corpus in hand, though the science is one of the three named in the reclassification thesis.
  • Which geomantic manuals circulated in Timurid Iran is treated in Persianate Geomancy rather than here.

The procedure, and the name

The Latin geomantia translates ʿilm al-raml imprecisely. The Arabic names — ʿilm al-raml, khaṭṭ al-raml, ḍarb, ṭarq — all point at the original operation: drawing sixteen random series of lines in sand or dust, reducing them to figures, and reading those against a tableau of twelve houses (Geomancy in the Islamic World, 1).

The sixteen figures were then correlated to further orders of being. One manuscript diagram maps them onto the limbs of the human body (Kitāb fī ʿilm al-raml, BnF Arabe 2631, f. 66b; Divining Past, Present, and Future in the Sand, 9, fig. 26) — the same species of correspondence table the [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist sciences]] run on. Sixteen figures, twelve houses, a body: the art is combinatorial all the way down once the marks are made.

How popular, and how far it travelled

Geomancy ranked in popularity only behind astrology and [[oneiromancy|oneiromancy]] throughout the early modern western Islamicate world — third on a very short list (Divining Past, Present, and Future in the Sand, 1).

Its reach was the longest of the three. It propagated across Afro-Eurasia and into sub-Saharan Africa as ifa, gara and sikidy, and thence to the western hemisphere, where those traditions remain very much in use (Geomancy in the Islamic World, 1). Melvin-Koushki elsewhere calls it another Pythagorean science, Afro-Arabic cognate to the I Ching — noting that unlike the Chinese oracle, this one travelled (A Grammar of Weirding, 7).

It was also, at one point, new. Ṭūsī's Jāmiʿ al-ʿulūm, the first comprehensive Persian encyclopedia of the rational and religious sciences, introduces geomancy to its eastern readership as a novel occult science by styling it gharīb — strange (Another Scientific Revolution, 5). The word that would later name the whole category of ʿulūm gharība arrives here as a description of novelty.

Yazdī's defence, in five moves

The confrontation with Ibn Khaldūn has a structure, and it is where the metaphysics shows (all: In Defense of Geomancy, 51).

1. On mechanism. Geomancy is wholly based on intention (niyyat) and mentation or imagination (pindārī). A powerful spiritual connection must first be established between intention and action; the practitioner must then mentally establish a correspondence (munāsabatī) with the all-emanating Source (mabdaʾ-i fayyāḍ) of [[neoplatonism|Neoplatonic]] cosmology. A lack of concentration, impure motivations or basic ineptitude cannot but produce invalid readings.

That last clause is the most interesting thing in the defence. The art has a failure condition, and it is a condition of the operator rather than of the procedure — which insulates the science from its practitioners, and answers the charlatan objection before it is made.

2. The lettrist proof. Geomancy's stated goal — to obtain information about unknown circumstances and bring clarity to ambiguous situations — is confirmed through its numerological correspondence with the divine name Light (Nūr). Given the central role of light in the lettrist metaphysics of Ibn Turka's circle, Melvin-Koushki's gloss is that Yazdī is here effectively declaring: Q.E.D.

3. The traditionalist proof. Ibn Khaldūn scornfully dismissed the claim that the prophet Daniel invented geomancy. Yazdī defends it on the same grounds: geomancy bears a numerological correspondence to the name Daniel, so the historical association necessarily holds. A question of attribution is settled by arithmetic.

4. On social status. He rejects the charge that geomancy is a low soothsaying persuasive only to the untutored and abused by charlatans.

5. On revelation and system. Its general rules were first prophetically revealed, then systematised into a science usable by anyone whose intuition is sound and who follows the correct procedure. The same two-stage structure — revealed, then made systematic — that Ibn Turka claims for [[pythagorean-cosmology|the perennial philosophy]].

Who the tract was for

Yazdī spent part of 795/1393–810/1408 in Cairo with his teacher and friend Ibn Turka, and Ibn Khaldūn lived in the city from 785/1383 until his death in 808/1406. The tract gives the distinct impression of having been addressed in the first instance to Ibn Khaldūn (In Defense of Geomancy, 5 and n.6).

Note also the description of the relationship: teacher and friend. Yazdī's own account confirms what the Munshaʾāt imply.

What the defence reveals about the circle

The characteristic move is visible in miniature. A popular divinatory practice is not defended on its own terms but absorbed into lettrism and validated by it — the proof that geomancy works is a numerological correspondence to a divine name. That is precisely the operation Ibn Turka performs on metaphysics at large.

It also sets the terms of the opposition, and explains why he in particular was dangerous. By Ibn al-Qayyim's and Ibn Khaldūn's standard, occult philosophers like Ibn Turka, who systematized lettrism as a superior metaphysics, must be considered a double threat to society and religion (In Defense of Geomancy, 23). A diviner threatens religion; a man who proves divination from first principles threatens the order of the sciences as well.
