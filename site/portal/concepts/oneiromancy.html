Oneiromancy (ʿilm taʿbīr al-ruʾyā)

ʿIlm taʿbīr al-ruʾyā — the science of dream interpretation, and the occult science this portal has most consistently underrated. Melvin-Koushki ranks it second in popularity only to astrology across the early modern Islamicate world, ahead of [[geomancy|geomancy]] and [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] alike. Its classificatory definition is unusually precise: physiognomy inquires into the inner states of rational souls while they are conscious, and oneiromancy does the same while they are unconscious. Both are readings of a soul; only the soul's condition differs.

ʿIlm taʿbīr al-ruʾyā: The Science of Dream Interpretation

Where it sits

Oneiromancy (Arabic ʿilm taʿbīr al-ruʾyā, Persian ʿilm-i taʿbīr) is classified among the applied natural sciences — not the mathematical ones — in the classifications of the sciences Melvin-Koushki works from. It appears in the standard list alongside medicine, veterinary medicine and falconry, physiognomy, judicial astrology, magic, talismans and illusionism.

t; Melvin-Koushki, Another Scientific Revolution, 6.

Its placement there is argued, not arbitrary, and the argument is the most useful sentence in the entry:

t; "Physiognomy then inquires into the inner states of rational human souls while they are
t; conscious, and oneiromancy does the same while they are unconscious."
t; Another Scientific Revolution, 6, quoting the classification at Melvin-Koushki 2017, 17.

So oneiromancy is not a separate faculty from reading a face. It is the same operation — inferring an interior state from an exterior sign — performed on a soul that has gone offline. That is a claim about continuity between waking and sleeping cognition, and it is what makes the science respectable rather than folkloric in these classifications.

How popular, actually

Very. The ranking is consistent across at least five separate places in the corpus, always in the same form: geomancy is described as "third in popularity only to astrology and oneiromancy" throughout the early modern Western (variously Islamo-Judeo-Christian, Helleno-Judeo-Christo-Islamic) world.

t; Melvin-Koushki, Divining Past, Present, and Future in the Sand, 1;
t; Geomancy in the Islamic World, 1; In Defense of Geomancy, 6;
t; Melvin-Koushki and Cummins, Geomancing Is Googling, 2.

Read that the other way and it says: dream divination outranked geomancy, lettrism and every operative occult science except astrology. The portal's coverage does not currently reflect that, which is worth noticing.

It also enjoyed heavy royal patronage in exactly the company you would expect: "astrology, lettrism, geomancy, oneiromancy and physiognomy in particular enjoyed heavy royal patronage."

t; Another Scientific Revolution, 1.

The Ottoman Sultan Murad III (r. 1574–1595), "highly sensitive to his own status as king of the millennium," invested heavily in Sufism and the occult sciences, "particularly astrology and oneiromancy," while identifying his royal persona with ʿAlī in the time-honoured Timurid-Safavid manner.

t; Melvin-Koushki, Early Modern Islamicate Empire, 16.

Ibn Turka's stake in it

The corpus does not preserve a dream narrative of Ibn Turka's own — see the caution below. What it does preserve is his epistemology, and dreaming is load-bearing in it. Melvin-Koushki describes Ibn Turka's position as the elegant dissolution of the Science versus Religion binary, on the grounds that

t; "empirical observation and dreamtime intuition are equally important to the business of
t; Science."
t; Melvin-Koushki, Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Sensorium, 15 (n. 33).

That is not a concession to piety. It is a claim that the sleeping mind is an instrument, and it belongs beside his demand — against Suhrawardī's ascent-only sensorium — that a total system requires descent (tanzīl) as well as ascent.

t; Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Sensorium, 2.

The dream as evidence

One concrete case of dream-vision doing evidentiary work in this literature. The attribution of the invention of Kufic script is contested between ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and Ibn Muqla. Āmulī simply reports that scholars differ. Qāżī Aḥmad, writing an album preface in the 10th/16th-century imperial Twelver context, instead supplies a dream-vision narrative to resolve the attribution in favour of ʿAlī.

t; Melvin-Koushki, Of Islamic Grammatology, 44.

A dream settles a philological dispute. That is oneiromancy operating not as divination about the future but as a warrant about the past — and it happens inside the album-preface genre that Melvin-Koushki elsewhere says embodies Ibn Turka's ontogrammatology.

Caution — an absence, recorded

No attested dream or vision narrative of Ibn Turka's own has been located in this corpus. Searching it for dream returns 25 hits in the Yale dissertation alone, but they are overwhelmingly the term used analytically or metaphorically rather than a report of something he dreamt. Anything built on "Ibn Turka's visionary life" should therefore be built as practice — a technique the sources describe, with the cost they specify — and not as an invented anecdote. See [docs/VISIONARY_ENVIRONMENTS.md](../../docs/VISIONARY_ENVIRONMENTS.md), which states this rule for the game work, and khalʿ (doffing the body) for the technique itself.

Where it touches Ibn Turka's life directly

At the most consequential point in the family's history. Fażl Allāh Astarābādī arrived in Isfahan around 766/1365 and quickly gained fame as a dream interpreter, and it was in that capacity that Ibn Turka's father Afżal al-Dīn and elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] sought him out.

Naṣr Allāh Nāfajī's Khwābnāma — a book of dreams, the genre itself a witness to how much of this material circulated — preserves the episode: Ṣadr al-Dīn attended an afternoon majlis, presented a dream in which he saw the Prophet seated and went up to kiss his hand, then noticed he had inadvertently left saliva on the back of his own hand, and felt ashamed (The Quest, 58 n.16).

In 766/1365 this was an ordinary transaction: a respected scholar consulting a famous interpreter. Melvin-Koushki is explicit that the contact does not imply Ḥurūfī inclinations, and that the story survives to establish Fażl Allāh's standing as a Sufi before his messianic transformation.

But it put a documented family connection to the founder of a proscribed movement into the record, and it was still there in 1427 when Ibn Turka's opponents needed a charge that would carry at [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court. The most respectable of the divinatory sciences is what supplied the evidence for the accusation that ended his career.

Why it is not a lesser science

Dream-vision sits differently from the other divinatory arts because it does not require an operation. [[geomancy|Geomancy]] needs marks and a tableau; [[jafr|jafr]] needs a text and a calculation. A dream arrives. What the diviner supplies is interpretation, which makes oneiromancy the divinatory science closest to exegesis — and therefore the one that sits most comfortably beside orthodox learning.

It also touches the [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]] more directly than any other divinatory art. If the imaginal is a domain that is real and perceived rather than invented, then a dream is not a private psychological event but a perception, and interpreting one is reading something that was there.
