Kitab al-Mafahis

Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ (Book of Inquiries). Ibn Turka's magnum opus and, in Melvin-Koushki's description, the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism. Begun shortly after Of Letters and almost certainly at the instance of his patron Iskandar Sulṭān; completed 823/1420, revised and expanded 828/1425. It sets out the fourfold analysis of the letter that is the technical core of his [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist metaphysics]].

Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ: the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism

Composition. Ibn Turka began the Book of Inquiries shortly after completing his earlier treatise Of Letters, and again almost certainly at the instance of Iskandar Sulṭān. He completed it in 823/1420 and revised and expanded it in 828/1425. Melvin-Koushki calls it the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism and the fullest expression of Ibn Turka's lettrist metaphysics (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

The fourfold schema. The Mafāḥiṣ massively expands the fourfold schema first proposed in Of Letters, treating the meanings of the letters according to their three forms —

  • numerological (iḥṣāʾī)
  • symbological (kitābī)
  • phonological (kalāmī)

— together with a fourth register: the letters as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā). That fourth term is what separates Ibn Turka's project from technique. The first three treat the letter as it is counted, written and spoken; the fourth treats it as a thing with its own being, prior to any of these. A science of letters in that fourth sense is an ontology, not a manual.

Theological ground. Lettrist theory of this kind is necessarily predicated on the doctrine of the uncreatedness of the Qur'an — if the letters were created artefacts they could not carry the ontological weight placed on them. Ibn Turka accordingly bemoans the contemporary popularity of Zamakhsharī's (d. 538/1144) Kashshāf, singling out for censure its failure to recognise the intrinsic ontological majesty of the quranic letters (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66 n.88, citing 'The Quest', 59, 54, 76, 116, 342).

Transmission. Over half of Ibn Turka's roughly forty-six known Persian and Arabic works remain in manuscript, the lettrist works most of all, on the assumption that they were the most marginal part of his output. The Mafāḥiṣ is the central casualty of that assumption and the principal object of Melvin-Koushki's dissertation, which presents and contextualises it.

The fourfold schema, unpacked

The technical core of the book is an analysis of the letter in four registers, expanding a scheme first proposed in the earlier Of Letters. The letters are treated according to their three forms —

  • numerological (iḥṣāʾī) — the letter as counted, the [[abjad-numerology|abjad]] value

and everything computable from it;

  • symbological (kitābī) — the letter as written, its shape, its geometry, its

disposition on the page;

  • phonological (kalāmī) — the letter as spoken, its sound, its point of articulation,

its behaviour in recitation;

— and then, in a fourth and separate register, as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā) (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

The fourth term is what makes the book a work of metaphysics rather than a manual. The first three treat the letter as it appears to counting, sight and hearing — three modes of access. The fourth treats the letter as a being with its own nature prior to any access at all. A science that stops at the first three is a technique; a science that reaches the fourth is an ontology, and it is on the fourth that the claim to be the [[occult-science-universal|only truly universal science]] rests.

What the book has to presuppose

Lettrist theory of this kind is necessarily predicated on the doctrine of the uncreatedness of the Qur'an. If the letters were created things, they could not be the principles through which creation is constituted; the argument would invert. Ibn Turka is alert to this and polices it: he bemoans the contemporary popularity of Zamakhsharī's (d. 538/1144) Kashshāf, singling out for censure its failure to recognise the intrinsic ontological majesty of the quranic letters (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66 n.88, citing The Quest, 54, 59, 76, 116, 342).

That is a revealing target. The Kashshāf is a celebrated and widely taught exegesis. To attack it on this point is to say that the standard way of reading the Qur'an in his own century had missed what the letters are.

Where it sits in the oeuvre

The Mafāḥiṣ is the summa, and Melvin-Koushki's description of it is precise: the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism, and the fullest expression of Ibn Turka's lettrist metaphysics (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66). Two points of placement matter.

Against the Tamhīd. The [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid]] is far better known in the modern period and is, in the estimation of the author and his contemporaries, strictly of secondary importance. The Mafāḥiṣ is the book he thought he had written. The inversion of their reputations is the central historiographical fact about his reception.

Against the chronology. It was completed in 823/1420 and revised and expanded in 828/1425 — the revision falling in the same window, 827–29/1425–27, in which two-thirds of the surviving oeuvre was being copied into [[ms-majlis-10196|MS Majlis 10196]], and immediately before the exile of 1427. The book was being finished and recopied while its author's position was collapsing.

The manuscript witness

The best witness is [[ms-majlis-10196|MS Majlis 10196]], and it is unusually good: the incipit and explicit of the Mafāḥiṣ, folios 52a and 118b, are in Ibn Turka's own hand. The copyist was in contact with the author at the opening and the close of this text specifically. Where a colophon in that codex dates the Mafāḥiṣ, the chain of custody is about as short as this material ever offers.

Access

Chapter 7 of the dissertation consists largely of translated sections from the Mafāḥiṣ, and Part 2 supplies editions and translations of five minor lettrist treatises. For a reader without Arabic that chapter is the closest thing to the book itself.

What we do not know

  • There is no critical edition. The work is known through manuscripts and through

Melvin-Koushki's translated selections.

  • Whether Iskandar commissioned it is a judgement — "almost certainly at his instance"

— not a documented fact.

  • What the 828/1425 revision changed is not laid out in the sources in hand; we know

that it was expanded, not how.

  • Whether the colophon dates composition or transcription is, for much of the codex,

undecidable.
