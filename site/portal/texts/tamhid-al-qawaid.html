Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid

Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid — a synthesis of Ibn Sīnā, Suhrawardī and Ibn ʿArabī/Qūnavī on absolute being, and by far the best known of Ibn Turka's works in the modern period. It is also, in the estimation of the author and his contemporaries, strictly of secondary importance. Its fame is the single largest reason his actual project was misread for a century.

The famous book that got him wrong

The work. The Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid addresses the doubts of the peripatetics and illuminationists on the topic of absolute being (al-wujūd al-muṭlaq), relying primarily on the [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] school tradition as formulated by [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] and [[jandi|Jandī]]. It is a serious and accomplished piece of scholastic metaphysics.

Its modern career. It became a staple teaching text in Imami seminary curricula in Iran, probably adopted in the late Qajar period, and Ibn Turka's modern reputation in Iran rests almost exclusively on it. ʿAllāma Ṭabāṭabāʾī celebrates him in al-Mīzān as a preeminent synthesizer of peripatetic philosophy and theoretical mysticism; the standard picture places him somewhere between Naṣīr al-Dīn Ṭūsī and Mullā Ṣadrā (The Quest, 6).

The problem. The Tamhīd is bare of any reference to the author's more central lettrist concerns, and Melvin-Koushki's verdict on its standing is blunt: it is by far the best known of the works in the modern period but strictly of secondary importance in the estimation of the author and his contemporaries. A reputation built on it produces a distorted image of a Sufi-philosopher in the scholastic mode — in short, a mere precursor to the philosophical school of Isfahan.

Why this matters beyond bibliography. The irony Melvin-Koushki draws out is exact: the acclaim is what caused the neglect. Being celebrated since the nineteenth century as the link between Ibn ʿArabī and Mullā Ṣadrā is precisely what marginalised the [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrist]] half of the oeuvre, by abstracting the project from its historical context and robbing it of its animating virtue (The Quest, ii). Over half his roughly forty-five works remain in manuscript, the lettrist ones above all, on the assumption that they are the marginal part. The Tamhīd is the reason that assumption was ever plausible.

The mechanism of the distortion

It is worth being exact about how a book's fame damages an author's reputation, because the case is unusually clean.

Step one: the work enters a curriculum. The Tamhīd became a staple teaching text in Imami seminary curricula in Iran, probably adopted in the late Qajar period. Curricular texts are read by everyone in a tradition and become the lens through which their author is known.

Step two: the curriculum defines the author. Ibn Turka's modern reputation in Iran rests almost exclusively on it. ʿAllāma Ṭabāṭabāʾī celebrates him in al-Mīzān as a preeminent synthesizer of peripatetic philosophy and theoretical mysticism.

Step three: the definition excludes the rest. Because the Tamhīd is bare of any reference to the author's more central lettrist concerns, a reputation built on it has no place to put the lettrist works. They become, by default, the marginal part of the oeuvre — and over half the corpus, disproportionately the lettrist half, stayed in manuscript on exactly that assumption (The Quest, ii, 6).

Step four: the neglect looks like a judgement. Later scholars, finding the lettrist works unedited, reasonably infer that specialists had assessed and set them aside. They had not; they had never looked.

The readings this produced

Three, all rejected by Melvin-Koushki, and each instructive:

  • Corbin embraced Ibn Turka warmly as a standard-issue Shiʿi esotericist — which at least complicated the featureless scholastic picture, but imported a confessional assumption the sources do not support.
  • The Iranian seminary consensus places him between Naṣīr al-Dīn Ṭūsī and Mullā Ṣadrā: a mystical philosopher, a precursor to the school of Isfahan.
  • Lewisohn, working from the [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]] rather than the Tamhīd, arrives at an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker — a different route to the same kind of error, since the apologies were produced under duress.

Melvin-Koushki's summary of the net effect is blunt: the de facto scholarly consensus presents Ibn Turka as a mystical philosopher, effectively obscuring his explicitly occultist project (The Quest, 6–7).

What the book is actually good for

None of this makes the Tamhīd a bad book. It addresses the doubts of the peripatetics and illuminationists on absolute being (al-wujūd al-muṭlaq), relying on the [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] school as formulated by [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] and [[jandi|Jandī]], and it is accomplished enough to have held a seminary curriculum for over a century. As evidence it establishes that Ibn Turka was a fully competent scholastic metaphysician — which matters, because it means the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] is not the work of someone who took up occultism for want of the harder discipline.

What we do not know

  • When it was written, relative to the lettrist works, is not established in the sources in hand — which would settle whether it is early scholastic work or a deliberate parallel track.
  • Why he wrote a book with no reference to his central concerns is not answered. Audience, patron, occasion and prudence are all candidates; the sources do not choose.
