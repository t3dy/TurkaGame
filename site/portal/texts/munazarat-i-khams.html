Risāla-yi Munāẓarāt-i Khams

Risāla-yi Munāẓarāt-i Khams — the Five Disputations, written in 808/1405, near the end of the Cairo years and a year after the [[sharh-nazm-al-durr|Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr]]. A disputation cycle in the Mamluk literary manner Ibn Turka had spent a decade absorbing.

Composition. Written in 808/1405 (The Quest, chronology), in Cairo, in the closing years of the fifteen-year residence. Tīmūr had died at Utrar the same year; Ibn Turka would return to Isfahan around 810/1408.

The genre. Munāẓara is formal disputation — a debate staged between positions, or between personified things, resolved by argument. The form was thoroughly at home in the Mamluk literary culture Ibn Turka was working inside: the same milieu produced the sword-versus-pen debates written by Ibn Nubāta and al-Ṣafadī, the two authors whose anthology-as-commentary genre he had just adapted into Persian in the [[sharh-nazm-al-durr|Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr]] (Of Islamic Grammatology, 38).

Why it belongs in a portal about occult philosophy. Because it shows the method before the doctrine. A man whose mature position is that [[ilm-al-huruf|only the letter]] is the coincidentia oppositorum — the point at which opposites coincide — spent his apprenticeship writing formal contests between opposed positions. The disputational habit is visible across the oeuvre, most sharply in the invidious comparison of lettrism with philosophy that runs through the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]].

The later echo. Imperial Talismanic Love reads a much later work — the Debate of Feast and Fight of 1426 — as simultaneously philosophical romance and lettrist mirror for princes. Ibn Turka was still writing debates twenty years on, by then with the whole system behind them.

What we do not know. What the five disputations are between; whether the text survives; whether it has been edited.

The form, and what it trains

Munāẓara is formal disputation: a contest staged between opposed positions and resolved by argument. The form is old, thoroughly institutionalised, and — crucially — a genre in which one writes both sides. A disputation author has to make the losing case well enough that its defeat means something.

That is an unusual apprenticeship for a philosopher whose mature claim is that the letter is the coincidentia oppositorum, the point at which opposites coincide. Ibn Turka spent his early career constructing oppositions and adjudicating them, and then argued that reality has a place where the adjudication is already performed.

The Mamluk setting

The same literary culture produced the celebrated sword-versus-pen debates of Ibn Nubāta (d. 768/1366) and al-Ṣafadī (d. 764/1363) — the two authors whose anthology-as-commentary genre Ibn Turka had adapted into Persian a year earlier in the [[sharh-nazm-al-durr|Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr]] (Of Islamic Grammatology, 38).

So 806/1404 and 808/1405 give two works in two Mamluk genres in successive years. The Cairo apprenticeship was, among other things, a training in form, and both forms came east with him.

The late echo

He never stopped writing debates. Imperial Talismanic Love reads the Debate of Feast and Fight of 1426 as simultaneously philosophical romance and lettrist mirror for princes — written in the middle of the years of accusation, twenty-one years after the Five Disputations, with the whole system behind it.

What we do not know

  • What the five disputations are between. The title names their number, not their subjects.
  • Whether the text survives, and whether it has been edited.
  • Whether they are already lettrist, or purely literary exercises.
