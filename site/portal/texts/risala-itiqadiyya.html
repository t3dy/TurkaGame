Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya

Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya — a statement of belief written for [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]] during the nine months Ibn Turka spent in weekly attendance at the Herat court, waiting on a review of his case that never came. A creed composed in those conditions is evidence about the conditions.

primary-sourcecreedbaysunghurheratduress

Composition. Written at Herat across 834–35/1431–32, in the same interval as the second recension of the [[nafsat-al-masdur|Nafsat al-Maṣdūr]], while Ibn Turka was for nine months in weekly attendance at court waiting for the review of his case (The Quest, chronology). He died there in 835/1432.

The addressee matters. Not the sovereign but [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh]] — the Timurid prince the Munshaʾāt show him closest to among the amirs, and the greatest book-patron of the age. Writing a creed for Bāysunghur rather than for [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] is a choice about who might carry the argument inward. It is the last patronage relationship in the biography and it was being worked to the end.

How to read it. With the same caution the [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]] require, and more of it. Melvin-Koushki's warning is that the self-image Ibn Turka presents in the apologies, produced under great duress, is hardly reflective of his primary concerns (The Quest, 6–7). An iʿtiqādiyya is by genre a statement of what one believes; one written in the ninth month of an unanswered wait, for a prince who might intervene, is a document about the wait.

What it is nonetheless good for. The vocabulary a man in his position thought would carry — which doctrines had to be affirmed, which affiliations disowned, what shape orthodoxy took when it was being demonstrated rather than assumed.

What we do not know. Its contents; whether Bāysunghur read it; whether it survives. Bāysunghur died in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka.

The genre, and why the date changes it

An iʿtiqādiyya is a statement of what one believes — the doctrinal core set out for inspection. In ordinary circumstances it is among the most straightforward things a scholar writes.

These were not ordinary circumstances. It was composed at Herat in 834–35/1431–32, during the [[nine-months-waiting|nine months of weekly attendance]] at a court that had promised to review his case and did not, in the same interval as the second recension of the [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]]. He died there.

Melvin-Koushki's caution about the apologies applies here with full force: the self-image Ibn Turka presents in writing of this kind, produced under great duress, is hardly reflective of his primary concerns (The Quest, 6–7). A creed written in the ninth month of an unanswered wait is a document about the wait.

The choice of addressee

Not [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]], who could restore him, but [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]], the prince the [[munshaat-i-turka|letters]] show him closest to among the Timurid amirs.

That is a calculation about routes. The sovereign was not receiving him; the son might carry an argument inward. It is the last patronage relationship in the biography, and it was being worked in the final year of a life spent working patronage relationships — all four of which ended in murder, blinding, death or deferral.

What it is good evidence for

Not his settled convictions. But an iʿtiqādiyya composed under these conditions is a precise record of what a man in his position judged would carry: which doctrines had to be affirmed, which associations disowned, what shape orthodoxy takes when it is being demonstrated rather than assumed. That is worth having, provided nobody mistakes it for the system.

What we do not know

Its contents; whether Bāysunghur received or read it; whether it survives. Bāysunghur died in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka, and no response is recorded.
