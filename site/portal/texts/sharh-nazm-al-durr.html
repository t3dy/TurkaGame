Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr

Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr — Ibn Turka's first major work, completed in Cairo in 806/1404 at the end of his fifteen years abroad. Formally a commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's al-Tāʾiyya al-kubrā, it timuridizes the Mamluk anthology-as-commentary genre and is the first such commentary to re-poeticize the Ibn ʿArabī school tradition.

The work. Completed toward the end of Ibn Turka's Cairo residence, the Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr is an ornate Persian commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's (d. 1235) al-Tāʾiyya al-kubrā, a favourite teaching text of the [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] school.

What is original in it. Melvin-Koushki shows that it closely tracks high Mamluk Arabic literary culture and effectively timuridizes the new hybrid Mamluk anthology-as-commentary genre, pioneered by Ibn Nubāta (d. 1366) and his student al-Ṣafadī (d. 1363) — both of whom also wrote seminal sword-versus-pen debates. It is the first such commentary to effectively re-poeticize that tradition, bringing each line of the poem into conjunction with several Persian or Arabic lines of verse, both contemporary and classical (Imperial Talismanic Love, 70).

Why it belongs in this portal. It is evidence about formation rather than doctrine. The man who would later argue that letters are cosmic principles began by writing a book about how verses answer to other verses — and he learned the form in Mamluk Cairo and carried it east. The lettrist project has a literary genealogy as well as a philosophical one.

It is also the base text for Akram Jūdī-Niʿmatī's edition, whose introduction supplies the list of works from which Melvin-Koushki's own annotated catalogue is built (The Quest, §2.1).

The genre, and why it was worth importing

The anthology-as-commentary was a Mamluk innovation: a commentary that answers each line of its base poem with further verses rather than with prose exposition, so the book becomes an anthology organised by another poem's structure. Melvin-Koushki names its developers — Ibn Nubāta (d. 768/1366), emulated by al-Ṣafadī (d. 764/1363) and by Ibn Ḥijja al-Ḥamawī (d. 837/1434), Ibn Turka's own contemporary. Ibn Nubāta and al-Ṣafadī both also wrote seminal sword versus pen debates (Of Islamic Grammatology, 38; Imperial Talismanic Love, 70).

Ibn Turka's book is the first Persian adaptation of that Mamluk form. He learned a genre in Cairo and carried it east, and Melvin-Koushki's verb for what he did with it is timuridize.

Re-poeticizing the Akbarian tradition

The base text is Ibn al-Fāriḍ's al-Tāʾiyya al-kubrā, a favourite teaching text of the [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] school. That school had spent two centuries turning a visionary inheritance into scholastic philosophy — the process [[qunavi|Qūnavī]] began.

Ibn Turka's commentary runs the other way. By answering each line with several further lines of Persian and Arabic verse, contemporary and classical, it is the first such commentary to effectively re-poeticize that tradition. The Akbarian material is returned to the medium it came in.

Why this is not a detour from the lettrism

Because on Ibn Turka's own principles it is the same subject. If letters are ontological entities, their arrangement is participation rather than decoration, and lettrist theory informs the ornate literary practice of the period — with Ibn Turka himself a vaunted stylist of Persian prose (The Quest, iii).

A book whose method is the conjunction of verses is a book about how arrangements of letters answer to one another. The first major work and the summa are doing related things in different registers.

Its place in the record

Completed 806/1404, at the end of the Cairo years — the first major work, written after roughly a decade under [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]]. It is also the base text for Akram Jūdī-Niʿmatī's edition, whose introduction supplies the list of works from which Melvin-Koushki builds his own annotated catalogue in §2.1 of the dissertation. The bibliography of the whole oeuvre rests partly on the edition of this book.

What we do not know

  • Whether it contains lettrist material in embryo is not stated in these sources.
  • For whom it was written — patron or occasion — is not recorded.
  • How it was received in Cairo, in the literary culture whose genre it adapted, is not discussed.
