Nafsat al-Maṣdūr (the Apologies)

Nafsat al-Maṣdūr — Ibn Turka's apologies, written in self-defence during the tribunals and the exile. The second was composed at Herat during the nine months he spent in weekly attendance at court waiting for his case to be reviewed. They are the most personal thing he wrote and, for exactly that reason, the least reliable guide to what he actually thought.

The works. The Nafsat al-Maṣdūr — 'the breath of one afflicted in the chest' — are Ibn Turka's apologies, written under accusation. The second recension belongs to 834–35/1431–32, the nine months at Herat in weekly attendance at court, waiting on a review of his case that never came; he wrote the Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya for [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]] in the same interval (The Quest, chronology).

The trap they set for readers. Leonard Lewisohn's article on the apologies takes them as evidence of Ibn Turka's real position and presents him as an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker. Melvin-Koushki's objection is not that the reading is careless but that the genre will not bear it: the image Ibn Turka presents of himself in the apologies, produced under great duress, is hardly reflective of his primary concerns (The Quest, 6–7).

This is a general lesson about the sources and worth stating plainly. A defendant's account of his own orthodoxy, written while waiting on a tribunal, is a document about the tribunal. Read as autobiography it produces a man who was never especially interesting; read as forensic rhetoric it shows what an accusation of this kind required a man to say.

What they are good evidence for. The mechanics of the charge, the vocabulary available for answering it, and the [[hurufism|distinctions]] he thought would carry weight with [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court. That is a great deal — provided nobody mistakes it for the system.

What kind of evidence a coerced document is

The methodological point here generalises beyond Ibn Turka and is worth stating plainly, because the portal relies on it in several places.

A document produced under duress is excellent evidence about the situation that produced it and poor evidence about the settled convictions of its author. It tells you what an accusation of this kind required a man to say, which distinctions his court thought would carry, which affiliations were dangerous enough to disavow, and how a defendant in this system constructed a persona. Those are substantial historical yields.

What it does not tell you is what the author thought when nobody was asking. Melvin-Koushki states the limit exactly: the image Ibn Turka presents of himself in the apologies, produced under great duress, is hardly reflective of his primary concerns (The Quest, 6–7).

The reading it produced

Leonard Lewisohn's article on the apologies is described as recent and welcome, and its call for more research is endorsed — the objection is specifically to the inference. Reading the apologies as a statement of position, Lewisohn presents Ibn Turka as an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker, which Melvin-Koushki considers a misrepresentation.

The error is symmetrical with the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd]] problem. In both cases a genuine work by Ibn Turka, read as representative, produces a man who is not the author of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]]. One is a scholastic treatise that omits his central concerns; the other is a defence that suppresses them. Between them they have supplied the modern consensus, which presents him as a mystical philosopher and effectively obscures his explicitly occultist project.

What the apologies do corroborate

They are not worthless as biography, and Melvin-Koushki uses them. The [[munshaat-i-turka|letters]] confirm what the apologies claim about his dealings with prominent Sufi shaykhs, primarily in an admonitory role — so the self-presentation is at least not fabricated at that point. Where an independent source agrees with a coerced document, the coerced document can be believed on that point.

The circumstances of the second recension

The second was written across 834–35/1431–32 at Herat, during the nine months of weekly attendance at court waiting for a review of his case that never came. In the same interval he wrote the Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya for [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]]. Both are documents of a man arguing for himself to a court that was not listening.

A creed composed in the ninth month of that wait is about as far from disinterested theological statement as a text can get, and any use of it should carry the date.

What we do not know

  • How many recensions there are, and how they differ. A second is attested; the first is not dated in the sources in hand.
  • Whether they achieved anything. No response, ruling or acknowledgement is recorded.
  • No edition or translation is discussed in these sources.
