MS Majlis 10196

MS Majlis 10196 — the earliest, most complete and most reliable collection of Ibn Turka's works, copied over the last eight years of his life and three years past his death. The incipit and explicit of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]], ff. 52a and 118b, are in the author's own hand. It is the manuscript on which the dating of the whole oeuvre rests.

The manuscript that dates everything else

What it is. A collection of Ibn Turka's works copied across the last eight years of his life and continuing three years past his death. Melvin-Koushki relies on its colophons wherever possible in establishing when each work was composed, because it is the earliest, most complete and most reliable witness available (The Quest, §2.1).

Why it is unusually good evidence. The incipit and explicit of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] — folios 52a and 118b — are written in Ibn Turka's own hand. A copyist who could hand the volume to its author for the opening and closing lines was in close contact with him at various points, which lends the colophons elsewhere in the manuscript a credibility that a later copy could not have.

The limits, stated honestly. Melvin-Koushki is careful about what the colophons can bear. In many instances it is impossible to tell whether a date records composition or merely transcription. Fully half the manuscript — representing almost two-thirds of the preserved works — was copied between 827–29/1425–27, the period bracketing the exile, so the clustering may reflect a copying campaign rather than a burst of writing. The chronology built on it is a preliminary attempt, and is presented as one.

Why a game and a portal should care. Nearly every date in the [[chronology|chronology]] descends from this codex. When an entry here says a work was finished in 823/1420, the chain of custody for that claim runs back to a colophon in MS Majlis 10196, and the honest description of it is the best available evidence, not the record.

What a colophon can and cannot establish

The codex is the portal's evidentiary foundation, so the limits of that foundation should be stated as carefully as its strengths.

What it establishes well. That a given work existed by a given date, in a form a copyist could transcribe. Where the author's hand appears, that the author saw that page. Where a sequence of colophons runs consecutively, a relative order of transcription.

What it cannot establish. Whether a date records composition or transcription. Melvin-Koushki says so directly: in many instances it is impossible to tell (The Quest, §2.1). This is not a small caveat — it means a substantial part of the chronology of the oeuvre is a chronology of copying, presented as the best available proxy.

The clustering problem. Fully half the manuscript, representing almost two-thirds of the preserved works, was copied between 827–29/1425–27 — the period bracketing the exile. That concentration may reflect a burst of composition; it may equally reflect a copying campaign undertaken because the author's position had become precarious and the works needed securing. The evidence does not decide between those, and the second reading is at least as plausible as the first.

The author's hand

The exception that gives the codex its authority: the incipit and explicit of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]], folios 52a and 118b, are written in Ibn Turka's own hand, which suggests the copyist was in close contact with the author at various points (The Quest, §2.1).

Opening and closing lines are exactly where an author would be asked to sign off. The practical inference is that this copyist had access to Ibn Turka and used it, which raises the credibility of the codex's other colophons without proving any of them individually.

Why the portal depends on it

Nearly every date on the [[chronology|chronology page]] descends from this manuscript. When an entry here says the Mafāḥiṣ was completed in 823/1420, the chain of custody is: this codex's colophon → Melvin-Koushki's analysis of the colophons → the chronology in the addenda → this portal. Each link is sound and the chain is short, but it is a chain, and the honest description of the result is the best available evidence, not the record.

What we do not know

  • Who the copyist was. The sources in hand do not name him.
  • Why this collection was assembled, and at whose expense.
  • What is not in it. With 44 or 45 known works and roughly two-thirds preserved here,

something between a dozen and fifteen works are attested from elsewhere — and the criteria of selection are unknown.

  • Whether other autograph fragments survive elsewhere in the codex beyond the two named

folios.
