Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka

Munshaʾāt-i Ṣāʾin-i Turka — some fifty-eight letters, fragments and notes. The single richest biographical source for Ibn Turka, and the reason we know about his dealings with Timurid amirs, his closeness to [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]], and his friendly relations with Sufis he criticised in print.

primary-sourcelettersbiographypatronagesufism

The collection. Ibn Turka's correspondence survives as fifty-eight letters, letter fragments and notes. They are written in the highly stylized ornate prose typical of period epistolography, and Melvin-Koushki notes that the formulaic quality is itself informative: the formulas encode the addressee's social or political office and spiritual status, which matters because most of these letters lack identifying headers. In a few cases the information is too scant for more than conjecture about who is being written to (The Quest, 135–36).

What the survey shows. Frequent dealings with various Timurid amirs, and a close relationship with [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh]] in particular. Friendly relations with prominent Iranian Sufis — Shāh Niʿmat Allāh Valī, [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], Aḥmad Samarqandī, Ḥasan ʿAṭṭār, Sayyid Muḥammad b. Shaykh Kajujī. Letters written to Sufis and administrative officials alike in hopes of resolving what he calls an intolerable situation.

The tension it exposes. These friendships sit oddly beside the criticisms of organised [[sufism|Sufism]] in his written work. Melvin-Koushki reads the discrepancy as informative rather than hypocritical: the first three of those Sufis were great promoters of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] in the face of often hostile reaction, which is the likely point of attraction. He criticised the institution and kept the company.

A companion collection. The Munshaʾāt-i Yazdī contains eight further items relating to Ibn Turka or his immediate family, and it is there rather than in his own letters that the closeness to [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī]] is adequately visible: attendance at his teacher's majālis, journeys to Herat together, and concern for Ibn Turka's children after their father's death. Only one letter to Yazdī survives in Ibn Turka's own collection, from the first stage of his career in Fars — perhaps the rest were too informal to be thought worth preserving.

Reading letters that have lost their headers

The methodological problem is acute and Melvin-Koushki addresses it head-on. Most of these letters lack identifying headers, so the addressee often has to be reconstructed from the text itself. What makes reconstruction possible is precisely the feature that looks least informative: the letters are written in the highly stylized ornate prose typical in the epistolography of the period, and the formulaic nature of these texts serves to encode contextual information — the addressee's identity, social or political office, and spiritual status — as well as subtextual information (The Quest, 135–36).

Formulae are a register system. Which honorifics a writer selects, how elaborate the opening, what spiritual standing is conceded — these vary with the recipient, and a reader fluent in the conventions can read a social position off the salutation. The ornament is the metadata.

Even so, Melvin-Koushki notes that in a few letters the information is too scant to allow more than conjecture as to the addressee's identity. The corpus is legible but not uniformly so.

What the collection is evidence for

  • Patronage. Frequent dealings with various Timurid amirs, and a close relationship with [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur b. Shāhrukh]] in particular.
  • The Sufi network. Friendly relations with Shāh Niʿmat Allāh Valī, [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], Aḥmad Samarqandī, Ḥasan ʿAṭṭār and Sayyid Muḥammad b. Shaykh Kajujī.
  • Distress. Letters written to Sufis and administrative officials alike in hopes of resolving his intolerable situation — the exile years documented from the inside.

The apparent contradiction, and its resolution

The Sufi friendships sit oddly beside the criticisms of organised [[sufism|Sufism]] Ibn Turka makes in his written work, and beside the admonitory role he claims to have taken with prominent shaykhs in his [[nafsat-al-masdur|apologies]]. Melvin-Koushki reads the tension as corroborative rather than contradictory: the letters confirm the contact the apologies describe, and the first three Sufis named were great promoters of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] in the face of often hostile reaction — which suggests the point of attraction (The Quest, 136).

He criticised the institution and kept the company, and the company was selected on doctrinal grounds.

The Yazdī gap

Only one letter to [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī]] survives here, from the first stage of Ibn Turka's career in Fars — surprising for his closest associate. Melvin-Koushki's suggestion is that the rest were simply too informal to be considered worth preserving.

The relationship is instead documented from the other side. The Munshaʾāt-i Yazdī contains eight items relating to Ibn Turka or his immediate family, showing Yazdī's attendance at his teacher's majālis, his accompanying Ibn Turka on journeys to Herat, and his concern for Ibn Turka's children (makhdūm-zādagān) after their father's death. The closest relationship in the corpus is preserved chiefly in the disciple's archive.

What we do not know

  • How many letters are lost. Fifty-eight survive; the original volume is unknown.
  • The addressees of several letters remain conjectural.
  • No critical edition or translation is treated in the sources in hand.
