Chronology

47 dated events in Ibn Turka’s life and its immediate context, taken from the chronology Melvin-Koushki assembles in the addenda to The Quest for a Universal Science (33–36). He notes there that, given the sparseness of the record, some dates are conjectured or approximate; most rest on the colophons of MS Majlis 10196 and on the two collections of letters, the Munshaʾāt-i Turka and the Munshaʾāt-i Yazdī.

767/1365

A Saturn–Jupiter conjunction in Scorpio

OCCULT · ATTESTED · CIRCA

Ibn Turka holds that his own generation's evolution was heralded by a portentous celestial conjunction — likely Saturn–Jupiter in Scorpio in 767/1365. His generation, poised on the edge of history and encompassing all that went before, is therefore the best generation, and the late 8th/14th century the beginning of a golden age in which the prophetic legacy transmitted by the Imams and the ancients would be actualized. Melvin-Koushki flags his own uncertainty about which conjunction is meant.

Full account

What the claim is. Ibn Turka holds that the evolution of his own generation was heralded by a portentous celestial conjunction. Melvin-Koushki identifies it as likely the Saturn–Jupiter conjunction in Scorpio of 767/1365, while stating in a footnote that he is not entirely sure which conjunction Ibn Turka has in mind (The Quest, 21 n.8). The uncertainty is preserved here because it is the scholar's own.

Why a conjunction. Saturn–Jupiter conjunctions are the backbone of Islamicate historical astrology. They recur roughly every twenty years, shift triplicity every couple of centuries, and complete a full cycle in something under a millennium; the tradition reads the greater ones as marking dynastic and religious turning points. To claim one as the herald of your own generation is to place that generation in a cycle of world-history, not merely to note an omen.

The claim it licenses. From the portent Ibn Turka draws a strong conclusion: his generation, poised on the edge of history and encompassing all that went before, is therefore the best generation, and the late 8th/14th century is the beginning of a golden age in which the prophetic legacy transmitted by the Imams and the ancients would be actualized (The Quest, 21).

Why this is not Ḥurūfism. The structure is millenarian, and Melvin-Koushki grants without hesitation that Ibn Turka is a profoundly millenarian thinker by any standard. What keeps it inside orthodoxy is the object: a golden age of knowledge, in which what the Imams and ancients already transmitted becomes fully actual — not a new revelation. The insistence on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad is what separates this from the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] claim (The Quest, 456).

Consequences. Almost everything programmatic in the career follows from this dating. If the golden age has begun, then the occult knowledge of the ancients is due to be made manifest, and a man who systematises it is not an antiquarian but a participant in a scheduled event. The urgency of the project, and its confidence, are both downstream of a conjunction.

766/1365 · Isfahan

Fażl Allāh Astarābādī in Isfahan

CONTEXT · ATTESTED · CIRCA

The future founder of the Ḥurūfiyya arrives in Isfahan and becomes famous as an interpreter of dreams. Ibn Turka's father and elder brother both consult him — a documented family contact that prosecutors would still be able to use sixty years later.

Full account

What happened. Fażl Allāh Astarābādī arrived in Isfahan around 766/1365 and quickly gained fame as an interpreter of dreams. He would later found the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfiyya]] and be executed for it in 796/1394.

The awkward fact. Ḥurūfī sources record that Ibn Turka's father Afżal al-Dīn and his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] both sought Fażl Allāh out during this Isfahan residence to have their dreams interpreted. Naṣr Allāh Nāfajī's Khwābnāma preserves the episode: Khwāja Ṣadr al-Dīn Turka attended an afternoon majlis of Fażl Allāh's and presented a dream in which he saw the Prophet seated, went up to kiss his hand, and then noticed he had inadvertently left saliva on the back of his own hand and felt ashamed (The Quest, 58 n.16).

What it does and does not show. Melvin-Koushki is careful in both directions. The contact does not imply Ḥurūfī inclinations on the part of the two Turkas; the hagiographical point of preserving such stories is to establish Fażl Allāh's fame as a Sufi before his messianic transformation, such that respected scholars of the religious sciences sought his counsel. In 766/1365 consulting him was unremarkable.

Why it nonetheless mattered. Because it is on the record, in the movement's own sources, and it was still on the record sixty years later when Ibn Turka's opponents needed a charge that would carry at [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court. A documented family connection to the founder of a proscribed movement is exactly the kind of material a prosecutor can use without having to prove anything about doctrine.

What we do not know. Whether Ibn Turka's opponents drew on the Ḥurūfī sources directly, on local memory, or on something else entirely.

770/1369 · Isfahan

Ibn Turka born in Isfahan

LIFE · ATTESTED · YEAR

Ṣāʾin al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Turka Iṣfahānī is born into an established Isfahani scholarly family. His father Afżal al-Dīn and elder brother Ṣadr al-Dīn are both men of the religious sciences; the brother will be appointed qāḍī and will teach him for his first twenty-five years.

Full account

The family. Ibn Turka was born into an established Isfahani scholarly house, not into obscurity. His father Afżal al-Dīn Turka is a figure of sufficient standing to have an Encyclopaedia of Islam article of his own (Eichner, 'Afḍal al-Dīn Turka', EI3). His elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] was an eminent scholar of hadith, Qur'anic exegesis, jurisprudence and uṣūl al-dīn, and was appointed qāḍī.

The office waiting for him. The most consequential inheritance was institutional. Tīmūr appointed Ṣadr al-Dīn qāḍī in Samarkand, and in doing so established the judgeship to the Turka family (The Quest, §3.1). The post Ibn Turka would later hold in Isfahan, and lose in the tribunals, was a family possession dating to the founding of the dynasty. He was born into a claim.

What the date sits next to. The conventional dates are 770–835/1369–1432. Two years before, around 766–67/1365, Fażl Allāh Astarābādī had arrived in Isfahan and become famous as an interpreter of dreams — and Ibn Turka would later hold that a celestial conjunction of that same moment heralded his generation as the best in history. The two facts sit oddly together: the portent he claimed and the heresy he was accused of belong to the same year and the same city.

What we do not know. The month and day are not recorded. His mother is not named. Of the household's circumstances beyond its scholarly standing the sources say nothing.

ibn turka sadr al din turka

770–796/1369–1394 · Isfahan

Studies under his brother, with Qāḍīzāda Rūmī

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

For the first twenty-five years of his life Ibn Turka studies under his elder brother Ṣadr al-Dīn — alongside Qāḍīzāda Rūmī, the future director of the Samarkand Observatory. He later remarks that his brother taught nothing beyond the outward sciences.

Full account

What happened. Ibn Turka studied under his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] for the first twenty-five years of his life, and so — the detail that reorganises everything — did [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]], the future second director of Ulugh Beg's observatory (The Quest, 41 and n.17).

Why it matters. The [[isfahan-circle|Isfahan Circle]] is usually described as an intellectual network that formed at Timurid courts. This shows that its two principal minds had been in the same household classroom for a quarter of a century first. The lettrist and the mathematical astronomer are not specialists who converged; they are schoolfellows who divided an inheritance.

What was and was not taught. Ibn Turka's own verdict is that his brother taught nothing beyond the outward meaning of the sharīʿa, deeming it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public (The Quest, 41). The reason is not ignorance but assessment of risk — and the tribunals of the 1420s suggest the assessment was correct.

The shape this gives the life. A complete grounding in the exoteric disciplines, deliberately stopping short of the bāṭin, delivered by a brother who thought the bāṭin was dangerous to teach openly. Everything Ibn Turka built afterwards can be read as the supply of what that education withheld — and as a demonstration that his brother's caution was warranted.

What we do not know. The curriculum's contents beyond the named disciplines; whether other students shared it; and whether Ṣadr al-Dīn's caution extended to private teaching.

771–807/1370–1405 · Samarkand / Isfahan

The judgeship of Isfahan becomes a Turka family office

INSTITUTION · ATTESTED · RANGE

Tīmūr appoints Ibn Turka's elder brother Ṣadr al-Dīn as qāḍī in Samarkand, establishing the office to the Turka family. The judgeship of Isfahan that Iskandar later installs Ibn Turka in — and that the tribunals take from him — is a hereditary family possession dating to the founding of the dynasty.

Full account

What happened. Tīmūr appointed Ibn Turka's elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] as qāḍī in Samarkand, and in doing so established the office to the Turka family. When [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] later installed Ibn Turka as qāḍī of Isfahan, he was presumably filling a post the family already held by right (The Quest, §3.1).

Why this reframes the career. It changes what the tribunals were taking. Read without this fact, Ibn Turka is a scholar who obtained a good appointment and later lost it through controversy. Read with it, he is the holder of a hereditary family office granted at the founding of the dynasty, and the accusations of the 1420s are an attempt to strip a lineage of a possession, not merely to remove an individual from a job.

The precise office. Melvin-Koushki describes him as Shāfiʿī chief judge of Isfahan and Yazd (Of Islamic Grammatology, 13) — a senior magistracy, in a named legal school, across two cities. This is a full working career in its own right. Everything in the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] was written around it.

What it explains about the opposition. Rival colleagues engineered the inquisitions, and a hereditary judgeship is exactly the kind of thing colleagues covet. The chronology notes that he had already moved back to Shiraz once, in 815–17/1412–14, due to harassment by envious individuals, putting forward his protégé Sharaf al-Dīn Yaʿqūb to replace him as qāḍī. The pattern of professional envy is attested a decade before the tribunals.

What we do not know. Whether the Isfahan judgeship was formally the same grant as the Samarkand one, or a family claim honoured by a later prince, is not spelled out in the sources in hand.

771–807/1370–1405

The reign of Tīmūr

POLITICS · ATTESTED · RANGE

Tīmūr rules the empire into which Ibn Turka is born and which will employ, elevate and eventually prosecute him. It is Tīmūr who appoints his brother Ṣadr al-Dīn qāḍī in Samarkand, establishing the judgeship as a Turka family office.

Full account

The frame. Tīmūr ruled 771–807/1370–1405 (Powers of One, 34), and the empire he built — the Timurid, 771–912/1370–1507 — is the whole political container of Ibn Turka's life.

The specific debt. Tīmūr appointed [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] qāḍī in Samarkand and thereby established the judgeship as a Turka family office (The Quest, §3.1). The post Ibn Turka held in Isfahan and lost in the tribunals descends from a personal act of the founder.

The instability it bequeathed. Tīmūr's death at Utrar in 807/1405 opened a succession struggle that structured everything after: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] against [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]], the Timurid princes of western Iran competing for prestige and scholars. That competition is why a returning occult philosopher found a receptive market in 810/1408, and why his patron was destroyed six years later.

What we do not know. Whether the Turka family had standing before Tīmūr's appointment, or whether it made them.

784–799/1382–1397 · Cairo

Akhlāṭī as heir of al-Būnī and Ibn ʿArabī

NETWORK · ATTESTED · RANGE

Melvin-Koushki characterises Akhlāṭī as the conscious heir of both al-Būnī and Ibn ʿArabī, and master to the most important occult philosophers of the following generation. The two streams the portal treats separately — operative talismanic lettrism and Akbarian metaphysics — are joined in one teacher before Ibn Turka inherits them.

Full account

The characterisation. Melvin-Koushki calls [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] the conscious heir of both al-Būnī and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]], and master to the most important occult philosophers of the following generation (Powers of One, 6).

Why "both" is the point. These are two different inheritances that do not obviously combine. From al-Būnī comes the operative talismanic literature — the Shams al-Maʿārif tradition, magic squares, divine names deployed for effect, procedures with materials and timings. From Ibn ʿArabī comes a metaphysics: the [[divine-names|divine names]] as the structure of manifestation, the [[imaginal-realm|imaginal realm]], an orientation that is contemplative rather than operative.

One is a manual; the other is an ontology. Holding both consciously — as a single teaching rather than as two competencies — is the achievement, and it is what Ibn Turka inherited as a going concern.

What it let his student skip. Because the manual and the metaphysics arrived already joined, Ibn Turka did not have to spend his career reconciling them. He could take the combination as given and spend the effort on something neither predecessor had attempted: arguing the result into the [[pythagorean-cosmology|mathematical sciences]].

The periodisation this sits in. Imperial Talismanic Love gives the three stages: thirteenth-century Ibn ʿArabian-Būnian science of the saints; fourteenth-century de-esotericization and adabization through the Mamluk mainstream; and neopythagoreanization at the turn of the fifteenth by the occultists who would form the [[new-brethren-purity|Brethren]]. Akhlāṭī stands at the hinge between the second and third.

What we do not know. Whether Akhlāṭī himself argued for the combination in writing, or simply taught it.

784–801/1382–1399 · Cairo

The reign of Sultan Barqūq at Cairo

POLITICS · ATTESTED · RANGE

Al-Malik al-Ẓāhir Barqūq rules the Mamluk sultanate. Akhlāṭī serves as his personal physician-alchemist, and under that influence Barqūq develops a keen interest in the occult sciences — evidenced by his personal library and his choice of courtiers, Ibn Turka among them. Melvin-Koushki asks whether he was the first Mamluk sultan to aspire to occult-philosopher-kingship, and answers: likely so.

Full account

The reign. Al-Malik al-Ẓāhir Barqūq ruled the Mamluk sultanate 784–801/1382–1399 (Powers of One, 6) — the sultan under whom Ibn Turka spent his first Cairo years.

The physician at the centre. [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] was summoned to Cairo as Barqūq's personal physician-alchemist. A court physician has repeated private access to the ruler's body, and that access is the mechanism behind what follows: under his influence Barqūq seems to have developed a keen interest in the occult sciences (Imperial Talismanic Love, 70).

The evidence, which is good. Not testimony about the sultan's opinions but two things a historian can check: his personal library and his choice of courtiers — among whom was Ibn Turka himself. Ibn Turka was not merely a student in Cairo; he was a courtier of Barqūq's, at the beginning of his career and in his twenties.

The question, and its answer. Melvin-Koushki asks whether Barqūq was the first Mamluk sultan to aspire to occult-philosopher-kingship, and so an inspiration for Timurid rivals, and answers: likely so. If that holds, the model of the occult court that [[isfahan-circle|Isfahan]] would develop has a Cairene prototype, and Ibn Turka saw it working before he ever served a Timurid.

What we do not know. What was in the library; whether Barqūq's interest survived his physician's death in 799/1397; and how far the aspiration was realised in policy.

785–808/1383–1406 · Cairo

Ibn Khaldūn settles in Cairo

CONTEXT · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Khaldūn lives in Cairo from 785/1383 until his death in 808/1406 — overlapping the years Ibn Turka and Yazdī spent there. Melvin-Koushki notes that the two historians possibly met: Yazdī spent at least part of 795–810/1393–1408 in the city with Ibn Turka. The most formidable critic of the occult sciences and their most consequential defender may have been in the same rooms.

Full account

What happened. Ibn Khaldūn lived in Cairo from 785/1383 until his death in 808/1406 — overlapping almost the whole of Ibn Turka's fifteen-year residence, and Yazdī's.

The possible meeting. Melvin-Koushki notes that the two historians possibly met: Yazdī spent at least part of 795–810/1393–1408 in the city with Ibn Turka (Powers of One, 37). The claim is carefully limited — possibly — and the portal keeps it that way.

Why it would matter. Ibn Khaldūn belongs to the [[neoplatonism|anti-occultist coalition]] whose four centuries of polemic the whole mathematicalization project was built to answer, alongside Ibn Sīnā, [[al-ghazali|Ghazālī]] and Ibn Taymiyya (Powers of One, 1). And [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī]] would go on to write in defence of divination against precisely that critique — the subject of the portal's intersection essay on geomancy.

If the defence was written by a man who had argued with Ibn Khaldūn in person, it reads differently: not a scholar answering a book but a scholar answering someone he had met.

The complication. Melvin-Koushki also notes that Ibn Khaldūn attempted, at his Damascus interviews with Tīmūr in 803/1401, to palm himself off as a seasoned occultist. The period's most rigorous critic of the occult sciences was willing to claim competence in them in front of a conqueror.

What we do not know. Whether any meeting occurred. No source in hand records one; the inference rests on overlapping residence in a shared scholarly world.

sharaf al din yazdi ibn turka

795–810/1393–1408 · Cairo

Fifteen years abroad: study under Akhlāṭī in Cairo

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Turka studies under Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī, Sirāj al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī and others, remaining abroad fifteen years. Akhlāṭī — summoned to Cairo as personal physician to Sultan Barqūq — is the pivot of the network that will call itself the New Brethren of Purity.

Full account

What happened. From roughly 795/1393 to 810/1408 — fifteen years — Ibn Turka studied in Mamluk Cairo under Sayyid Ḥusayn [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]], Sirāj al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī and others, remaining abroad the whole time (The Quest, chronology).

What he trained in. Melvin-Koushki lists three fields: law, Arabic letters and occult science — and adds that he launched his writing career there, and that his prose from this period is masterfully prosimetric (Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Sensorium, 5). The combination is the whole later career in miniature: the jurist, the stylist and the occultist are formed together, not sequentially.

Bulqīnī matters as much as Akhlāṭī. Sirāj al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī was a mainstream Shāfiʿī authority of the first rank. Studying under him is what makes Ibn Turka a credentialed jurist — and it is why he could later hold the chief judgeship of two cities and argue his own case in front of a ruler. The occult philosophy was written by a man with impeccable orthodox training, and that is not incidental to how he defended it.

What Cairo was at the time. The largest city in the Islamic world and the centre of its scholarly life, under a sultan — [[chronology|Barqūq]] — whose personal physician-alchemist was Ibn Turka's own master, and whose choice of courtiers included Ibn Turka himself. The [[new-brethren-purity|network]] that would call itself the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ was forming in these rooms.

The cost. The same years produced mortal enemies at court. The opposition that culminated in the tribunals of the 1420s begins in Egypt, decades before it bore fruit.

What we do not know. What he wrote before 806/1404; how he supported himself; and the identity of the Cairo enemies.

795/1393 · Samarkand → Cairo

Leaves Samarkand for Mecca and Cairo, with Yazdī

LIFE · ATTESTED · CIRCA

Ibn Turka departs for the Ḥijāz and then Cairo. Sharaf al-Dīn Yazdī accompanies him — the disciple is with him from the beginning of the formative journey, not acquired later at court.

Full account

What happened. Around 795/1393 Ibn Turka left Samarkand for Mecca and then Cairo. [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Dīn Yazdī]] accompanied him (The Quest, chronology).

Why the companion matters. Yazdī is usually introduced as a member of the Isfahan Circle, which makes him sound like a colleague acquired at the height of Ibn Turka's career. He was there at the start. The fifteen formative years under [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] were shared, and Melvin-Koushki notes that Yazdī spent at least part of 795–810/1393–1408 in Cairo with him (Powers of One, 37). The relationship the dissertation calls disciple and closest associate runs the whole length of the adult life.

Why Samarkand, why leave. The family had standing there — Tīmūr had appointed his brother qāḍī in the city. Departing for the Ḥijāz and Egypt at around twenty-four means leaving a secure position for a long absence, and the chronology gives no stated reason. The ḥajj is the conventional occasion; fifteen years abroad is not conventional.

What we do not know. Whether the journey was planned as a long stay or extended once begun; who funded it; and whether the choice of Cairo — rather than Damascus or Baghdad — was made in advance or arrived at.

ibn turka sharaf al din yazdi

795–810/1393–1408 · Cairo

Makes mortal enemies at the Cairo court

NETWORK · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Turka launches his writing career during the fifteen-year Cairo tenure, where he trains in law, Arabic letters and occult science — and makes mortal enemies at court. The opposition that culminates in the tribunals of the 1420s begins in Egypt, decades before it bears fruit.

Full account

What happened. During the fifteen-year Cairo tenure Ibn Turka made mortal enemies at court (Ibn Turka's Pythagorean Sensorium, 5).

Why it belongs on a chronology. Because it relocates the origin of the tribunals. The inquisitions of the 1420s are easy to read as a late-career collision between an occultist and a pious Timurid establishment — a story about Iran, [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] and the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] scare. This entry says the enmity is older than that and began somewhere else, in a different sultanate, before he had written the works he would be tried for.

The pattern it establishes. The chronology records the same dynamic again in Iran: between 815 and 817/1412–14 he moved back to Shiraz due to harassment by envious individuals, putting his protégé forward to replace him as qāḍī. Twice, in two countries, the record uses the language of personal rivalry rather than doctrinal objection.

What this suggests about the charges. That the accusations were instruments rather than motives. Colleagues who wanted a hereditary judgeship reached for the vocabulary the court would respond to — Sufi bias in 1422, a Ḥurūfī connection in 1427 — because those were the charges available, not because they described what was actually at stake.

What we do not know. Who they were. Only one opponent is named anywhere in the chronology: Muḥammad al-Jazarī, who died at Herat in 833/1429 and is described as powerful — and he belongs to the Iranian phase, not the Egyptian one.

796/1394 · Alinjaq

Fażl Allāh Astarābādī executed

CONTEXT · ATTESTED · YEAR

The founder of the Ḥurūfiyya is executed at the fortress of Alinjaq by Mīrānshāh b. Tīmūr. The movement he leaves behind becomes the heresy from which Ibn Turka's own science must afterwards be distinguished.

Full account

What happened. Fażl Allāh Astarābādī was executed at the fortress of Alinjaq by Mīrānshāh b. Tīmūr in 796/1394 (The Quest, chronology).

Who killed him. The detail worth registering is the executioner's house. Mīrānshāh was Tīmūr's son. The dynasty under which Ibn Turka would spend his entire career had killed the founder of the [[hurufism|Ḥurūfiyya]] within Ibn Turka's own lifetime, when he was twenty-five and already abroad.

What it made the charge worth. When Ibn Turka was exiled in 1427 on a putative Ḥurūfī connection, the label did not name an abstract theological error. It named the movement whose founder the ruling family had personally executed, and which would rise in armed revolt at Isfahan and Tabriz in 835/1432. The accusation carried the weight of a live security question.

The distinction that had to be held. Ibn Turka's own position is millenarian — he proclaims a new age heralded by heavenly portent — but it is a golden age of knowledge in which the legacy transmitted by the Imams and the ancients becomes actual, insisting on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad. The Ḥurūfīs proclaimed a new dispensation. The difference is real and it is fine, and a court that will not attend to it convicts both.

What we do not know. The formal grounds of Fażl Allāh's execution, and whether Ibn Turka ever wrote about him directly.

799/1397 · Cairo

Akhlāṭī dies in Cairo

NETWORK · ATTESTED · YEAR

The master of the Cairo circle dies. Ibn Turka will present himself thereafter as the systematizer of Akhlāṭī's teachings, and was received as such by the later lettrist tradition.

Full account

What happened. Sayyid Ḥusayn [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] died in Cairo in 799/1397 (The Quest, chronology). Ibn Turka had been studying under him for roughly four years and would remain abroad another eleven.

What was lost and what was inherited. Akhlāṭī was the pivot of the network that would call itself the [[new-brethren-purity|Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ]], and had proclaimed a new era of human development through the retrieval and open promulgation of the occult sciences (The Quest, 2). He was also personal physician-alchemist to Sultan Barqūq, and the conscious heir of both al-Būnī and [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] (Powers of One, 6) — the operative and metaphysical traditions held together in one teaching.

The succession. Two men inherited it in opposite directions. Ibn Turka took it east; [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī]] took it into the Ottoman lands. They were both the intellectual heirs of Akhlāṭī, and it is unclear whether they ever met during their overlapping Cairo residences (The Quest, 2). Melvin-Koushki judges the Ottoman outcome the more successful.

How Ibn Turka positioned himself afterwards. As simply the systematizer of Akhlāṭī's teachings — and he was received as such by the later lettrist tradition (The Quest, 2). The modesty is partly genuine debt and partly the prudence of a man in a dangerous field, for whom a chain of transmission is a defence. It also understates the case: Akhlāṭī does not appear to have argued lettrism into the [[pythagorean-cosmology|mathematical sciences]].

What we do not know. The circumstances of the death; whether Barqūq's interest in the occult sciences survived his physician; and almost anything of Akhlāṭī's own writings.

sayyid husayn akhlati ibn turka

1400–1432 · Isfahan / Yazd

Ten children; two sons known by name

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Turka states that he had ten children — three sons and seven daughters. Only two sons are known by name: Jamāl al-Dīn Muḥammad, for whom he wrote his manual of logic the K. al-Manāhij, and Najīb al-Dīn Ḥabīb Allāh, who appears to have written a brief lettrist treatise of his own, the Qavāʿid-i Jafr-i Jāmiʿ. The science stayed in the family for at least one generation.

Full account

What the sources say. Ibn Turka states that he had ten children — three sons and seven daughters (The Quest, 41).

The two named sons. Only two are known by name, and both are known through texts. Jamāl al-Dīn Muḥammad, for whom he wrote his manual of logic the Kitāb al-Manāhij while [[natanz-winter|wintering at Natanz]] in exile in 833/1430; and Najīb al-Dīn Ḥabīb Allāh, who appears to have written a brief lettrist treatise of his own, the Qavāʿid-i Jafr-i Jāmiʿ.

Why the second matters. Because it means the science stayed in the family for at least one further generation. Jafr is the divinatory-lettrist discipline; a son writing on it, after his father had been tried three times and exiled once, is a small piece of evidence about how the household understood what had happened to it. Nothing further is known about Najīb al-Dīn.

The eight who are not named. Seven daughters and a third son leave no trace in these sources. This is normal for the period and worth stating anyway, because a chronology that records only the named risks implying the rest did not exist.

What the family cost him, and vice versa. [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī's]] letters show concern for Ibn Turka's children (makhdūm-zādagān) after their father's death — the detail that establishes the character of that friendship. And the exile years include sending for the family to Natanz: the case that consumed his last five years was being pursued by a man with a household to move.

What we do not know. The names of eight children; whether Jamāl al-Dīn continued the work — the dissertation says he may have been a key player in something the passage in hand does not complete; and anything of his wife or wives.

803/1401 · Damascus

Ibn Khaldūn's Damascus interviews with Tīmūr

CONTEXT · ATTESTED · YEAR

At the famous Damascus interviews, Ibn Khaldūn — the age's leading anti-occultist — attempts, in Melvin-Koushki's reading, to palm himself off as a seasoned occultist. The hypocrisy is instructive about how much occult competence was worth in front of a conqueror.

Full account

What happened. At the famous Damascus interviews of 803/1401, Ibn Khaldūn attempted — in Melvin-Koushki's reading — to palm himself off as a seasoned occultist, and did so with some hypocrisy, given his own disallowing of occultism elsewhere (In Defense of Geomancy, 36).

Why it is worth a chronology entry. Because it establishes the market value of occult competence at the top of the political world. Ibn Khaldūn is the most formidable critic the occult sciences had — a member of the [[neoplatonism|anti-occultist coalition]] with Ibn Sīnā, [[al-ghazali|Ghazālī]] and Ibn Taymiyya. In front of a conqueror he claimed to be one of the practitioners he elsewhere dismissed.

What it implies about the tribunals. If occult competence was worth claiming falsely before Tīmūr in 1401, then the accusations levelled at Ibn Turka two decades later were not straightforward expressions of a settled hostility. The same courts that prosecuted occultists also employed them, and the difference between the two treatments has more to do with faction than with doctrine.

The connection to Yazdī. [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Yazdī]] would write in defence of divination against precisely Ibn Khaldūn's critique — the subject of the portal's intersection essay on geomancy. Melvin-Koushki also notes the two historians possibly met in Cairo. The defence may have been aimed at a man its author had encountered.

What we do not know. What Ibn Khaldūn actually said at Damascus about the occult sciences; the accounts in hand are Melvin-Koushki's characterisation rather than a quoted transcript.

806/1404 · Cairo

Completes the Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr

TEXTS · ATTESTED · YEAR

His first major work, in Persian: a commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's al-Tāʾiyya al-kubrā, a favourite teaching text of the Ibn ʿArabī school. It timuridizes the Mamluk anthology-as-commentary genre and is the first such commentary to re-poeticize that tradition.

Full account

What happened. Ibn Turka completed the Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr in 806/1404, toward the end of his Cairo residence — his first major work (Imperial Talismanic Love, 70).

What it is. An ornate Persian commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's (d. 1235) al-Tāʾiyya al-kubrā, a favourite teaching text of the [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] school. Formally a commentary; in practice an anthology, since it answers each line of the poem with several further lines of Persian or Arabic verse, contemporary and classical.

Why it matters more than a first book usually does. Three claims are made for it. It closely tracks high Mamluk Arabic literary culture; it timuridizes the new hybrid Mamluk anthology-as-commentary genre pioneered by Ibn Nubāta (d. 768/1366) and his student al-Ṣafadī (d. 764/1363); and it is the first such commentary to effectively re-poeticize that tradition (Imperial Talismanic Love, 70; Of Islamic Grammatology, 38).

The direction of travel. The Akbarian school had spent two centuries turning a visionary inheritance into scholastic philosophy. This book runs the other way, returning the material to verse. That is not a detour from the later lettrism: if [[ilm-al-huruf|letters are ontological entities]], their arrangement is participation rather than decoration, and a book whose method is the conjunction of verses is a book about how arrangements of letters answer to one another.

Its role in modern scholarship. It is the base text for Akram Jūdī-Niʿmatī's edition, whose introduction supplies the list of works from which Melvin-Koushki builds the annotated catalogue in §2.1. The bibliography of the whole oeuvre rests partly on this book's edition.

What we do not know. For whom it was written, and how it was received in the Cairo literary culture whose genre it adapted.

807/1405 · Utrar

Tīmūr dies at Utrar

POLITICS · ATTESTED · YEAR

The founder of the dynasty dies on campaign, opening the succession struggle out of which Shāhrukh will emerge and within which Ibn Turka's patrons will rise and fall.

Full account

What happened. Tīmūr died at Utrar in 807/1405 while campaigning (The Quest, chronology). Ibn Turka was in Cairo, three years from returning home.

What it opened. A succession contest among sons and grandsons that would not resolve for years. The competing courts of the Timurid princes of western Iran — Pīr-Muḥammad at Shiraz, then [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]] in Fars and Isfahan — are products of it, and they are the courts that would employ Ibn Turka within five years.

The pairing worth noticing. Ibn Khaldūn died in Cairo the following year, 808/1406. The two men had met at Damascus in 803/1401, where — Melvin-Koushki notes — Ibn Khaldūn attempted to palm himself off to Tīmūr as a seasoned occultist. Within twelve months the conqueror and his most famous interviewer were both dead, and Ibn Turka was still in the city where the second of them had lived.

What we do not know. Whether news of the death shaped Ibn Turka's decision to return to Iran c. 810/1408.

810–812/1408–1409 · Shiraz

At Pīr-Muḥammad's court in Shiraz

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

A first Timurid attachment, to the court of Pīr-Muḥammad b. ʿUmar-Shaykh — cut short when Pīr-Muḥammad is murdered in 812/1409 and his brother Iskandar rises to power in Fars.

Full account

What happened. Around 810–12/1408–9 Ibn Turka was a member of the court of Pīr-Muḥammad b. ʿUmar-Shaykh at Shiraz. In 812/1409 Pīr-Muḥammad was murdered, and his brother [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]] rose to power in Fars (The Quest, chronology, §3.1).

The first patron, and the first loss. This is the forgotten entry in the patronage sequence. The story is usually told as Iskandar → Bāysunghur → the unreached Ulugh Beg; Pīr-Muḥammad comes before all of them, and his court lasted Ibn Turka perhaps a year before its holder was killed.

The pattern it starts. Every Timurid patron in this life ends badly or ends inaccessible. Pīr-Muḥammad murdered in 1409. Iskandar defeated, blinded and executed in 1414–15. [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]] dead in 1433, a year after Ibn Turka. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] never quite reached, then murdered in 1449. Attaching yourself to a Timurid prince in this generation was a structurally unsafe career move, and Ibn Turka did it four times.

The continuity. Iskandar was Pīr-Muḥammad's brother, and Ibn Turka moved from the one court to the other. The transition was lateral within a family — which is presumably how a scholar attached to a murdered prince avoided sharing his fate.

What we do not know. What Ibn Turka did at this court; who murdered Pīr-Muḥammad and why; and whether the move to Iskandar was immediate.

810/1408 · Isfahan

Returns to Isfahan and takes up teaching

LIFE · ATTESTED · CIRCA

After fifteen years abroad Ibn Turka comes home and begins to teach. The Cairo formation is complete; the Persian career begins.

Full account

What happened. Around 810/1408, after fifteen years abroad, Ibn Turka returned to Isfahan and took up teaching (The Quest, chronology).

What he came back with. A Shāfiʿī legal training under Sirāj al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī, a decade under [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]] in the occult sciences, a completed major work in the [[sharh-nazm-al-durr|Sharḥ-i Naẓm al-Durr]] (806/1404), a mastery of Mamluk literary form, membership in a transregional [[new-brethren-purity|network]], and a set of mortal enemies.

What he came back to. A political landscape being reorganised. Tīmūr had died at Utrar in 807/1405 and the succession was unresolved; the Timurid princes of western Iran were building competing courts. Melvin-Koushki's judgement is that Ibn Turka found a receptive audience for his occult philosophy at the courts of the Timurid princes of western Iran (The Quest, §3.1) — a seller's market for exactly what he had.

Why the timing was fortunate and then wasn't. The fragmentation that made princes compete for prestigious scholars is the same fragmentation that would destroy [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar]] in 817/1414 and leave Ibn Turka attached to a defeated house. The conditions that made the career possible are the conditions that ended it.

What we do not know. Where he taught, and to whom, in these first years back.

812–815/1409–1412 · Isfahan

Appointed qāḍī at Iskandar Sulṭān's court

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Turka joins the court of Iskandar Sulṭān, appointed qāḍī and teacher. Iskandar commissions Of Letters and, shortly after, the Book of Inquiries — the patronage that produces his central work.

Full account

What happened. From 812–15/1409–12 Ibn Turka was a member of [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān's]] court in Isfahan, appointed qāḍī and teacher (The Quest, chronology).

The office was his family's. Melvin-Koushki judges that Iskandar was presumably responsible for installing him in his hereditary office as qāḍī of Isfahan — a post established to the Turka family by Tīmūr himself when he appointed [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] qāḍī in Samarkand (The Quest, §3.1). The appointment was a restoration as much as a favour.

Why this patron in particular. Iskandar was known for his imamophilism and esotericist bent, is accounted the leading Timurid patron of culture in the early Shahrukhid period, and showed a particular interest in alchemy and astrological and astronomical illustrated works (The Quest, §3.1 and n.1). He was not a generic prince who happened to employ an occultist; he was looking for one. [[imamophilism|Imamophilism]] is precisely the confessional position the portal ascribes to Ibn Turka too.

What the patronage produced. It is possible that Iskandar personally commissioned the [[risalat-huruf|R. Ḥurūf]], Ibn Turka's first treatise on lettrism; and the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was begun shortly afterwards, again almost certainly at his instance. The two central lettrist works of the century have one patron behind them.

What we do not know. Whether the commission is documented or inferred — Melvin-Koushki writes "it is possible that" and "almost certainly", not that a record survives.

812–817/1409–1414 · Fars / Isfahan

Iskandar Sulṭān rules Fars

POLITICS · ATTESTED · RANGE

Iskandar Sulṭān — grandson of Tīmūr, ruler of Fars and contender with his uncle Shāhrukh for control of Iran — is described by Melvin-Koushki as Ibn Turka's first and most important patron.

Full account

The reign. [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] ruled Fars 812–817/1409–1414, grandson of Tīmūr and contender with his uncle [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] for control of Iran. Melvin-Koushki calls him Ibn Turka's first and most important patron (Powers of One, 34).

What kind of court it was. Accounted the leading Timurid patron of culture in the early Shahrukhid period, with a particular interest in alchemy and astrological and astronomical illustrated works, and known for his imamophilism and esotericist bent (The Quest, §3.1 n.1). Brilliant and short-lived, in the chronology's phrase.

Why "most important" is right. Iskandar installed Ibn Turka in his hereditary judgeship, possibly commissioned the [[risalat-huruf|R. Ḥurūf]], and was almost certainly behind the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]]. The two central lettrist works of the century have one patron behind them — a patron who was blinded before the second was finished.

The larger claim. Melvin-Koushki argues that the model established by Iskandar Sulṭān and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] is what ensured astronomy-astrology and lettrism would continue to be heavily patronised by later Timurid, Indo-Timurid, Safavid and Ottoman courts (Philosopher-Kings, 599). Ibn Turka's first patron and his would-be last are the two poles of a model that outlived them both.

What we do not know. Whether Iskandar read the works he commissioned.

1409–1432 · Isfahan / Yazd

Shāfiʿī chief judge of Isfahan and Yazd

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

Ibn Turka's office is described precisely by Melvin-Koushki as Shāfiʿī chief judge of Isfahan and Yazd — a working senior magistracy in two cities, in a named legal school. The occult philosophy was written by a man with a full-time judicial day job.

Full account

What the office was. Melvin-Koushki's description is exact: Shāfiʿī chief judge of Isfahan and Yazd (Of Islamic Grammatology, 13). Three things are specified — the legal school, the rank, and the fact that it covered two cities.

Why the precision matters. Shāfiʿī places him in a named madhhab and settles that his legal training was mainstream; he studied under Sirāj al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī, a Shāfiʿī authority of the first rank, in Cairo. Chief judge is a senior appointment with jurisdiction and staff, not an honorific. Isfahan and Yazd means the workload was doubled, and that Yazd was added by [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] in 825/1422 as the reward for surviving the first inquisition.

The bearing on how the work was written. Everything in the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Mafāḥiṣ]] was produced around a full-time senior magistracy. This is not the leisure of a court philosopher; it is the second job of a working judge who heard cases, supervised subordinates and administered a jurisdiction. The career sim's obligation mechanic — an office that taxes every turn — is the accurate model.

The vulnerability it created. A hereditary chief judgeship in two prosperous cities is exactly the sort of prize that generates the envious individuals the chronology blames for driving him out of Isfahan in 815–17/1412–14. The office that made the intellectual work possible is also what made him worth attacking.

What we do not know. The dates of appointment to each city (Yazd is fixed at c. 825/1422; Isfahan is not precisely dated); whether he held both simultaneously; and when he lost them.

811–850/1409–1447 · Herat

The reign of Shāhrukh

POLITICS · ATTESTED · RANGE

Shāhrukh rules for thirty-eight years, spanning almost the whole of Ibn Turka's Persian career — the patron who received him back into favour in 825/1422 and the court that exiled him in 1427.

Full account

The reign. [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] ruled 811–850/1409–1447 (Powers of One, 34) — thirty- eight years, spanning almost the whole of Ibn Turka's Iranian career and outlasting him by fifteen.

Three turns and a silence. He destroyed [[iskandar-falls|Iskandar]] in 817/1414; [[first-inquisition|vindicated Ibn Turka and gave him the judgeship of Yazd]] around 825/1422; [[exiled-from-herat|exiled him]] in 1427; and thereafter would not see him — a refused hearing at Simnan in 833/1429, a [[promise-of-reinstatement|promise at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa]] around 834/1430, and [[nine-months-waiting|nine months of unanswered weekly attendance]] at Herat.

Not a hostile reign, exactly. He completed a madrasa-khānaqāh complex at Herat in 813/1410 and appointed its teachers. His sons ran the century's two greatest cultural enterprises: [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur's]] kitābkhāna and [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg's]] [[samarkand-observatory|observatory]]. This is not a court indifferent to learning. It is a court hostile to a particular claim about where authority over knowledge lies.

The mechanism. By refounding legitimacy on the sharīʿa he elevated the class of men who interpret it — and gave professional rivals a vocabulary in which envy could be prosecuted as heresy. The tribunals are the predictable output of that arrangement rather than an expression of the ruler's own animus, and the 825/1422 vindication suggests Shāhrukh himself was persuadable.

What we do not know. Whether he personally decided any of the three turns, or ratified factional outcomes.

813/1411

Completes the Sharḥ Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam

TEXTS · ATTESTED · YEAR

A commentary on Ibn ʿArabī's Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam, completed in the year he travels to the Ḥijāz and invites Sharaf al-Dīn to join him. A corrected version follows in 817/1415.

Full account

What happened. In 813/1411 Ibn Turka completed the Sharḥ Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam, a commentary on [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī's]] Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam, in the same year he travelled to the Ḥijāz — possibly on pilgrimage — and invited [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Dīn]] to join him. He completed a corrected version in 817/1415 (The Quest, chronology).

What it establishes. That Ibn Turka was a working Akbarian commentator, not merely an inheritor of Akbarian ideas at second hand. The Fuṣūṣ is the central text of the school and commenting on it is the standard way of entering the tradition's scholarly conversation.

Why it complicates the reception story. This is the kind of work that produced the modern picture of Ibn Turka as a mystical philosopher linking Ibn ʿArabī to Mullā Ṣadrā — the picture the [[tamhid-al-qawaid|Tamhīd]] entry argues obscured everything else. He really did write Akbarian commentary, and a great deal of it; the error is not in noticing that but in stopping there.

The revision. A corrected version four years later, in the year after [[iskandar-falls|Iskandar's fall]]. Ibn Turka returned to his own texts — this one in 1415, the [[mafahis-revised|Mafāḥiṣ]] in 828/1425 — which is worth knowing when using colophon dates: a date may record a revision rather than a first composition.

What we do not know. What the 817/1415 corrections changed; whether the Ḥijāz journey was in fact a pilgrimage (the chronology marks it with a question mark); and whether Yazdī accepted the invitation.

813/1411 · Herat

Shāhrukh abrogates yasa law for the sharīʿa

POLITICS · ATTESTED · YEAR

Shāhrukh declares the abrogation of Mongol yasa law, enforces the sharīʿa and closes the taverns. Timurid legitimacy is refounded on religious rather than steppe grounds — and the ground on which an occult philosopher will be judged shifts with it.

Full account

What happened. In 813/1411 Shāhrukh declared the abrogation of yasa law, enforced the sharīʿa, and closed the taverns (The Quest, chronology). The previous year he had completed a madrasa-khānaqāh complex at Herat and appointed its teachers.

What the yasa was. The Mongol customary law code attributed to Chinggis Khan, which had served as the juridical backbone of Chinggisid and early Timurid authority. Timurid rulers governed as heirs to a steppe legal order as much as to an Islamic one, and the two coexisted uneasily.

What the abrogation did. It relocated the basis of dynastic legitimacy from descent and custom to religious orthodoxy. This is a re-founding rather than a reform: a ruler who governs under the sharīʿa is answerable to the class of men who interpret it, and elevates that class in the act of submitting to it.

Why it matters for Ibn Turka. Not because it targeted him — it did not, and he was in Iskandar's Isfahan at the time — but because it set the conditions under which his career would be judged. A court that has staked its legitimacy on public piety acquires an interest in policing doctrine, a class of officials whose standing rises with that policing, and a vocabulary in which professional rivalry can be pursued as accusation. All three appear in the tribunals of the 1420s.

The contrast with Iskandar. Melvin-Koushki sets the two rulers against each other: if the rise of absolutist politics is a benchmark for early modernity, then in 816/1412 Shāhrukh was the last medieval Islamic ruler and Mīrzā Iskandar the first early modern absolutist sovereign. Shāhrukh's answer to the legitimacy question was conservative and juridical; [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar's]] was eschatological, with occultists on the staff. Ibn Turka's science belonged to the programme that lost.

815–817/1412–1414 · Isfahan

Iskandar takes Isfahan and builds a brilliant court

POLITICS · ATTESTED · RANGE

Iskandar Sulṭān establishes at Isfahan one of the most lavishly bookish and most astrologically preoccupied courts of the age. Ibn Turka is attached to it — then moves back to Shiraz under harassment by envious individuals, putting forward his protégé Sharaf al-Dīn Yaʿqūb to replace him as qāḍī.

Full account

What happened. Between 815 and 817/1412–14 Iskandar took Isfahan and established what was by every measure a brilliant though short-lived court there (The Quest, §3.1). Ibn Turka was attached to it — and then moved back to Shiraz due to harassment by envious individuals, putting forward his protégé Sharaf al-Dīn Yaʿqūb to replace him as qāḍī (The Quest, chronology).

The withdrawal is the important half. This is the first documented instance of the pattern that would end the career, and it happens at the height of his patron's power, a decade before the first tribunal. He was not driven out by doctrinal opposition or by the state. He was driven out by colleagues, and the chronology's phrase for their motive is envy.

What he did about it. He left, and he installed his own protégé in the post. That is a managed retreat rather than a rout: the office stays within his influence even as he vacates it. It also tells us he had protégés worth placing.

The absolutism experiment. This court is the setting for what Melvin-Koushki treats as a turning point in Islamic political history — the [[eschatological-absolutism|experiment of 815/1412]] involving Iskandar, Shāh Niʿmatullāh Valī and Sayyid Sharīf Jurjānī, from which he concludes that Iskandar was the first early modern absolutist sovereign. Ibn Turka was attached to the court where that happened.

What we do not know. Who the envious individuals were; whether Sharaf al-Dīn Yaʿqūb is connected to [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Dīn Yazdī]] (the sources in hand distinguish them); and how long the Shiraz interval lasted.

815/1412 · Isfahan

The eschatological-absolutism experiment

POLITICS · ATTESTED · YEAR

Melvin-Koushki devotes a study to Mīrzā Iskandar, Shāh Niʿmatullāh Valī and Sayyid Sharīf Jurjānī in 815/1412 as an experiment with eschatological absolutism, and draws a sharp conclusion: if the rise of absolutist politics is a benchmark for early modernity, then in 816/1412 Shāhrukh was the last medieval Islamic ruler and Mīrzā Iskandar the first early modern absolutist sovereign. Ibn Turka is attached to the court where this happens.

Full account

What the study argues. Melvin-Koushki devotes an article to Mīrzā Iskandar, Shāh Niʿmatullāh Valī and Sayyid Sharīf Jurjānī in 815/1412 as an experiment with eschatological absolutism, and draws from it a conclusion about periodisation that is unusually blunt: if the rise of absolutist politics is one of the benchmarks for early modernity, in 816/1412 Shāhrukh was the last medieval Islamic ruler, and Mīrzā Iskandar was the first early modern absolutist sovereign (Imperial Talismanic Love, 16, 21).

The two answers to one problem. Both rulers faced the same question — on what basis does a Timurid govern, now that Tīmūr is dead? Their answers were incompatible. [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] was conservative and juridical: abrogate the [[yasa-abrogated|Mongol yasa]], enforce the sharīʿa, rule as a Muslim sovereign answerable to the religious classes. [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar's]] was eschatological and absolutist: claim a sacred role in the unfolding of history, with occultists, astrologers and philosophers on the staff to articulate it.

Where Ibn Turka stood. On the losing side, and not by accident. A science that reads cosmic cycles, dates golden ages from [[saturn-jupiter-conjunction|conjunctions]] and supplies rulers with a place in sacred time is precisely staff work for the second programme. It has no comparable role in the first.

Why this reframes the tribunals. They stop looking like a clash between religion and magic and start looking like the aftermath of a political defeat. The man prosecuted in the 1420s had been the intellectual furniture of a rival claim to sovereignty, and the prosecution was conducted by the winners' court.

What we do not know. What Ibn Turka's personal involvement in the 815/1412 experiment was, if any; the sources in hand place him at the court rather than in the episode.

817–825/1414–1422

Attempts to retire from public life

LIFE · ATTESTED · RANGE

For eight years after Iskandar's fall Ibn Turka tries to withdraw. It is the most productive stretch of his life: the Book of Inquiries is completed inside it.

Full account

What happened. From roughly 817 to 825/1414–22 — eight years, following [[iskandar-falls|Iskandar's destruction]] — Ibn Turka attempted to retire from public life (The Quest, chronology).

Why "attempted" is the right word. The retirement did not hold: it ends with his opponents denouncing him at Herat around 825/1422 and his travelling there to answer them. A man whose patron has been executed by the reigning sovereign does not get to withdraw quietly by choosing to.

What the retirement produced. The most important eight years of his working life. The [[mafahis-completed|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was completed in 823/1420, inside this window. The summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism is the work of a man in political eclipse, without a patron, trying to stay out of view.

The pattern worth naming. Much of what survives was written in the gaps between disasters. Of Letters in the year his patron fell; the Mafāḥiṣ during the attempted retirement; the Kitāb al-Manāhij wintering at Natanz in exile; the second [[nafsat-al-masdur|apology]] and the Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya during nine months of waiting at Herat. The productivity is not despite the trouble; it tracks it.

What we do not know. Where he spent these years, how he supported himself, and whether the retirement was formal — a resignation of office — or simply a withdrawal from court.

817/1414 · Iraq

Shāhrukh destroys Iskandar Sulṭān

POLITICS · ATTESTED · YEAR

Shāhrukh marches against Iskandar in Iraq. Iskandar flees, is captured and blinded, and is executed afterwards; Ibrāhīm Sulṭān is installed in his place. Ibn Turka's first patron is destroyed by the ruler he must now live under.

Full account

What happened. In 817/1414 [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] marched against [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] in Iraq. Iskandar fled, was captured and blinded, and was executed afterwards; Shāhrukh installed his own son Ibrāhīm Sulṭān in his place (The Quest, chronology). Iskandar's death is generally dated 818/1415.

A correction this portal has had to make. Earlier versions of the entries here compressed the sequence into "defeated, blinded and killed in 1415". The chronology separates the defeat and blinding (817/1414) from the execution that followed. The distinction matters because blinding was the standard Timurid method of disqualifying a rival from rule without killing him — the execution was a second decision.

What it cost Ibn Turka. Everything, in slow motion. The patron who had installed him in his hereditary judgeship and commissioned his two central lettrist works was destroyed by the man who would rule for the next thirty-three years. The commission became a liability; the association became a fact on a file. From 817 to 825/1414–22 Ibn Turka attempted to retire from public life.

The deeper problem. It was not merely a change of employer. Iskandar and Shāhrukh represented incompatible answers to the legitimacy question — eschatological absolutism with occultists on staff, against a conservative juridical order refounded on the [[yasa-abrogated|sharīʿa]]. Ibn Turka's science belonged to the programme that lost the war.

What we do not know. Where Ibn Turka was during the campaign, and whether he was ever formally associated with Iskandar's rebellion.

817/1414 · Shiraz

Writes the Risālat al-Ḥurūf in Shiraz

TEXTS · ATTESTED · YEAR

Of Letters, written for Iskandar Sulṭān — the treatise that first proposes the fourfold analysis of the letter which the Book of Inquiries will expand.

Full account

What happened. In 817/1414 Ibn Turka wrote the Risālat al-Ḥurūf — Of Letters — in Shiraz, apparently for [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] (The Quest, chronology; the chronology marks the attribution with a question mark).

The timing is extraordinary. 817/1414 is the year Shāhrukh marched against Iskandar and had him captured and blinded. Ibn Turka's first treatise on lettrism was written for a patron in the act of being destroyed, or immediately after.

What it contains. The fourfold schema of the letter that the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] would later massively expand: the letters treated according to their numerological (iḥṣāʾī), symbological (kitābī) and phonological (kalāmī) forms, and then as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā) (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66). The technical architecture of the whole system is present in this short treatise.

Its place in the oeuvre. It is the first lettrist work, and the summa was begun shortly after completing it. Read together, 817/1414 and 823/1420 bracket the period in which the science took its mature form — a period that begins with the destruction of his patron and ends in the year Ulugh Beg broke ground at Samarkand.

What we do not know. Whether Iskandar commissioned it — the chronology's question mark is deliberate. Whether Iskandar ever saw it. And whether it survives in edited form.

823/1420 · Isfahan or Yazd

Completes the Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ

TEXTS · ATTESTED · YEAR

The Book of Inquiries is finished — the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism. It is revised and expanded in 828/1425. Ulugh Beg breaks ground on the Samarkand Observatory the same year: two foundations laid in one year, one in stone and one on paper.

Full account

What happened. Ibn Turka completed the Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ in 823/1420, in Isfahan or Yazd — the chronology is uncertain which. He revised and expanded it in 828/1425 (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

What was finished. Melvin-Koushki calls it the first Arabic summa of Islamic neopythagoreanism and the fullest expression of Ibn Turka's lettrist metaphysics. It massively expands the fourfold schema first proposed in the earlier Of Letters, treating the letters in their numerological (iḥṣāʾī), symbological (kitābī) and phonological (kalāmī) forms, and then separately as they are in themselves (fī anfusi-hā).

Who it was for. Begun shortly after Of Letters and, like it, almost certainly at the instance of [[iskandar-sultan|Iskandar Sulṭān]] — though by 1420 Iskandar had been dead for five years. The summa was completed for a patron who no longer existed.

The coincidence worth noticing. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] broke ground on the [[samarkand-observatory|Samarkand Observatory]] in the same year. Two foundations laid in 1420, a few hundred miles apart, by men acting on one conviction: that a science earns its standing by becoming mathematical. Melvin-Koushki's argument is that these are not neighbouring events but one movement, studied separately only by accident of modern disciplinary boundaries.

How we know the date. From the colophons of [[ms-majlis-10196|MS Majlis 10196]], the codex in which the incipit and explicit of this very text are written in Ibn Turka's own hand. For this work specifically, the evidence is about as good as the corpus offers — though even here the general caution applies that a colophon may record transcription rather than composition.

What the revision changed is not stated in the sources in hand. We know the work was expanded in 828/1425, in the same window in which two-thirds of the surviving oeuvre was being copied, and immediately before the exile.

823/1420 · Samarkand

Ulugh Beg founds the Samarkand Observatory

INSTITUTION · ATTESTED · YEAR

Ground is broken on the largest astronomical instrument on earth. Melvin-Koushki argues that the mathematicalization of the occult sciences is the immediate context for the mathematization of astronomy achieved here — the two are one movement, not two.

Full account

What happened. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] broke ground on the [[samarkand-observatory|Samarkand Observatory]] in 1420 — a meridian arc of unprecedented scale sunk into bedrock, housed in a three-storey building, staffed by the best mathematical astronomers of the age.

The coincidence, and the argument built on it. The [[mafahis-completed|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was completed in 823/1420. Two foundations laid in one year, a few hundred miles apart. Melvin-Koushki's claim is that this is not coincidence but a single movement seen twice: the mathematicalization, neopythagoreanization and sanctification of occultism in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran is the immediate intellectual and sociopolitical context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy achieved at Samarkand — processes which have heretofore been studied in atomistic isolation (Powers of One, 1).

The human link. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]], the observatory's second director, had spent twenty-five years in the same schoolroom as Ibn Turka, taught by Ibn Turka's own brother. The two institutions share a classroom.

Why the size. A meridian arc's precision scales with its radius: a larger instrument resolves finer angles. The Zīj-i Sulṭānī produced from its observations remained the most accurate star catalogue in the world for roughly two centuries.

What we do not know. Whether Ibn Turka knew of the founding, and whether any communication passed between Isfahan and Samarkand in this year.

825/1422 · Herat

Accused of Sufi bias; clears his name at Herat

LIFE · ATTESTED · CIRCA

His opponents go to Shāhrukh's court and accuse him of Sufi bias. Ibn Turka travels to Herat to answer them in person, gains Shāhrukh's favour, and is given the judgeship of Yazd. The first tribunal, and he wins it.

Full account

What happened. Around 825/1422 Ibn Turka's opponents went to [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] court at Herat and accused him of Sufi bias. He travelled to Herat to clear his name, gained Shāhrukh's favour, and was given the judgeship of Yazd (The Quest, chronology).

The shape of the win. This is the most impressive thing in the biography and it is easy to skip past. He answered the charge in person, in front of the ruler who had destroyed his previous patron eight years earlier, and left not merely acquitted but promoted. The accusation of Sufi bias was converted into an additional judicial office.

Why "Sufi bias" was the charge. It is a revealing choice. Ibn Turka criticised organised [[sufism|Sufism]] in his written work while maintaining friendly relations with prominent Sufi shaykhs, several of them promoters of [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] against hostile reaction. A prosecutor could point at the associations without engaging the doctrine — which is easier than arguing about [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]] and, in a court that had recently refounded itself on public orthodoxy, more likely to land.

Where it sits in the sequence. Historically he faces three inquisitions, winning the first two and losing the third. This is the first. The chronology dates it; the second and third are not dated in the sources in hand, which is a real gap given that the whole dramatic arc of the life turns on them.

What it did not settle. Five years later he was exiled from Herat anyway, on a different charge, from the same court. The vindication of 825/1422 bought time rather than security — and the chronology gives no explanation for why it failed to hold.

827–839/1425–1435

MS Majlis 10196 copied, partly in the author's hand

TEXTS · ATTESTED · RANGE

The earliest, most complete and most reliable collection of Ibn Turka's works, copied over the last eight years of his life and three years past his death. Full account

What it is. [[ms-majlis-10196|MS Majlis 10196]], the earliest, most complete and most reliable collection of Ibn Turka's works — copied across the last eight years of his life and three years past his death, so roughly 827–839/1425–1435 (The Quest, §2.1).

The autograph. The incipit and explicit of the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]], folios 52a and 118b, are in Ibn Turka's own hand, which suggests the copyist was in close contact with the author at various points. Opening and closing lines are exactly where an author would be asked to sign off.

Why it is the portal's foundation. Melvin-Koushki relies on its colophons wherever possible to date the oeuvre, and nearly every date on this chronology descends from it. The chain runs: colophon → his analysis → the chronology in the addenda → these pages.

The limits, which are real. In many instances it is impossible to tell whether a date records composition or transcription. And fully half the manuscript, representing almost two-thirds of the preserved works, was copied between 827–29/1425–27 — the window bracketing the exile. That clustering may record a burst of writing; it may equally record a copying campaign undertaken because the author's position was collapsing and the works needed securing. The evidence does not decide, and the second reading is at least as plausible as the first.

What we do not know. Who the copyist was; who paid for the collection; what was omitted and why; and whether other autograph fragments survive in it beyond the two named folios.

TEXTS · ATTESTED · YEAR

Full account

What happened. Ibn Turka revised and expanded the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] in 828/1425, five years after completing it (Of Islamic Grammatology, 66).

Where it falls. Inside the window 827–29/1425–27, in which half of [[ms-majlis-10196|MS Majlis 10196]] — two-thirds of the surviving oeuvre — was being copied, and immediately before the [[exiled-from-herat|exile of 1427]]. What we do not know. What the revision changed. That it was expanded is recorded; the content of the expansion is not described in the sources in hand.

830/1427 · Herat

Exiled from Herat with Qāsim-i Anvār

LIFE · ATTESTED · YEAR

Ibn Turka is expelled alongside Qāsim-i Anvār, his charismatic associate from the Cairo years, over suspect connections — the official excuse being a putative Ḥurūfī link. The stated charge and the operative reason are not the same.

Full account

What happened. In 1427 Ibn Turka was expelled from Herat together with [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]], his charismatic associate from the Cairo years. The dissertation is specific about the mechanism: they were exiled for suspect connections, though it was Qāsim's putative connection with the Ḥurūfīs that provided the official excuse (The Quest, 456 n.16).

The distinction that matters. The stated charge and the operative reason are not the same, and the sources let us see the gap. The operative worry was association — Qāsim was a propagandist of the Ṣafaviyya order and was apparently claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own, at a moment when that was dangerous: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] had attempted a purge of Ismāʿīlīs in Quhistan in 1415. The [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] label was the charge that would carry in court.

Why the label was available. Because it was not baseless in the family's case. Ḥurūfī sources record that Ibn Turka's father Afżal al-Dīn and his elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] had consulted Fażl Allāh Astarābādī during his Isfahan residence, presenting dreams for interpretation. Melvin-Koushki cautions that this implies no Ḥurūfī inclination and that the story's hagiographical purpose is to show Fażl Allāh's standing before his messianic turn — but the episode is on the record and a prosecutor could reach it.

What it cost. The exile ends the settled portion of the career. What follows is the sequence the [[chronology|chronology]] records: a fruitless attempt at a hearing in Simnan around 833/1429, a winter at Natanz, a promise of reinstatement at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa around 834/1430, and then nine months of weekly attendance at court in Herat waiting for a review that had not come when he died in 835/1432.

The irony of the timing. In 835/1432, the year of his death, there were Ḥurūfī uprisings in Isfahan and Tabriz. The charge that had removed him five years earlier acquired, at exactly the wrong moment, the appearance of foresight.

Full account

What happened. Around 833/1429 Ibn Turka made a fruitless attempt to obtain a hearing with [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] at Simnan (The Quest, chronology).

What the exile had become by then. Not a sentence being served but a case awaiting attention. Two years after the expulsion from Herat, the obstacle was no longer a verdict — it was access. Ibn Turka needed to be in front of the sovereign, and could not get there.

Why he thought it would work. Because it had, once. In 825/1422 he had travelled to Herat to answer an accusation of Sufi bias in person, gained Shāhrukh's favour and been given the judgeship of Yazd. The [[first-inquisition|first inquisition]] was won by exactly this method. Simnan is an attempt to repeat a proven manoeuvre, and it fails.

Full account

What happened. Muḥammad al-Jazarī, described in the chronology as Ibn Turka's powerful opponent, died at Herat in 833/1429 (The Quest, chronology). The same year saw [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] second Azarbayjan campaign against the Qara Quyunlu.

Why the entry exists. Because he is the only opponent the chronology names. The tribunals are attributed throughout to rival colleagues and envious individuals, and the record is otherwise silent about who they were. Al-Jazarī is the single face on the other side.

What his death did not do. Restore him. Ibn Turka was two years into exile when his powerful opponent died, and the machinery outlived the man: the very next entries in the chronology are the fruitless attempt to obtain a hearing at Simnan in the same year, and then a further two years before even a promise of reinstatement. Full account

What happened. Around 834/1430 Ibn Turka obtained a hearing at [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh's]] camp at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa near Sultaniyya, and received a promise of reinstatement (The Quest, chronology). Shāhrukh returned to Herat from the Azarbayjan campaign the same year.

The high point of the last five years. After the refusal at Simnan, he finally got in front of the sovereign — in a military camp, on campaign, rather than at court — and was told he would be restored.

And it is only a promise. The chronology's next entries are the journey to Herat and [[nine-months-waiting|nine months of weekly attendance]] waiting on a review that never came. Whatever was said at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa did not convert into an order.

Why this may be the cruellest entry on the chronology. A refusal can be answered: appealed, worked around, waited out with a clear view of the obstacle. A promise cannot. It removes the grounds for further pleading while producing nothing, and it obliges the recipient to keep attending in good faith. The last two years of the life are structured by a commitment that was probably sincere and certainly never actioned.

The rhyme. This is the same shape as the [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] problem — interest that is real, an arrangement to be settled later, and later turning out to have a great deal of court business in front of it. Ibn Turka's career ends twice over in a deferral rather than a refusal.

What we do not know. Who conveyed the promise, in what terms, and whether it was ever recorded in writing.

834–835/1431–1432 · Herat

Full account

What happened. Having obtained a promise of reinstatement at Shāhrukh's camp at Ṣāʾin Qalʿa near Sultaniyya around 834/1430, Ibn Turka travelled to Herat. There, across 834–35/1431–32, he was for nine months in weekly attendance at court waiting for the review of his case (The Quest, chronology). The review did not come. He died at Herat in 835/1432.

What he did with the time. He wrote. The chronology places two works in this interval: the second recension of the [[nafsat-al-masdur|Nafsat al-Maṣdūr]] — the apologies — and the Risāla Iʿtiqādiyya, composed for [[baysunghur|Bāysunghur]]. Both are documents of a man arguing for himself: one a defence, one a statement of belief addressed to the Timurid prince he had been closest to.

How to read the apology written here. With care. Melvin-Koushki's warning about the genre applies with maximum force to a text produced in these conditions: the self-image Ibn Turka presents in the apologies, produced under great duress, is hardly reflective of his primary concerns (The Quest, 6–7). Leonard Lewisohn's reading of him as an orthodox Sunni Sufi thinker rests on exactly this material. A creed written in the ninth month of waiting on a tribunal is evidence about the tribunal.

Why the shape of it matters. This is not a dramatic ending. There is no verdict, no condemnation, no scene. There is an old man attending court once a week for the better part of a year because a promise had been made, and then there is a death. The most consequential occult philosopher of Timurid Iran spent his last year in an antechamber.

The rhyme with Samarkand. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] — the patron whose programme was his own programme — had seventeen years left. The [[samarkand-observatory|observatory]] was running. The patronage that would have made sense of the whole career was three weeks' ride away and never arrived, for the same reason the case review never arrived: not refusal, but a court's ordinary capacity to defer.

835/1432 · Samarkand

What happened. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]] — noted astronomer and mathematician, and the second director of [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg's]] [[samarkand-observatory|observatory at Samarkand]] — died in 835/1432, the same year as Ibn Turka (The Quest, 41 n.17, citing Ragep, 'Ḳāḍī-zāda Rūmī', EI2).

835/1432 · Isfahan, Tabriz

CONTEXT · ATTESTED · YEAR

In the year of his death the movement he had spent a career being distinguished from rises in his own city. The charge that exiled him is, at exactly the wrong moment, politically vindicated.

Full account

What happened. [[hurufism|Ḥurūfī]] uprisings broke out at Isfahan and Tabriz in 835/1432 — the year Ibn Turka died at Herat (The Quest, chronology).

The timing. He had been exiled from Herat five years earlier on a putative Ḥurūfī connection — a charge Melvin-Koushki describes as the official excuse for an expulsion actually driven by association with [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]]. In the year of his death, the movement he had spent a career being distinguished from rose in arms in his own city.

What it did to the record. Nothing good. A distinction that was difficult to maintain in 1427 became harder still once Ḥurūfīs were in open revolt at Isfahan, and posthumous reputation is made in exactly such conditions. The [[tamhid-al-qawaid|reception history]] that reduced him to a mystical philosopher had, among its other causes, a strong incentive after 1432 to keep his lettrism out of view.

The distinction, one more time. The Ḥurūfīs proclaimed a new, Persian dispensation. Ibn Turka proclaimed a golden age of knowledge in which what the Imams and ancients had already transmitted becomes actual, insisting on the binding finality of the dispensation given through Muḥammad. Both are millenarian. Only one is a prophetic claim (The Quest, 456).

What we do not know. The scale and outcome of the risings; and whether Ibn Turka, at Herat, heard of them before he died.

What happened. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān [[bistami|Bisṭāmī]] died in 858/1454, twenty-two years after Ibn Turka.

The comparison the portal cannot avoid. They were both the intellectual heirs of [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Sayyid Ḥusayn Akhlāṭī]], and it is unclear whether they ever met during their respective long residences in Mamluk Cairo (The Quest, 2). The same teaching went east with one and west with the other.

Melvin-Koushki's judgement on the outcome is the uncomfortable one: the Ottoman projects are cognate — and more successful. The Iranian version produced three tribunals, an exile, and an oeuvre half of which is still unedited. The Ottoman version produced a durable tradition at a court that wanted it.

What the asymmetry is about. Not ideas. The inheritance was the same and the doctrines were close. What differed was politics: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] refounded Timurid legitimacy on the sharīʿa and elevated the class of men who interpret it, while the Ottoman court of the same decades made room for exactly this kind of universalist project. The comparison is the sharpest available argument that Ibn Turka's fate was contingent rather than deserved.

Why the portal is only half the story. Because the Ottoman half is being written elsewhere — by Cornell Fleischer and his Chicago Circle — and the two branches are described as largely coterminous, so research on either bears on the whole.

What we do not know. Whether they met. Nothing in the sources in hand settles it.

What happened. İhsan Fazlıoğlu posited the existence of the fifteenth-century Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ network in a 1996 article on the Brethren and [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī]] in early Ottoman intellectual and cultural life (The Quest, ch. 1 n.88).

Why a 1996 event belongs on a fifteenth-century chronology. Because the network is a modern reconstruction, not a category the sources hand over. The men involved called themselves the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ cryptically; no roll, rule or manifesto survives. That there was a coherent transregional network at all is a scholarly finding with a date and an author, and a portal that presents it as simple fact would be concealing its own foundations.

Who is building it. Melvin-Koushki states that his understanding of late medieval Islamic occult philosophy is dependent on the ongoing research of Cornell Fleischer and his Chicago Circle into the cognate Ottoman projects; Fleischer's A Mediterranean Apocalypse is expected to provide definitive corroboration of the network's transregional and transcultural character (The Quest, ch. 1 nn. 87–88).

What follows for the reader. The two branches are largely coterminous both logistically and conceptually, so research on either has vital bearing on the whole complex. This portal treats the Iranian arm in detail and the Ottoman arm at second hand, which means it gives a partial account by construction — and the partiality is a property of the sources chosen, not an oversight.

What we do not know. Whether the reconstruction will hold in its present form. Fleischer's corroborating work is described as forthcoming.
