The Samarkand Observatory

The Samarkand Observatory, founded by [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] in 1420 around a meridian arc of unprecedented scale. Its Zīj-i Sulṭānī was the most accurate star table in the world for two centuries. Melvin-Koushki's argument places it inside Ibn Turka's story rather than beside it: the mathematization of astronomy achieved here and the [[neoplatonism|mathematicalization of the occult sciences]] are the same movement, and have been studied in isolation only by accident of modern disciplinary boundaries.

The Samarkand Observatory

The institution. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] broke ground in 1420 on what became the largest astronomical instrument on earth: a meridian arc sunk into bedrock, housed in a three-storey building, staffed by the best mathematical astronomers of the age — Qāḍīzāda Rūmī among them, and later Jamshīd al-Kāshī and ʿAlī Qushjī. The Zīj-i Sulṭānī it produced remained the most accurate star catalogue in the world for roughly two centuries.

Why it belongs to this portal. The conventional account treats Samarkand as a triumph of exact science and Timurid occultism as a separate, embarrassing phenomenon. Melvin-Koushki's thesis denies the separation. He argues that the mathematicalization, neopythagoreanization and sanctification of occultism in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran is the immediate intellectual and sociopolitical context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy by the members of the Samarkand Observatory — processes which, he notes, have heretofore been studied in atomistic isolation (Powers of One, 1).

The observatory and the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Book of Inquiries]] were begun in the same year, 1420, a few hundred miles apart, by men working out the same conviction: that a science earns its standing by becoming mathematical. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]] worked at both ends of it.

The end. Ulugh Beg was killed in 1449 and the observatory was abandoned and dismantled not long after; its foundations were not rediscovered until 1908. The tables outlived the building, and reached Mughal India, where Shāhjahān's Zīj-i Shāhjahānī updated them behind the first thoroughly lettrist preface in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition (Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists, 599).

The argument for putting it in this portal at all

The observatory's presence here is a thesis, not a courtesy. The standard division treats Samarkand as exact science and Timurid occultism as a separate embarrassment. Melvin-Koushki's claim is that this separation is an artefact of modern disciplinary boundaries: the mathematicalization, neopythagoreanization and sanctification of occultism in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran is the immediate intellectual and sociopolitical context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy achieved here — processes which have heretofore been studied in atomistic isolation (Powers of One, 1).

Three concrete links support it.

The year. Ground was broken in 1420; the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Kitāb al-Mafāḥiṣ]] was completed in 823/1420.

The classroom. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]], the observatory's second director, spent twenty-five years being taught alongside Ibn Turka by Ibn Turka's own brother. He died in 835/1432, the same year as Ibn Turka.

The patron. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] was styled al-sulṭān al-faylasūf and did the mathematics himself — the audience Ibn Turka's mathematicalized science was constructed for.

The instrument

The defining feature was a meridian arc of unprecedented scale, sunk into bedrock and housed in a three-storey building — an instrument whose accuracy came from sheer size, since a larger radius means finer graduations. The Zīj-i Sulṭānī produced from its observations remained the most accurate star catalogue in the world for roughly two centuries, and was still in use in Europe long after.

The afterlife the tables had

The lettrist connection surfaces explicitly two centuries later. Shāhjahān commissioned the Zīj-i Shāhjahānī, an updated and corrected version of Ulugh Beg's tables, and its preface is the first in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition to be explicitly and thoroughgoingly lettrist in tenor (Philosopher-Kings, 599).

Whatever was separate in 1420 was not separate by the 1650s: the most prestigious astronomical project of Mughal India introduced itself in Ibn Turka's idiom.

The end

Ulugh Beg was killed in 1449 by his own son's faction. The observatory was abandoned and dismantled not long after, and its foundations were not rediscovered until 1908. The institution lasted under thirty years; its tables lasted two centuries.

What we do not know

  • Whether Ibn Turka ever visited, or corresponded with anyone there beyond Qāḍīzāda.
  • What the observatory's staff thought of lettrism. The link is argued at the level of intellectual context, not documented as an exchange of views.
  • Almost nothing of the physical instrument survives to be examined.

The staff, and the shared classroom

The observatory's directors and computers are the other half of Melvin-Koushki's argument about disciplinary isolation. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]] was its second director; Jamshīd al-Kāshī and ʿAlī Qushjī worked there.

Qāḍīzāda is the link that makes the claim concrete rather than thematic. He had studied under Ibn Turka's elder brother [[sadr-al-din-turka|Ṣadr al-Dīn]] alongside Ibn Turka, for twenty-five years, in an education that stopped deliberately at the outward sciences because their teacher deemed it too dangerous to delve into esoteric matters in public.

Two pupils of one household then spent their careers making the hidden order of things calculable — one through the stars, one through the letters. They died in the same year, 835/1432.

What was actually built

A meridian arc of unprecedented radius sunk into bedrock and housed in a three-storey building. The principle is simple and the engineering was not: angular precision scales with the instrument's size, so accuracy was bought by sheer scale. The Zīj-i Sulṭānī compiled from its observations remained the most accurate star catalogue in the world for roughly two centuries, and was still consulted in Europe long after.

The lettrist preface, two centuries on

The clearest evidence that the two sciences were never as separate as the modern division assumes comes from the tables' afterlife. Shāhjahān — self-proclaimed Second Lord of Conjunction, after Tīmūr the First — commissioned for his accession the Zīj-i Shāhjahānī, an updated and corrected version of Ulugh Beg's tables of two centuries prior.

Tellingly, it features the first preface in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition to be explicitly and thoroughgoingly lettrist in tenor (Philosopher-Kings, 599). The same feature appears in the preface to the Sirr-i Akbar, Dārā Shukūh's Persian translation of the Upaniṣads.
