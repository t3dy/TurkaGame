The Samarkand Observatory

The Samarkand Observatory, founded by [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] in 1420 around a meridian arc of unprecedented scale. Its Zīj-i Sulṭānī was the most accurate star table in the world for two centuries. Melvin-Koushki's argument places it inside Ibn Turka's story rather than beside it: the mathematization of astronomy achieved here and the [[neoplatonism|mathematicalization of the occult sciences]] are the same movement, and have been studied in isolation only by accident of modern disciplinary boundaries.

The Samarkand Observatory

The institution. [[ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]] broke ground in 1420 on what became the largest astronomical instrument on earth: a meridian arc sunk into bedrock, housed in a three-storey building, staffed by the best mathematical astronomers of the age — Qāḍīzāda Rūmī among them, and later Jamshīd al-Kāshī and ʿAlī Qushjī. The Zīj-i Sulṭānī it produced remained the most accurate star catalogue in the world for roughly two centuries.

Why it belongs to this portal. The conventional account treats Samarkand as a triumph of exact science and Timurid occultism as a separate, embarrassing phenomenon. Melvin-Koushki's thesis denies the separation. He argues that the mathematicalization, neopythagoreanization and sanctification of occultism in Ilkhanid–Timurid–Aqquyunlu Iran is the immediate intellectual and sociopolitical context for the celebrated mathematization of astronomy by the members of the Samarkand Observatory — processes which, he notes, have heretofore been studied in atomistic isolation (Powers of One, 1).

The observatory and the [[kitab-al-mafahis|Book of Inquiries]] were begun in the same year, 1420, a few hundred miles apart, by men working out the same conviction: that a science earns its standing by becoming mathematical. [[qazizada-rumi|Qāḍīzāda Rūmī]] worked at both ends of it.

The end. Ulugh Beg was killed in 1449 and the observatory was abandoned and dismantled not long after; its foundations were not rediscovered until 1908. The tables outlived the building, and reached Mughal India, where Shāhjahān's Zīj-i Shāhjahānī updated them behind the first thoroughly lettrist preface in the Arabo-Persian astronomical tradition (Philosopher-Kings as Sultan-Scientists, 599).
