New Brethren of Purity

Ikhwan al-Safa al-Jadid (The New Brethren of Purity). Transregional network of late 14th- and 15th-century scholars, occultists, and courtiers claiming succession to the 10th-century Brethren of Purity. Members included [[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]], [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlati]], [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Din Yazdi]], and figures across Ottoman, Mamluk, and Persian courts. United by commitment to [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]], [[abjad-numerology|numerology]], and the integration of occult sciences with [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean philosophy]]. Represented a self-conscious intellectual movement to establish occultism as legitimate, rational universal science.

Ikhwan al-Safa al-Jadid: The New Brethren of Purity

The New Brethren of Purity (Ikhwan al-Safa al-Jadid) was a transregional network of scholars and practitioners spanning three major power centers—Ottoman Anatolia, Mamluk Egypt/Syria, and Timurid Persia—in the late 14th and 15th centuries. They invoked the name of the legendary 10th-century Brethren of Purity (Ikhwan al-Safa), but understood themselves as a new order inaugurating a fresh era of occult science.

Network Structure

Unlike formal institutions, the New Brethren functioned as a loose confederation bound by shared intellectual commitments rather than hierarchy or bylaws. Key nodes included:

  • Isfahan Circle ([[ibn-turka|Ibn Turka]], [[sharaf-al-din-yazdi|Sharaf al-Din Yazdi]], [[qazizada-rumi|Qazizada Rumi]])
  • Cairo/Mamluk court ([[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Sayyid Husayn Akhlati]], Ottoman and Mamluk occultists)
  • Anatolian scholars (Ottoman court philosophers and talismanic practitioners)

Core Project

The New Brethren were united by a single grand project: to establish that [[ilm-al-huruf|lettrism]], [[abjad-numerology|numerology]], [[pythagorean-cosmology|Pythagorean mathematics]], and [[divine-names|divine-names doctrine]] constitute a comprehensive, rational science encompassing metaphysics, ethics, cosmology, and operative practice. This was not mysticism in the Sufi sense but rational occultism—grounded in logic, mathematics, and philosophical rigor.

Historical Significance

Melvin-Koushki's research demonstrates that the New Brethren were not marginal occultists but central intellectual figures in their respective courts, commanding patronage, commanding audiences, and exerting real influence over political and administrative thought. They succeeded in their project: by the early 16th century, their intellectual synthesis had become canonical in Ottoman and Safavid philosophy.

The New Brethren represent a crucial moment in global intellectual history—when Islamic occult philosophy achieved systematic articulation, international coordination, and institutional legitimacy, rivaling and arguably exceeding contemporary European and Chinese philosophical achievements.

Who established that this network existed

The network is not a category the sources hand over ready-made; it is a modern reconstruction, and the portal should say whose. Its existence was posited by İhsan Fazlıoğlu in a 1996 article on the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ and [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī]] in early Ottoman intellectual life. Melvin-Koushki states that his own understanding of late medieval Islamic occult philosophy is dependent on the ongoing research of Cornell Fleischer and his Chicago Circle into the cognate Ottoman projects (The Quest, ch. 1 nn. 87–88).

The two branches are described as largely coterminous both logistically and conceptually, so work on either has vital bearing on the whole complex. The [[isfahan-circle|Isfahan Circle]] is the eastern arm of something that reached Anatolia through Cairo, and reading the Iranian half alone produces a partial picture by construction.

One comparative fact is worth sitting with: Melvin-Koushki calls the Ottoman projects cognate and more successful. The same inheritance from the same teacher flourished at one end of the network and ended in tribunals at the other.

Why the name, and why cryptically

Taking the name of the tenth-century Basran encyclopedists was a considered act with a cost. The original Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ had produced the most ambitious encyclopedia of the sciences Arabic had seen, unified by a Neoplatonic-Pythagorean conviction that number is the ladder by which the soul climbs back toward its origin — which is precisely the fifteenth-century programme, four hundred years early.

But the name also carried Ismāʿīlī associations, and Ismāʿīlism was dangerous: [[shahrukh|Shāhrukh]] attempted a purge of Ismāʿīlīs in Quhistan in 1415, and [[qasim-i-anvar|Qāsim-i Anvār]] was apparently claimed by Ismāʿīlīs as one of their own — part of what got him and Ibn Turka expelled from Herat in 1427.

Hence cryptically. A borrowed name that announces succession to the right readers and nothing to the wrong ones is the only way to claim that inheritance safely, and the fact that they needed the manoeuvre tells you the conditions they worked in.

The two arms, and the comparison

The network's eastern arm is the [[isfahan-circle|Isfahan Circle]]; the western is Ottoman, led by [[bistami|ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Bisṭāmī]]. Both men were intellectual heirs of [[sayyid-husayn-akhlati|Akhlāṭī]], and it is unclear whether they met during their overlapping Cairo residences.

The outcomes diverged sharply, and the portal should not soften it. Melvin-Koushki calls the Ottoman projects cognate and more successful, and the concrete measure is that Bisṭāmī's Miftāḥ al-jafr al-jāmiʿ came to function in the 16th century as a dynastic handbook, by which the political fate of the House of ʿOs̱mān could be managed over the generations (Another Scientific Revolution, 2).

Same teacher, same science, same generation. One branch's manual becomes imperial equipment; the other branch's theorist is tried three times and dies waiting on a case review. The difference is political, not doctrinal, and it is the strongest argument available that Ibn Turka's fate was contingent.
